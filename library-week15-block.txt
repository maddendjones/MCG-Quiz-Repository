## What bone tissue does

- Biomechanical support and movement, since muscles pull on bones to produce motion
- Internal organ protection, as the rib cage does for the heart and lungs
- A center for hematopoiesis, which happens in the marrow
- Storage of calcium, and roughly 99% of the body's calcium sits in the skeleton

## The five shape classes

| Class | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Long | Movement and leverage | Femur, tibia, humerus, metacarpals, metatarsals |
| Short | Stability and support with limited motion | Carpals, tarsals |
| Flat | Muscle attachment and organ protection, mostly axial | Scapula, cranial bones |
| Irregular | No easily categorized shape | Vertebrae, sacrum, mandible, pelvis |
| Sesamoid | Embedded in a tendon, protects it and lengthens the lever arm | Patella |

Sesamoid bones are treated as a subset of the short and irregular groups rather than a
wholly separate category, and the patella is the example the lecture uses. A sesamoid is
embedded in a tendon, where it protects that tendon and acts as a biomechanical lever arm.

> Sesamoid bones are defined by sitting inside a tendon, which is why the patella counts and a carpal bone does not.

Library: Bone Matrix: Mineral, Organic and Water Fractions
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s04.jpg)

## The three fractions by wet weight

| Fraction | Share of wet weight | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Hydroxyapatite mineral | 60% | Calcium phosphate, Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2 |
| Organic matrix | 30% | 90% type I collagen, 10% ground substance (GAGs) and noncollagenous proteins |
| Water | 10% | |

## What each fraction contributes

The mineral fraction gives bone its stiffness and its compressive strength, which is the
ability to resist being pushed. The organic fraction, and type I collagen in particular,
gives bone its toughness and its tensile strength, which is the ability to resist being
pulled. A material with only mineral would be stiff but brittle, and a material with only
collagen would be tough but floppy, so the two properties of bone come from two different
components and a disease that damages one leaves the other intact.

Osteocalcin is the most abundant of the noncollagenous proteins, and it binds calcium
during mineralization.

> 60% mineral, 30% organic, 10% water: mineral makes bone stiff, collagen makes it tough.

Library: Osteogenesis Imperfecta and Osteomalacia as Matrix Composition Diseases
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s05.jpg)

## Two diseases, two fractions

| Disease | Common name | Fraction affected | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Osteogenesis imperfecta | Brittle bone disease | Organic, type I collagen | Genetic defect in type I collagen structure |
| Osteomalacia | Rickets, in children | Mineral, hydroxyapatite | Insufficient mineralization of the matrix |

## Why they look different

Osteogenesis imperfecta removes the toughness that collagen supplies, so the bones fracture
easily and a radiograph of an affected infant shows multiple fractures at different stages
of healing together with deformed, thin walled long bones.

Osteomalacia removes the stiffness that mineral supplies, so the bone bends rather than
breaking. The classic image is bowing of the femora and tibiae with widened, irregular,
poorly mineralized metaphyses. Causes include vitamin D insufficiency, which reduces
absorption of dietary calcium and therefore the calcium available for mineralization.

> A child whose legs bow has a mineral problem, and a child whose bones snap has a collagen problem.

Library: Macrostructure of a Growing Long Bone
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s06.jpg)

## The parts, from the end inward

- Epiphysis, the expanded end, with a thin outer shell of compact bone over an interior of
  spongy bone, capped at the joint by articular cartilage
- Physis, also called the growth plate or epiphyseal plate, a layer of hyaline cartilage
  between epiphysis and diaphysis during the growing years
- Metaphysis, the narrowing portion between the physis and the shaft
- Diaphysis, the long shaft, built as a cylinder of compact bone around a marrow cavity
- Marrow or medullary cavity, the hollow center holding hematopoietic tissue

## Two surfaces worth naming

The periosteal surface faces outward and the endosteal, or endocortical, surface faces the
marrow cavity. These two surfaces behave differently during growth and aging, which matters
later for modeling.

## What the plate does on film

The physis is cartilage, so it is radiolucent and shows as a dark line between the
epiphysis and the metaphysis. It disappears between about 20 and 25 years of age, leaving
only a physeal scar, so the presence or absence of that line separates a skeletally
immature radiograph from an adult one.

> The growth plate is hyaline cartilage, which is why it reads as a lucent line on a radiograph and vanishes at skeletal maturity.

Library: Cortical Versus Trabecular Bone: Mass Against Surface Area
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s07.jpg)

## The comparison the lecture builds

| | Cortical (compact) | Cancellous (trabecular, spongy) |
|---|---|---|
| Share of adult skeletal mass | 80% | 20% |
| Surface to volume ratio | 2.5 | 20 |
| Where | Diaphysis of long bones, outer shell of other bones | Epiphysis and metaphysis, interior of vertebral bodies |
| Main functions | Biomechanical movement, support, protection | Mineral homeostasis, multidirectional mechanical support |

## Why the ratio decides the function

Everything that happens in bone remodeling happens on a surface, so a tissue with a surface
to volume ratio of 20 exchanges mineral far faster than one with a ratio of 2.5. That is
why trabecular bone is the mineral homeostasis compartment and also why it is the
compartment that visibly thins first when resorption runs ahead of formation.

In a flat bone the same two tissues are arranged as an outer and an inner table of compact
bone around a central core of spongy bone called the diploe.

> Cortical bone holds most of the mass, trabecular bone holds most of the surface, and surface is where turnover happens.

Library: Periosteum, Endosteum and Sharpey's Fibers
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s09.jpg)

## Periosteum

Periosteum is the external covering of a bone and has two layers. The outer layer is dense
connective tissue and the inner osteogenic layer is cellular, holding mesenchymal stem cell
progenitors along with osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Sharpey's fibers are bundles of
collagen fibers that run from the periosteum into the bone matrix and anchor the covering
to the bone beneath it.

## Endosteum

Endosteum is the lining of the marrow cavity and the covering of spongy bone. It is a
cellular lining rather than a two layer membrane, and it holds mesenchymal stem cells,
osteoblasts and osteoclasts.

## Why both matter

Both surfaces carry the progenitor populations, so both are places where new bone can be
added or old bone taken away. A section labeled "decalcified bone" lets you see these
cellular layers, since decalcification removes the mineral and allows the tissue to be
sectioned conventionally.

> Sharpey's fibers are collagen bundles that anchor periosteum to bone, and seeing them crossing from the covering into the matrix identifies the periosteal surface.

Library: Compact Bone in Ground Section: Osteons and Lamellar Patterns
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s10.jpg)

## What a ground section is

A ground section is bone that has been abraded thin rather than decalcified and sectioned,
so the mineralized architecture survives and the cells do not. What you see is the
geometry: canals, lamellae, lacunae and canaliculi, all in shades of grey or brown with no
histologic stain.

## What to find in the field

- The osteon, a cylinder of bone running longitudinally along the shaft, which is the main
  microstructural unit of cortical bone
- Concentric, or Haversian, lamellae, the rings making up each osteon
- The Haversian canal at the center of each osteon, carrying blood vessels and nerves
- Volkmann's canals, running transversely and connecting Haversian canals to each other
  and to the outside vasculature
- Circumferential lamellae, running as long parallel sheets around the whole circumference
  of the shaft just under the periosteal surface
- Osteocyte lacunae with canaliculi radiating from them

## Woven against lamellar

Woven bone, also called primary or immature bone, is laid down during development and as
the first bone after a fracture. Its collagen bundles are randomly interwoven and its
lacunae are randomly distributed. Lamellar bone, also called secondary or mature bone, is
arranged in layers, with the collagen within a lamella running parallel and the collagen in
adjacent lamellae running perpendicular, and that alternation is what gives the structure
its strength.

> Concentric lamellae circle a canal and belong to one osteon, while circumferential lamellae run around the whole shaft.

Library: Bone Cell Lineages and the Build, Crunch, Sense, Line Roster
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s11.jpg)

## Two stem cell lineages

| Lineage | Bone products | Other products |
|---|---|---|
| Mesenchymal stem cell | Osteoblast, and from it the osteocyte and the bone lining cell | Chondroblast and chondrocyte, adipocyte, myocyte, fibroblast |
| Hematopoietic stem cell | Osteoclast | Macrophage, lymphocytes, blood cells |

Hematopoietic stem cells sit in the bone marrow. Mesenchymal stem cells sit in the marrow,
in the periosteum and in the endosteum, and also around the vasculature, which is how
progenitors reach a remodeling site.

Three of the four bone cells are one lineage wearing different hats, because the osteocyte
and the bone lining cell are both former osteoblasts. The osteoclast is the outsider, a
blood cell relative whose precursors are monocytes and macrophages, which is why it is the
only bone cell that is multinucleate.

## The lecturer's memory hook for the four jobs

| Cell | Word | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Osteoblast | Build | Secretes osteoid onto a surface |
| Osteoclast | Crunch | Solubilizes mineral and matrix in a lacuna |
| Osteocyte | Sense | Detects load and mineral status, then directs the other cells |
| Bone lining cell | Line | Covers quiescent surfaces and stands in reserve |

The osteocyte, locked in the matrix, is the only cell positioned to feel what the tissue is
experiencing, so it is the one that issues orders. The osteoblast and osteoclast carry
those orders out on surfaces, and the bone lining cell holds the surface in the meantime
and supplies replacement osteoblasts when the order to build arrives.

> Osteoblast, osteocyte, bone lining cell and chondrocyte are all mesenchymal; only the osteoclast is hematopoietic, and only the sensing cell cannot build or resorb.

Library: Osteoclasts: Appearance, Stain and Resorption Machinery
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s12.jpg)

## Function

Osteoclasts solubilize bone, and they do it to both fractions separately. The organic
matrix is digested by secreted proteases such as cathepsin K, and the mineral is dissolved
by secreted acidic hydrogen ions, with ion pumps generating hydrochloric acid in the cavity
under the cell.

## Appearance

- Large and multinucleated, the product of fusion of as many as 30 mononuclear macrophages
- Sitting in a resorption cavity called a Howship's lacuna on a compact or spongy surface
- A ruffled border made of folded plasma membrane facing the bone
- A sealing zone around the perimeter, where integrins bind osteopontin outside the cell
  and the actin cytoskeleton holds the seal inside it
- Numerous lysosomes

## Stain

Tartrate resistant acid phosphatase, abbreviated TRAP, is the enzymatic histochemical
stain. A TRAP positive cell sitting in a scalloped bay on a bone surface is an osteoclast
and nothing else, which is why the stain is described as conclusive identification.

## Origin

Mononuclear hematopoietic stem cells, by way of monocytes and the macrophages they become.

> Multinucleate, in a scalloped lacuna, TRAP positive: that combination is the osteoclast.

Library: Osteoclast Fusion and the RANKL Pathway
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s13.jpg)

## The sequence

A bone marrow precursor becomes a preosteoclast under M-CSF, several preosteoclasts fuse
into a multinucleate polykaryon, and the polykaryon is activated into a resorbing
osteoclast. Fusion is what turns a mononuclear precursor into the large multinucleated cell
seen on a bone surface.

## Who gives the signal

RANKL is presented by osteoblasts and osteocytes, either membrane bound or soluble, and it
binds RANK on the osteoclast lineage cell. That binding drives
differentiation, fusion and activation, so the end result is bone resorption.

Osteoprotegerin, abbreviated OPG, is released by the same bone cells and acts as a decoy.
It binds RANKL before RANKL can reach RANK, so resorption falls.

## Why the pathway is clinically loaded

Because resorption is controlled by the ratio of RANKL to OPG coming from bone forming
cells, an antibody against RANK ligand (denosumab) takes away the signal the osteoclast
depends on. The lecture groups it with the other anti-resorptive drugs, all of which target
osteoclasts to decrease bone resorption.

> Osteoclasts do not decide their own fate: osteoblasts and osteocytes set the RANKL to OPG ratio that drives them.

Library: Osteoporosis and Osteopetrosis as Disorders of Osteoclast Activity
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s14.jpg)

## The two ends of one axis

| | Osteoporosis | Osteopetrosis |
|---|---|---|
| Osteoclast activity | Too much resorption | Too little resorption, defective osteoclasts |
| Nickname | Porous bones | Marble bone disease |
| Cause | Resorption outruns formation | Osteoclasts fail, so resorption does not keep up |
| Bone quantity | Reduced, thinned and disconnected trabeculae | Increased, abnormally dense |
| Why it fractures | Too little bone to carry load | Dense but abnormal bone, and medullary canals can be obliterated |

## Scale of osteoporosis

The lecture puts it at over 50 million Americans affected, with 50% of women and 25% of men
at risk of a catastrophic fracture in their lifetime.

## What each looks like

On scanning electron microscopy, osteoporotic trabecular bone shows thin, perforated and
disconnected struts where normal bone shows a continuous plate and rod network. On
radiographs, vertebral bodies lose height and wedge or collapse as the trabecular
compartment fails. Osteopetrotic bone is the opposite picture, a uniformly dense, poorly
modeled bone with the marrow space encroached on.

> Both diseases fracture, so density alone is the finding: too little bone and too much bone both break.

Library: Osteoblasts and the Osteoid Seam
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s15.jpg)

## Function

Osteoblasts synthesize and secrete unmineralized bone matrix, called osteoid, along with
glycoproteins and other proteins that regulate the deposition of mineral into it. Because
they can only work on a surface, bone grows appositionally and never interstitially, which
is a direct consequence of having a hard matrix that cells cannot expand from within.

## Appearance

Mononucleated and roughly cuboidal, usually standing shoulder to shoulder in a row on a
bone surface, with a pale unmineralized osteoid seam lying between the row of cells and the
mineralized bone beneath.

## Stain

Alkaline phosphatase is the enzymatic histochemical marker. Matrix vesicles released by
osteoblasts carry this enzyme on their surface as an ectoenzyme, where it generates
phosphate that combines with calcium and precipitates as hydroxyapatite crystals into the
collagen bundles.

## Origin

Mesenchymal stem cells, from the marrow, from around the vasculature or from the
periosteum. Bone lining cells, which are inactive osteoblasts, can also be reactivated into
working osteoblasts.

> A row of plump cuboidal cells sitting on a pale seam of matrix is an osteoblast layer making osteoid.

Library: Mineralization of Osteoid and Its Systemic Regulators
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s16.jpg)

## The local process

Mineralization is described as a passive process. Secretory vesicles released by the
osteoblast cause mineral crystals to nucleate and then grow within the organic matrix that
has already been secreted. Osteocalcin binds calcium, alkaline phosphatase on the surface
of matrix vesicles generates phosphate from matrix molecules, and the resulting high local
concentrations of calcium and phosphate precipitate as hydroxyapatite onto the surface of
collagen fibers.

## The systemic regulators

| Ion controlled | Regulators |
|---|---|
| Calcium | Vitamin D, parathyroid hormone (PTH), calcitonin (CT) |
| Phosphate | Fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) |

Vitamin D and PTH raise serum calcium, through the gut and the kidney as well as through
bone, while calcitonin lowers it. FGF23 is the one the lecture singles out for phosphate.

## Why the distinction matters

Osteoid forms first and mineral follows, so anything that blocks mineralization leaves wide
seams of unmineralized matrix on surfaces where osteoblasts are still working normally.
That is the histologic definition of osteomalacia.

> Vitamin D, PTH and calcitonin are the calcium regulators; FGF23 is the phosphate one.

Library: Osteocytes: Lacunae, Canaliculi and Mechanosensation
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s17.jpg)

## Function

The osteocyte is the primary mechanosensor of bone and also senses and helps regulate
mineral homeostasis. In both roles it directs osteoblasts and osteoclasts to adapt the
matrix, so it controls turnover without doing any of the building or breaking itself.

## Appearance

- Small, mononucleated, with a high nucleus to cytoplasm ratio
- Completely encased in mineralized matrix, sitting in a lacuna, which translates as a
  little lake
- Artifactual shrinkage usually leaves a visible gap between cell and lacunar wall
- Dendritic cellular processes, the filopodia, run through canaliculi, the little canals,
  and connect to neighboring cells through gap junctions

## Origin

Former osteoblasts, from the mesenchymal lineage, that became trapped in the matrix they
were depositing. Osteocytes are non-mitotic, so they do not proliferate and the population
cannot be replaced except by trapping new osteoblasts.

## Why the network has a size limit

Nutrients reach osteocytes by diffusion through the canaliculi around their processes,
which puts a ceiling on how far an osteocyte can sit from a blood vessel. That constraint
is one reason the osteon is built around a central canal.

> The osteocyte is the only bone cell inside the matrix, and it is the one that tells the other three what to do.

Library: Osteocyte Signals That Control Formation and Resorption
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s20.jpg)

## The four molecules

| Target | Molecule | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Osteoclasts | RANKL, M-CSF | Increase resorption |
| Osteoclasts | OPG | Decrease resorption |
| Osteoblasts | Sclerostin, Dkk1 | Decrease formation |

## How to keep them straight

Everything aimed at the osteoclast is about whether RANKL reaches RANK, so RANKL and M-CSF
push resorption up and OPG, as a decoy for RANKL, pulls it down. Everything aimed at the
osteoblast in this list is inhibitory, since both sclerostin and Dkk1 turn formation down.

## Where the drugs land

An antibody against sclerostin (romosozumab) releases the brake on formation, which is why
it is grouped with the anabolic drugs rather than the anti-resorptives.

> Osteocytes have an on switch and an off switch for resorption (RANKL and OPG) but only off switches for formation (sclerostin and Dkk1).

Library: Bone Lining Cells and the Quiescent Surface
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s21.jpg)

## Function

Bone lining cells act as a barrier covering bone surfaces that are not currently being
built or resorbed, and they serve as a quiescent osteoblast progenitor population that can
be reactivated.

## Appearance

Flat and highly elongated, with a spindle shaped nucleus, forming a thin unbroken layer
directly on the mineralized surface. Some textbooks call them inactive osteoblasts, which
is exactly what they are.

## Origin

Former osteoblasts that finished working and did not become entrapped in the matrix, so
they are mesenchymal lineage like the osteocyte.

## How to tell the surface apart

A surface covered by plump cuboidal cells over a pale seam is forming bone. A surface
covered by flat elongated cells with no seam is quiescent. A scalloped surface with a large
multinucleate cell in the bay is resorbing. All three can appear within a few hundred
microns of each other on the same trabecula.

> Flat spindle shaped cells on a smooth surface mean the surface is resting, not that nothing lives there.

Library: Bone Modeling Against Bone Remodeling
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s24.jpg)

## The two processes side by side

| | Modeling | Remodeling |
|---|---|---|
| Cell action | Osteoclasts or osteoblasts acting independently | Osteoclasts and osteoblasts coupled in a team |
| Surfaces | Formation and resorption on different surfaces | Both on the same surface |
| Coupled | No | Yes |
| Net result | Net gain of bone mass during growth | Net change can go either way |
| Main role | Major regulator of bone size and shape, including long bone diameters, with age | Alters structure, repairs damage, assists calcium homeostasis |

## Net gain belongs to growth, not to age

The net gain of bone mass is part of how the lecture defines modeling during bone growth.
Modeling keeps acting on size and shape with age, but it does not keep adding bone: the
aging figure shows apposition continuing on the periosteal surface while resorption
proceeds on the endocortical surface, so the cortex ends up wider and thinner at the same
time. Read the net gain line as a statement about the growing skeleton.

## The trap in the word coupled

Coupled does not mean balanced. Coupling only says that osteoblasts are recruited to the
same surface the osteoclasts just resorbed. Whether the cavity is filled completely,
incompletely or more than completely is a separate question, and that is where net gain or
net loss of bone comes from.

## How coupling is achieved

Proteins such as TGF-beta 1 and IGF-1 are deposited into osteoid while it is being made.
When osteoclastic resorption releases and activates them, they recruit osteoblast
progenitors to the resorbed surface, which is one of the mechanisms that links the two
cells together.

> Modeling shapes a growing bone on separate surfaces; remodeling maintains an existing bone on one surface, and coupled is not the same as balanced.

Library: The Basic Multicellular Unit in Trabecular and Cortical Bone
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s25.jpg)

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s26.jpg)

## What a BMU is

A basic multicellular unit is the team of osteoclasts and osteoblasts that carries out one
episode of remodeling, together with the progenitors and vasculature that supply it.
Progenitor cells typically arrive at the site through the vasculature.

## In trabecular bone

The BMU works along the surface of a trabecula, cutting a shallow cavity and filling it
back in. The compartment is sequestered beneath a canopy of bone lining cells, although
that canopy is often hard to see in histologic preparations.

## In cortical bone

The BMU tunnels straight through the compact bone. Osteoclasts at the front carve out the
tunnel, osteoblasts follow and fill it back in layer by layer, and a central canal is left
open for vasculature. That filling pattern is where concentric lamellae come from, which
means an osteon is the fossil record of a cortical BMU.

## Why the two look so different

Trabecular remodeling is a trench dug on an existing surface, so it shows as a scalloped
bay with cells on it. Cortical remodeling is a tunnel through solid tissue, so it shows in
cross section as a tunnel with osteoclasts at its advancing front or, once finished, as a
complete osteon.

> An osteon is not a building block that was placed there, it is the backfilled tunnel left by a cortical BMU.

Library: Unbalanced Remodeling in Menopause, Aging and Drug Therapy
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s27.jpg)

## The four unbalanced states

| State | Relationship | What changed | Net effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Menopause | Resorption increased, greater than formation | Loss of estrogen signaling raises resorption | Bone loss |
| Aging | Resorption greater than decreased formation | Osteoprogenitors and osteoblasts make less bone | Bone loss |
| PTH therapy (teriparatide) | Resorption less than increased formation | Formation is driven up | Bone gain |
| Bisphosphonate therapy | Decreased resorption, less than formation | Resorption is driven down | Bone gain |

Balanced remodeling is simply resorption equal to formation, which holds bone mass steady.

## Reading the arrows

Both causes of bone loss and both causes of bone gain reach the same endpoint by opposite
routes, so the arrow tells you which limb moved. Menopause and anabolic therapy are both
changes in the direction of more activity, and aging and anti-resorptive therapy are both
changes in the direction of less, yet the pairs give opposite outcomes because the limb
that moved is different.

## The drug classes

Anti-resorptives include bisphosphonates, selective estrogen receptor modulators and
denosumab, an antibody against RANK ligand, all of which target the osteoclast. Anabolic or
osteogenic drugs include teriparatide, abaloparatide and romosozumab, an antibody against
sclerostin, all of which target the osteoblast.

> Name the limb that moved and the direction it moved, because two different stories give bone loss and two different stories give bone gain.

Library: Reading a Human Bone Biopsy: Sampling and Normal Appearance
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s29.jpg)

## Where the sample comes from

Histologic assessment of bone remodeling in a living person is done by biopsy of the iliac
crest. The anterior approach marks a point about 2 cm behind the anterior superior iliac
spine and takes a vertical core with a drill or a manual trephine, while a posterior
approach takes a horizontal core near the sacroiliac joint. The core includes both cortical
tables with the trabecular bone between them, so one sample reports on both compartments,
and it can be imaged intact, for example by microCT, before being sectioned. The procedure
is rarely done today unless a metabolic bone disorder is suspected, because circulating
markers have taken over routine assessment.

## Two stains, two questions

An H and E section of normal bone shows pink mineralized trabeculae with cellular marrow in
the spaces between them. It answers questions about cells and architecture, but it cannot
separate mineralized matrix from unmineralized osteoid, because both are pink.

Goldner's trichrome on an undecalcified section stains mineralized bone green and
unmineralized osteoid red or orange, which makes it the stain for one specific question:
how much of this matrix has been mineralized? Normal bone in Goldner's is mostly green
matrix with only a thin red rim where osteoblasts are laying down osteoid that has not yet
mineralized, with marrow fat and cells in the spaces.

## Why the section must be undecalcified

Decalcification dissolves the mineral, which is the very thing Goldner's is being used to
demonstrate, so a decalcified section cannot report on mineralization status at all.

> If the question is mineralized against unmineralized, the answer needs an undecalcified section and a trichrome, not H and E.

Library: Osteomalacia and Paget's Disease on Bone Biopsy
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s29.jpg)

## Osteomalacia

On Goldner's trichrome the finding is increased unmineralized osteoid. Broad red seams
cloak small cores of green mineralized bone, reversing the normal proportion in which green
dominates and red is a thin rim. The osteoblasts are producing matrix normally, so the
defect is in mineralization rather than in formation.

## Paget's disease of bone

Paget's disease, also called osteitis deformans, is disorganized bone remodeling. It occurs
in focal areas of the skeleton, most often the pelvis, skull, spine and legs.
Histologically there are areas of excessive osteoclastic resorption, with larger and more
numerous osteoclasts, followed by coupled but disorganized osteoblastic formation. The bone
produced is a mosaic of woven and lamellar bone, and that mosaic in
thick irregular trabeculae is the diagnostic appearance. The summary phrase the lecture
uses is increased turnover with disorganized bone structure.

## Telling the two apart on a slide

Osteomalacia is a staining problem: the matrix is in the right places but the wrong
composition. Paget's is an architecture problem: the matrix is mineralized but the
trabeculae are the wrong shape, the wrong thickness and assembled out of mismatched pieces.

> Increased unmineralized osteoid points to osteomalacia; a mosaic of woven and lamellar bone in thickened disorganized trabeculae points to Paget's disease.

Library: Circulating Markers of Bone Remodeling
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s30.jpg)

## The two panels

| Cell activity measured | Markers |
|---|---|
| Osteoclast, resorption | Type 1 collagen telopeptides, type 1 collagen crosslinks (pyridinolines), TRAP |
| Osteoblast, formation | Type I collagen propeptides, osteocalcin, alkaline phosphatase |

## The logic that sorts them

Resorption breaks collagen apart, so resorption markers are collagen breakdown products
plus the osteoclast's own enzyme, TRAP. Formation builds collagen, so formation markers are
the pieces trimmed off during collagen synthesis, the propeptides, plus the osteoblast's
own products, osteocalcin and alkaline phosphatase.

Collagen appears on both sides of the table, so the fragment type is what decides:
telopeptides and crosslinks come from mature collagen being destroyed, while propeptides
come from new collagen being assembled.

## Why these are used

Serum or plasma markers are commonly measured because they reflect the same osteoblast and
osteoclast activity a biopsy would show, without the biopsy.

> Breakdown fragments and TRAP read out resorption; propeptides, osteocalcin and alkaline phosphatase read out formation.

Library: Fundamentals of Cartilage: Avascular, Aneural and Covered
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s32.jpg)

## Common ground across all cartilage

Every cartilage matrix contains type II collagen and abundant, highly charged GAGs in
proteoglycan aggregates. The subtypes are then separated by which fibrils and fibers are
present: hyaline has type II collagen fibrils only, elastic adds elastic fibers, and
fibrocartilage adds type I collagen fibers.

## Avascular and aneural

There are no blood vessels within cartilage, so cells are nourished by diffusion, helped
along by movement of the body part, and the capacity for healing and regeneration is very
limited. There are also no nerve fibers within cartilage, so the pain of a cartilage tear
comes from nerves in the perichondrium or in nearby structures rather than from the
cartilage itself.

## Two methods of growth

Appositional growth adds cells and matrix at the surface from the perichondrium.
Interstitial growth divides cells already inside the tissue and secretes new matrix around
them. Cartilage can do both, which is a direct contrast with bone, where the hard matrix
permits appositional growth only.

## Perichondrium and its exceptions

Cartilage is covered by perichondrium, which has a fibrous outer layer of dense irregular
connective tissue and a chondrogenic inner layer holding stem cells for new chondroblasts.
The exceptions are articular cartilage and epiphyseal plate cartilage, which have none.
Fibrocartilage also lacks a perichondrium, because it grades gradually into the dense
connective tissue around it.

> Cartilage grows both appositionally and interstitially; bone only grows appositionally, because its matrix is hard.

Library: Cartilage Matrix Composition by Weight
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s33.jpg)

## The fractions

| Component | Share |
|---|---|
| Intercellular water | 60 to 80% |
| Collagens | 15% |
| Proteoglycans (aggrecan) | 9% |
| Multiadhesive glycoproteins | 5% |
| Cells | 3 to 5% |

Within the collagen fraction, type II makes up about 80%, with types IX and XI making up
about 15% and types III, VI, X, XII and XIV making up the remaining 5%.

## What the numbers mean

More than half of cartilage's wet weight is water, and type II collagen fibrils are about
15% of the wet weight but about 40% of the dry weight of hyaline cartilage. Cells are a
very small share of the tissue, which is consistent with a tissue whose mechanical behavior
comes almost entirely from its matrix.

## Other ground substance components

Chondroitin 4-sulfate and chondroitin 6-sulfate are the common GAGs, with keratan sulfate
less common. Chondronectin is the glycoprotein that attaches chondrocytes to collagen, in a
way analogous to how fibronectin works elsewhere.

> Cartilage is mostly water held in place by proteoglycans, which is why it behaves like a hydrated gel rather than a solid.

Library: Aggrecan, Hyaluronan and the Water That Carries Load
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s34.jpg)

## The aggregate

Proteoglycan monomers such as aggrecan are bound along a backbone of hyaluronic acid,
anchored by link protein, giving the bottlebrush structure the lecture draws. Each
proteoglycan carries many GAG side chains.

## Why the structure holds water

The GAG chains are densely negatively charged. Anionic groups repel each other, so the
molecule occupies a large volume and resists being compressed, and the same negative charge
attracts water to form a hydration shell, a gel-like sac of fluid.

## What that does mechanically

The trapped water is what absorbs shock, so the compressive behavior of cartilage comes
from fluid held electrostatically rather than from the collagen. The collagen fibrils
interact with the aggregates and resist tensile forces, so the two components divide the
job the way mineral and collagen divide it in bone. The water also matters for nutrition,
since diffusion through that fluid is how an avascular tissue feeds its cells.

> Negative charge on GAGs does two jobs at once: it pushes the molecule open and pulls water in, and that water is what carries compressive load.

Library: Territorial and Interterritorial Matrix
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s35.jpg)

## The two zones

Matrix immediately surrounding a lacuna, or an isogenous group, is the territorial matrix.
It stains more intensely because the concentration of acidic GAGs is higher there. Matrix
lying between lacunae is the interterritorial matrix, and it stains more lightly because
the GAGs are diluted by intercellular fluid.

## How to read it on a slide

The pattern appears as a dark halo around each cell or cell cluster against a paler
background. The halo is the matrix that stains most intensely, because that is where the
concentration of acidic GAGs is highest.

## Why it exists

Chondrocytes secrete matrix, so the matrix nearest the cell is the newest and the most
concentrated in the cell's products. The gradient away from the cell is a record of how far
those products have diffused and how much they have been diluted.

> A dark rim around a lacuna is not a membrane, it is matrix with a higher concentration of acidic GAGs.

Library: Collagen Fibrils Against Collagen Fibers in Cartilage
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s36.jpg)

## The distinction

| | Fibrils | Fibers |
|---|---|---|
| Diameter | Small | Larger, and can group further into bundles |
| Refractive index | The same as the other extracellular matrix components | Different enough to be seen |
| Visible by light microscopy | No | Yes |

## Why this one distinction organizes the whole cartilage section

Hyaline cartilage contains type II collagen fibrils only, so its matrix looks homogeneous
and glassy under the light microscope even though it is full of collagen. Elastic cartilage
adds elastic fibers and fibrocartilage adds type I collagen fibers, and because those are
fibers rather than fibrils they are visible, which is precisely what lets you tell the
three types apart on a slide.

A matrix that looks empty is therefore not a matrix without collagen. It is a matrix whose
collagen is invisible because the fibrils are too small and share a refractive index with
everything around them.

> You are not identifying cartilage types by what collagen is present, you are identifying them by which collagen you can actually see.

Library: Chondroblasts, Chondrocytes and Isogenous Groups
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s37.jpg)

## The two cells

Chondroblasts sit in the inner, chondrogenic layer of the perichondrium and produce a
matrix of mostly type II collagen and proteoglycans. Once a cell is completely surrounded
by matrix it is called a chondrocyte and the space it occupies is a lacuna. Chondrocytes
are rounded, and artifactual shrinkage during processing usually leaves a visible gap
between the cell and the wall of its lacuna.

## Appositional growth

Cells differentiate within the perichondrium and then become trapped in the matrix they
have just synthesized, so new tissue is added at the surface. On a slide this reads as a
gradient: flattened fibroblast-like cells in the fibrous layer, flatter chondroblasts
beneath them, and progressively rounder chondrocytes deeper in.

## Interstitial growth and isogenous groups

Chondrocytes already inside the tissue divide and secrete new matrix, expanding the
cartilage from within. An isogenous group is a cluster of chondrocytes generated by recent
mitosis, where there has not yet been time to lay down matrix between the daughter cells.
Finding such clusters is direct evidence that interstitial growth is happening.

> An isogenous group is a family of recent daughter cells sharing one territory, and it is the histologic signature of interstitial growth.

Library: Hyaline Cartilage: The Glassy Default
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s41.jpg)

## Matrix and appearance

Matrix is primarily type II collagen fibrils and proteoglycans, with no fibers visible by
light microscopy, so the matrix looks homogeneous. Hyaline means glassy, and in fresh
tissue the cartilage is semitransparent with an opalescent blue color. Histologically it
is basophilic, because the negatively charged GAGs pick up a basic dye such as hematoxylin.

## Where it is and what it does

The most abundant cartilage in the body. It is found in the trachea and other airways, on
joint surfaces, in the costal cartilages and in the growth plate. Its jobs are flexible
support, as in the C shaped rings that keep the airway open while allowing the diameter to
vary, cushioning at the ends of bones, and reducing friction in a joint, where its smooth
surface works with synovial fluid.

Its ability to grow under pressure, through interstitial growth, is what makes it the
tissue of the epiphyseal plate.

> Homogeneous basophilic matrix with chondrocytes in lacunae and no visible fibers is hyaline cartilage.

Library: Elastic Cartilage: Built to Bend and Return
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s42.jpg)

## Matrix and appearance

The matrix is like hyaline, with type II collagen fibrils and proteoglycans, but it also
contains numerous elastic fibers. Those fibers are visible by light microscopy as a dense
branching network running between the lacunae. The tissue is more cellular than hyaline
cartilage and obvious isogenous groups are not a feature. Fresh elastic cartilage is yellow
in color.

## Properties and location

The elastic fibers raise flexibility relative to hyaline, and the tissue is designed to
withstand repeated bending and return to its original position. It is not as supportive as
hyaline and cannot grow under pressure the way hyaline can. It is found in the auricle of
the ear, the external auditory meatus, the eustachian tubes, the epiglottis and some
laryngeal cartilages.

> Dense branching fibers between the lacunae and a more crowded field than hyaline is elastic cartilage.

Library: Fibrocartilage: Chondrocytes in Rows Between Collagen Bundles
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s43.jpg)

## Matrix and appearance

The matrix is like hyaline but with abundant type I collagen fibers added, and those fibers
are thick enough to see. Chondrocytes are few and are organized in longitudinal rows
between the collagen bundles, in either a herring bone pattern or a columnar pattern. Fresh
fibrocartilage is white, because of the fibers.

## Properties and location

Fibrocartilage forms tough, compressible connections and resists both compressive and
tensile forces, which is why it is the cartilage of the intervertebral disc, the pubic
symphysis and the meniscus. It is often found as a transition between dense regular
connective tissue and bone, or between dense regular connective tissue and hyaline
cartilage, which is the reason type I collagen is present alongside type II.

Because it grades gradually into the surrounding dense connective tissue, fibrocartilage
has no perichondrium.

> Sparse chondrocytes lined up in rows between visible parallel collagen bundles is fibrocartilage, and the type I collagen is why the fibers are visible at all.

Library: Comparing the Three Cartilage Types
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s40.jpg)

## The comparison in one table

| | Hyaline | Elastic | Fibrocartilage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matrix beyond type II fibrils | Nothing visible | Elastic fibers | Type I collagen fibers |
| Light microscopy | Homogeneous, basophilic | Branching fiber network | Thick parallel bundles |
| Cell arrangement | Lacunae, isogenous groups | Numerous cells, no obvious isogenous groups | Few cells, in longitudinal rows |
| Fresh color | Opalescent blue | Yellow | White |
| Perichondrium | Present, except articular and growth plate | Present | Absent |
| Main property | Flexible support, low friction, grows under pressure | Repeated bending with return | Tough and compressible, resists tension |
| Examples | Trachea, joint surface, growth plate, costal | Ear, epiglottis, eustachian tube | Intervertebral disc, pubic symphysis, meniscus |

## How to run the comparison on an unknown slide

Look at the matrix first, because that is where all three differ. A homogeneous field
means hyaline. Visible fibers then split into two: a dense branching network with crowded
cells is elastic, and thick parallel bundles with sparse cells in rows is fibrocartilage.
Only after the matrix question is settled is it worth looking at the cells, since the cell
arrangement follows from what the matrix is made of.

> All three contain type II collagen, so the identification turns entirely on what else is in the matrix and whether you can see it.

Library: Why Avascular Cartilage Does Not Heal: Osteoarthritis and Microfracture
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone and Cartilage Histopathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonehisto-s44.jpg)

## The problem

Cartilage is avascular, so damage will not heal. In osteoarthritis the articular surfaces
become fibrillated and damaged, and the healthy smooth surface is replaced by a surface
with clefts and irregularity. Compared side by side, healthy articular cartilage has an
even surface with evenly spaced chondrocytes, while osteoarthritic cartilage shows surface
fibrillation and disorganized cells.

## Microfracture surgery

The damaged cartilage is debrided and holes are drilled into the underlying subchondral
bone, which lets mesenchymal stem cells from the marrow form a clot in the defect. That
clot differentiates into cartilage, but typically fibrocartilage rather than hyaline.

## Why it fails

Fibrocartilage is not suited to the loading environment of an articular surface, which is a
job hyaline cartilage does because of its smooth, low friction, homogeneous matrix.
Function returns quickly, but the long term failure rate is 66% with a mean time to failure
of 4.0 years. The lecture lists NBA and NFL players who have had the operation to make the
point that this is a common athletic procedure with a known expiry date.

> Microfracture works by recruiting marrow stem cells through the subchondral plate, and it fails because what they make is fibrocartilage, not hyaline.

Library: Bone Strength in Non-Neoplastic Disease: Quantity, Quality, Mineralization, Architecture
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

## Identify it

- The lecture opens with one organizing idea: bone strength depends on quantity, quality, mineralization, and architecture.
- Every disorder in this lecture, inherited or acquired, damages one of those four and leaves the others relatively intact.
- Naming which of the four is broken is what lets you predict the histology, the laboratory panel, and the imaging before you have seen any of them.

## Key features

- Quantity means how much bone is present. Osteoporosis is the pure quantity failure, because the bone that remains is normal in every other respect.
- Quality means how good the matrix itself is. Osteogenesis imperfecta is the quality failure, since the collagen scaffold is abnormal from the start.
- Mineralization means whether calcium and phosphate are being laid into the osteoid that osteoblasts have already made. Osteomalacia is the mineralization failure.
- Architecture means whether the bone is organized in a way that handles load. Paget disease and osteopetrosis both make plenty of bone, and both make it badly.
- Dense bone on a radiograph tells you about quantity, not about strength. Osteopetrosis is radiographically dense and still fractures.

> Bone strength depends on quantity, quality, mineralization, and architecture, and each non-neoplastic bone disease in this lecture breaks one of those four.

Library: The Four Coupled Processes Behind Metabolic Bone Disease
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonepath-s03.jpg)

## Identify it

- The deck frames bone biology as four processes that run continuously and stay coupled to one another.
- Formation is osteoblasts making osteoid and regulating matrix deposition.
- Mineralization is calcium and phosphate crystallizing as hydroxyapatite within that osteoid.
- Resorption is osteoclasts removing old or damaged bone.
- Remodeling is formation and resorption running together so that bone adapts to the load it carries.

## Key features

- Formation and mineralization are separate steps, and that separation is the whole reason osteomalacia exists as a distinct disease. Osteoid gets made, and then it doesn't get mineralized.
- Resorption and formation are normally coupled, which is why a resorption defect such as osteopetrosis produces dense bone rather than simply less turnover.
- When remodeling becomes uncoupled in both direction and tempo, as it does in Paget disease, the result is abundant bone with disorganized internal structure.
- Osteoporosis is the coupling failing quantitatively rather than qualitatively, with resorption outpacing formation over years.

> Different mechanisms create different tissue-level signatures, so identifying which of the four processes has failed predicts what you will see down the microscope.

Library: Three Tissue-Level Signatures: Too Little, Poorly Mineralized, Disorganized
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

## Identify it

- The lecture reduces the three acquired disorders to three tissue-level signatures, and this triad is the backbone of the whole second half of the deck.
- Too little bone is osteoporosis, where bone mass is reduced and the matrix that remains is normally mineralized.
- Poorly mineralized bone is osteomalacia, where excess unmineralized osteoid accumulates.
- Disorganized turnover is Paget disease, with mosaic lamellar bone and abnormal remodeling.

## Key features

- These three are not points on a single severity scale. They are three different lesions, and each one has a different laboratory and imaging fingerprint.
- Osteoporosis and osteomalacia are both conditions of weak bone in adults, which is exactly why the lecture keeps contrasting them. Osteoporosis has less normal bone, and osteomalacia has normal amounts of abnormal bone.
- Paget disease is the odd one out, because the bone is usually increased rather than decreased in the affected area.
- The question the deck wants you to ask first is whether there is too little bone, unmineralized bone, or too much disorganized turnover.

> Too little bone, unmineralized bone, or disorganized turnover: picking the right one of those three is the first and most discriminating step in every vignette in this lecture.

Library: Achondroplasia: FGFR3 Activation and Impaired Endochondral Ossification
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonepath-s04.jpg)

## Identify it

- Achondroplasia is caused by an activating variant in FGFR3, inherited as an autosomal dominant trait, though most cases arise de novo.
- Excess FGFR3 signaling inhibits chondrocyte proliferation in the growth plate.
- The growth plate shows reduced orderly columnar organization and decreased endochondral growth.

## Key features

- The receptor is doing too much, not too little. This is a gain of function, and that is the opposite of the loss of function pattern students tend to assume for an inherited skeletal disease.
- Endochondral bone growth is impaired while intramembranous ossification is relatively preserved, and that single distinction explains the entire phenotype.
- Bones that lengthen through a cartilage model (the limbs) are short. Bones that form directly in membrane (much of the skull vault) are not, which is why the head looks large relative to the limbs.
- The result is short limbs with a relatively normal-sized trunk, and the skull base and midface may be affected.

> Achondroplasia impairs endochondral ossification while leaving intramembranous ossification relatively intact, which is why the limbs are short and the trunk and cranial vault are not.

Library: Achondroplasia: Rhizomelic Shortening and the Clinical Picture
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

## Identify it

- Grossly the long bones are shortened, and the shortening is rhizomelic, meaning it is worst in the proximal segment of each limb (the humerus and the femur).
- The clinical features the lecture names are macrocephaly, frontal bossing, midface hypoplasia, trident hands, and possible spinal stenosis.
- Intelligence is usually normal.

## Key features

- Rhizomelic is the word worth holding on to, because it separates achondroplasia from the diffuse or distal shortening patterns of other skeletal dysplasias.
- Macrocephaly here is relative rather than a brain problem. The cranial vault forms by intramembranous ossification and grows normally while the limbs do not.
- Midface hypoplasia reflects the skull base, which does use an endochondral mechanism.
- The complications that matter clinically relate to airway, neurologic, and orthopedic anatomy rather than to the bone itself, with spinal stenosis the classic example.

> Rhizomelic limb shortening with a normal-sized trunk, macrocephaly, and normal intelligence is the clinical signature of achondroplasia.

Library: Osteogenesis Imperfecta: COL1A1, COL1A2, and a Fragile Matrix
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonepath-s05.jpg)

## Identify it

- Osteogenesis imperfecta usually comes from COL1A1 or COL1A2 variants, producing either a reduced quantity of type I collagen or collagen that is structurally abnormal.
- The tissue consequence is poor-quality osteoid and fragile bone, so fractures occur with minimal trauma.
- Inheritance is often autosomal dominant, and severity ranges widely, including lethal perinatal forms.

## Key features

- The defect is in the matrix scaffold itself. Osteoblasts are present and working, and mineralization is not the problem.
- Histology shows thin cortices, reduced trabecular bone, and in some forms disorganized or immature bone.
- Because type I collagen is not confined to bone, the disease shows up in sclera, teeth, ears, and ligaments as well as in the skeleton.
- Severity varies enormously between families and between variants, which is why the clinical spectrum runs from mildly increased fracture risk to death in the perinatal period.

> The primary defect in osteogenesis imperfecta is the collagen matrix, not a failure to mineralize osteoid as in osteomalacia.

Library: Osteogenesis Imperfecta: Skeletal and Extra-Skeletal Clues
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

## Identify it

- Skeletal findings are recurrent fractures, deformity, short stature, and wormian bones.
- Extra-skeletal findings are blue or gray sclerae, dentinogenesis imperfecta, hearing loss, and ligamentous laxity.
- Radiographs in a child show osteopenia, bowed long bones, and healed fractures at different ages.

## Key features

- The extra-skeletal findings are what make the diagnosis obvious at the bedside, because nothing else in this lecture turns the sclerae blue or wrecks the dentition.
- Blue-gray sclerae occur because the sclera is thin and abnormal, so the underlying choroid shows through.
- Hearing loss comes from the ossicles and the otic capsule, which are also built from type I collagen.
- Wormian bones are extra intrasutural bones in the skull, and they belong to the inherited fracture picture rather than to any acquired disorder.
- A child with repeated fractures after minor trauma is the setting where this matters most, because the extra-skeletal clues are what separate a collagen disease from everything else on the list.

> In a child with repeated low-trauma fractures, blue-gray sclerae and abnormal dentition point to osteogenesis imperfecta rather than to a mineralization disorder.

Library: Osteopetrosis: Failure of Osteoclastic Resorption
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonepath-s06.jpg)

## Identify it

- Osteopetrosis is a heterogeneous group of genetic disorders of osteoclast development or of the acidification and resorption machinery.
- Because resorption fails, bone accumulates as dense sclerotic bone with abnormal architecture.
- Histology shows retained calcified cartilage and poorly remodeled primary bone.

## Key features

- The failing cell is the osteoclast, which makes this the mirror image of osteoporosis. One disease resorbs too much, the other cannot resorb at all.
- Retained calcified cartilage is the histologic giveaway. In normal development the cartilage model is removed as bone replaces it, and here it is never cleared.
- Primary bone that is never remodeled is mechanically poor, which produces the central paradox of the disease.
- Radiographically dense bone can be brittle and fracture-prone, so high bone density does not mean normal bone strength.

> Osteopetrosis is an osteoclast problem, and the retained calcified cartilage left behind in unremodeled bone is its histologic signature.

Library: Osteopetrosis: Dense Bone, Narrowed Marrow, and Cranial Nerve Effects
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

## Identify it

- Imaging shows diffuse osteosclerosis, and bone-within-bone and sandwich vertebrae may be seen.
- Clinically the lecture names fractures, cranial nerve compression, dental problems, and marrow failure in severe forms.
- Gross specimens show the medullary cavity narrowed or obliterated by dense bone.

## Key features

- Narrowing of the medullary cavity causes anemia, thrombocytopenia, and extramedullary hematopoiesis, because the marrow has nowhere left to live.
- Cranial nerve compression happens because the foramina through which the nerves pass are narrowed by the same unresorbed bone.
- The combination of a hematologic problem and a neurologic problem in a patient with dense bones is specific to this disease among the three inherited disorders.
- Dental problems follow from the same failure of remodeling in the jaws.

> Dense bones with anemia and cranial nerve compression point to osteopetrosis, because unresorbed bone crowds out both the marrow cavity and the skull foramina.

Library: Inherited Bone Disorders Side by Side: Defect, Process, Classic Clue

## Identify it

- The lecture lays the three inherited disorders out against three columns, because these are the three that get confused with one another.

| Achondroplasia | FGFR3 activation | Reduced endochondral growth | Rhizomelic short limbs |

Library: Acquired Bone Disorders: Scarce, Unmineralized, or Disorganized Bone
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonepath-s08.jpg)

## Identify it

- Osteoporosis is a problem of decreased bone quantity. The matrix is normally mineralized, there is simply less of it, and the clinical outcome is fragility fractures.
- Osteomalacia is defective mineralization of osteoid in adults, unmineralized matrix accumulates, and the outcome is bone pain, weakness, and pseudofractures.
- Paget disease is focal, excessive, disorganized remodeling. Bone becomes enlarged and architecturally abnormal, and the outcome is deformity, pain, fracture, and hearing effects.

## Key features

- Each of the three has a different primary issue, and each has a clinical outcome that follows directly from it.
- Fragility fractures follow from having less bone. Bone pain and proximal weakness follow from soft, poorly mineralized bone. Deformity and enlargement follow from excess disorganized turnover.
- Hearing loss shows up in Paget disease because the skull is a favored site and the bone around the auditory apparatus enlarges.
- The clinical outcomes are not interchangeable, so the presenting complaint is already a clue before any test is ordered.

> The first question in any acquired bone disease vignette is whether there is too little bone, unmineralized bone, or too much disorganized turnover.

Library: Osteoporosis: Resorption Outpacing Formation
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonepath-s09.jpg)

## Identify it

- Osteoporosis is low bone strength due to reduced bone mass and deterioration of microarchitecture.
- The mechanism is resorption exceeding formation, which may be age-related, postmenopausal, medication-related, or secondary to another condition.
- Histology shows thinned trabeculae, cortical thinning, and increased porosity, and the remaining bone is normally mineralized.

## Key features

- Gross specimens of vertebral bodies show sparse thin trabeculae, a thin cortical shell, and loss of vertebral height from compression.
- The phrase worth memorizing is that the remaining bone is normally mineralized. That one clause is what separates osteoporosis from osteomalacia at the tissue level.
- Clinically the fractures are vertebral compression, hip, and distal radius fractures after low-energy trauma.
- Low-energy means the fracture is out of proportion to the force, which is why a fall from standing height counts and a car accident does not.

> Osteoporosis is a quantity problem and not an osteoid mineralization problem, so the trabeculae are thin but the bone that is there is mineralized normally.

Library: Osteoporosis: The DXA T-Score and a Normal Laboratory Panel
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

## Identify it

- On DXA, a T-score of -2.5 or lower at a qualifying site supports densitometric osteoporosis in postmenopausal women and in men aged 50 and over.
- Routine labs are often normal, and secondary causes are investigated when the clinical picture suggests them.
- Imaging may reveal the disease before symptoms, through vertebral height loss or a fragility fracture.

## Key features

- The normal laboratory panel is diagnostically useful rather than unhelpful. Calcium, phosphate, and alkaline phosphatase that come back normal in a patient with low bone density argue against both osteomalacia and Paget disease.
- This is the only one of the three acquired disorders in which the lecture expects the routine panel to be normal.
- The T-score threshold is one of the few numeric cut-offs the lecture states, and it applies to a qualifying site rather than to any measurement anywhere.
- A fragility fracture can be the first sign, which is why the disease is often described as silent until it is not.

> Low bone density with a normal routine laboratory panel is the osteoporosis pattern, and it is the normal labs that rule out the other two acquired disorders.

Library: Osteomalacia: Osteoid Laid Down but Not Mineralized
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonepath-s10.jpg)

## Identify it

- Osteomalacia is inadequate mineralization of newly formed osteoid in adults.
- Common causes are vitamin D deficiency, phosphate depletion, malabsorption, and renal phosphate wasting.
- Histology shows increased unmineralized osteoid, and mineralization lag time is prolonged when assessed by tetracycline labeling.

## Key features

- Osteoblasts are working normally here. They are producing osteoid at a normal rate, and the step that fails is the deposition of hydroxyapatite into it.
- The result is bone that is soft rather than scarce, which is why gross specimens show long bones bowed along their length rather than fractured across.
- Clinically the patient has diffuse bone pain, proximal muscle weakness, gait difficulty, and insufficiency fractures.
- Proximal muscle weakness is the clue students most often skip. Difficulty rising from a chair or climbing stairs belongs to this disease and not to osteoporosis.
- Unmineralized osteoid on a von Kossa preparation sits as a thick uncalcified layer on the surface of trabeculae that are calcified in their cores.

> In osteomalacia the osteoid is made and then not mineralized, so the bone is soft and bows rather than being simply reduced in amount.

Library: Osteomalacia: Vitamin D, Phosphate, and the Childhood Form
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

## Identify it

- In the typical vitamin D deficiency pattern, 25-OH vitamin D is low and calcium may be low or normal.
- Phosphate is often low and alkaline phosphatase is often elevated, and PTH may be elevated.
- Imaging may show Looser zones, also called pseudofractures.

## Key features

- The laboratory panel in osteomalacia is abnormal in several places at once, and that is what separates it from osteoporosis, where the panel is usually normal.
- Alkaline phosphatase rises because osteoblasts are active and producing osteoid, even though that osteoid is not being mineralized.
- PTH may be elevated, and phosphate is often low, but the lecture lists these as parts of the pattern rather than linking one to the other.
- Looser zones are bands of unmineralized osteoid crossing a bone that look like incomplete fractures on radiographs.
- In children the same defect occurs at the growth plates and is called rickets, which produces a prominent zone of hypertrophic cartilage and bowing of the soft long bones.

> Low vitamin D, low phosphate, and elevated alkaline phosphatase with low or normal calcium is the osteomalacia pattern, and the same defect at the growth plate in a child is rickets.

Library: Paget Disease of Bone: Focal High-Turnover Remodeling
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonepath-s11.jpg)

## Identify it

- Paget disease begins with focal areas of markedly increased osteoclastic resorption, which are then followed by exuberant osteoblastic formation.
- Histology shows a mosaic pattern of lamellar bone separated by irregular cement lines, often described as a jigsaw appearance.
- Grossly and on imaging the bone is enlarged and thickened, with mixed lytic and sclerotic phases.

## Key features

- The word focal matters. This is not a systemic metabolic state like osteoporosis or osteomalacia, it is a disease of particular bones.
- Common sites are the pelvis, the spine, the skull, and the long bones.
- The mosaic pattern comes from repeated cycles of resorption and formation that never line up, leaving cement lines running in every direction through otherwise lamellar bone.
- A calvarium from an affected patient shows markedly thickened coarse diploe, which is the gross counterpart of the histologic disorder.
- Paget bone is abundant but structurally disorganized, so more bone and weaker bone coexist in the same specimen.

> The mosaic pattern of lamellar bone separated by irregular cement lines is the histologic hallmark of Paget disease and reflects repeated uncoordinated cycles of resorption and formation.

Library: Paget Disease: Presentation, Alkaline Phosphatase, and Secondary Osteosarcoma
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

## Identify it

- Paget disease is often asymptomatic, and when symptoms do occur they are bone pain, deformity, warmth, fractures, or hearing loss.
- Alkaline phosphatase is often elevated, while calcium and phosphate are usually normal in uncomplicated disease.
- Rare secondary osteosarcoma is the complication the lecture names, and high-output cardiac failure is uncommon and associated with extensive disease.

## Key features

- An isolated elevation of alkaline phosphatase with normal calcium and phosphate is the classic incidental finding that leads to the diagnosis.
- Warmth over an affected bone reflects the increased vascularity of high-turnover bone, and that same vascularity is what underlies the rare high-output cardiac failure.
- Hearing loss follows from skull involvement, and increasing hat size is the way patients themselves notice the skull enlarging.
- A new destructive mass arising in bone already affected by Paget disease raises the possibility of secondary osteosarcoma, and gross specimens show a fleshy tumour breaking out through thickened abnormal cortex.
- New pain in a patient with known stable Paget disease is the clinical change that prompts that concern.

> Elevated alkaline phosphatase with normal calcium and phosphate is the laboratory fingerprint of Paget disease, and a new destructive lesion in pagetic bone raises the question of secondary osteosarcoma.

Library: Separating the Three Acquired Bone Disorders on Histology
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

## Identify it

- The lecture calls histology the fastest way to separate osteoporosis, osteomalacia, and Paget disease, and lays them out against four features.

| Feature | Osteoporosis | Osteomalacia | Paget disease |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amount of bone | Reduced | Variable, with osteoid accumulating | Often increased locally |
| Mineralization | Normal | Defective | Mineralized but disorganized |
| Signature | Thin trabeculae and cortex | Excess unmineralized osteoid | Mosaic lamellar bone with cement lines |
| Typical labs | Often normal | Alkaline phosphatase raised, calcium and phosphate may vary | Alkaline phosphatase raised, calcium and phosphate usually normal |

## Key features

- Read the amount row and the mineralization row together. Osteoporosis has less bone that is mineralized properly, osteomalacia has bone that is not mineralized properly, and Paget disease has more bone that is mineralized but assembled wrongly.
- The signature row is what you would actually report from a biopsy, and each of the three signatures is unique to its disease.
- Excess unmineralized osteoid is the single finding that establishes osteomalacia, because neither of the other two produces it.
- Mosaic lamellar bone with irregular cement lines is the single finding that establishes Paget disease.
- Thin trabeculae with a thin cortex and otherwise unremarkable mineralization is what osteoporosis looks like, which is to say it looks like normal bone with pieces missing.

> Excess unmineralized osteoid means osteomalacia and mosaic lamellar bone with cement lines means Paget disease, while osteoporosis is normal bone in reduced amount.

Library: Laboratory Patterns in Metabolic Bone Disease and the Limits of ALP
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

## Identify it

- Osteoporosis typically gives a normal routine panel.
- Osteomalacia typically gives a raised alkaline phosphatase with calcium and phosphate that may vary, classically low phosphate and low or normal calcium with low 25-OH vitamin D.
- Paget disease typically gives a raised alkaline phosphatase with calcium and phosphate that are usually normal.

## Key features

- Alkaline phosphatase is raised in two of the three, so by itself it cannot tell you which disease you are looking at.
- What separates those two is everything else on the panel. Osteomalacia disturbs calcium, phosphate, and vitamin D, while Paget disease leaves them alone.
- Put differently, a raised alkaline phosphatase with an otherwise normal panel points to Paget disease, and a raised alkaline phosphatase with a disturbed mineral panel points to osteomalacia.
- A completely normal panel in a patient with low bone density points to osteoporosis.
- The lecture's own caution is that alkaline phosphatase is a clue to osteoblast activity and is not disease-specific, so it tells you osteoblasts are busy without telling you why.

> Alkaline phosphatase is a clue to osteoblast activity rather than to a diagnosis, so it is the calcium, phosphate, and vitamin D around it that identify the disease.

Library: A Practical Diagnostic Approach: Clinical Pattern, Then Imaging, Then Labs
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

![Lecture slide](library/images/msk-week-15/bonepath-s13.jpg)

## Identify it

- Step one is the clinical pattern: fractures, bone pain, deformity, short limbs, or a family history.
- Step two is imaging: low density, sclerosis, growth-plate changes, pseudofractures, or bone enlargement.
- Step three is labs: calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, PTH, 25-OH vitamin D, and renal function as indicated.

## Key features

- The order is deliberate. Clinical pattern narrows the list before any test is ordered, and the tests then confirm rather than discover.
- Short limbs with a family history belongs to the inherited group, while fractures and bone pain in an adult belongs to the acquired group.
- Each imaging finding in the list maps onto a specific disease. Sclerosis points to osteopetrosis or to the sclerotic phase of Paget disease, growth-plate changes point to rickets or achondroplasia, pseudofractures point to osteomalacia, and enlargement points to Paget disease.
- Renal function appears on the laboratory list because renal phosphate wasting is one of the named causes of osteomalacia.

> Match the dominant mechanism to the findings: the clinical pattern narrows the list, imaging localizes the process, and the laboratory panel names it.

Library: Three Clinical Patterns and the Mechanism Each One Points To
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Non-Neoplastic Bone Pathology

## Identify it

- Low bone density with normal routine labs means you should consider osteoporosis, then assess fracture risk and look for secondary causes.
- Bone pain and weakness with elevated alkaline phosphatase and low vitamin D or phosphate means you should consider osteomalacia and define the cause.
- Focal bony enlargement with a high alkaline phosphatase and characteristic imaging means you should consider Paget disease.

## Key features

- These three lines are the lecture's own compressed answer key, and they combine a symptom, an imaging finding, and a laboratory result into a single pattern for each disease.
- Notice that each pattern contains a laboratory element that excludes the other two, which is why the combinations work where any single finding would not.
- Osteomalacia is the only pattern in which vitamin D or phosphate is abnormal.
- Paget disease is the only pattern that is focal, so bony enlargement in one region rather than generalized change is the structural clue.
- Osteoporosis is defined here partly by what is absent, since the normal routine labs are doing diagnostic work.

> Each of the three acquired disorders has a pattern that combines symptom, imaging, and labs, and no single element of any of those three patterns is diagnostic on its own.

Library: Flat bone architecture and the diploe
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Slide on basic flat bone structure](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s03.jpg)

## Identify it

- A flat bone in cross section is a sandwich, with two plates of compact bone enclosing a layer of
  spongy bone between them.
- In the cranial vault the outer plate is called the external table, the inner plate the internal
  table, and the spongy layer between them the diploe.
- On a radiograph or an axial CT of the cranium the two tables appear as dense white lines and the
  diploe as the lower density band sandwiched between them.

## Key features

- The lecture uses the flat bone as the starting point because this is the shape that
  intramembranous ossification produces directly, without any cartilage model.
- Spongy bone and compact bone are the same tissue arranged differently, and the deposition that
  thickens the plates happens underneath the periosteal covering.

Library: Intramembranous ossification: steps and sites
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Slide on intramembranous ossification](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s04.jpg)

## Identify it

- Flat bones develop directly from connective tissue with no cartilage intermediate at any stage.
- The sites are the cranial vault (frontal, parietal, and parts of the occipital and temporal), the
  mandible and maxilla, the body of the scapula, and the lateral clavicle.

## Key features

1. Mesenchymal fibroblasts condense and differentiate directly into osteoblasts.
2. Those osteoblasts deposit strips and columns of bone within the mesenchyme, and continued
   deposition interconnects the columns into spongy bone.
3. Enhanced deposition on the outside and the inside produces compact bone, which thickens by
   deposition beneath the periosteal covering.
4. All of the bone laid down this way starts as woven bone, and remodeling converts it to lamellar.

- The sequence on the slide runs mesenchymal condensation, ossification center, osteoblast
  progenitors around osteoid and a blood vessel, mineralized bone, and finally a plate with diploe
  and periosteum.

> The defining feature of intramembranous ossification is that mesenchymal cells become osteoblasts directly, so no cartilage model is ever built.

## Identify it

- The same principle is used deliberately in limb lengthening and in mandibular extension surgery,

Library: Long bone architecture and the physis

## Identify it

- The physis goes by four names that all mean the same thing, physis, growth plate, epiphyseal

- On a radiograph an open physis is a transverse lucent band separating the epiphysis from the

Library: Endochondral ossification: the cartilage model and the bone collar
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Slide on endochondral ossification](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s06.jpg)

## Identify it

- Long, short and irregular bones (humerus, carpals, vertebrae) are modeled first in cartilage, and
  that cartilage is then converted to bone.
- The first visible sign of conversion is not inside the cartilage, it is around the outside.

## Key features

1. A mesenchymal condensation forms and the cells in the very center differentiate into
   chondrocytes.
2. Interstitial and appositional cartilage growth builds a mass of hyaline cartilage roughly the
   shape of the future bone, covered by perichondrium.
3. The bone collar is deposited by appositional growth under the periosteum around the midlength of
   the diaphysis, and this is the first bone tissue to appear.
4. Inside the middle of the diaphysis the chondrocytes hypertrophy, calcify the matrix around
   themselves, and then die.

> The bone collar forms before anything inside the cartilage has been replaced, so it is the earliest sign that a cartilage model is becoming bone.

Library: Primary and secondary centers of ossification
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Alizarin stained fetal skeleton showing cartilage and mineralized bone](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s06.jpg)

## Identify it

- The primary center is in the diaphysis and the secondary centers are in the epiphyses, and both
  run the same sequence of events.
- The number of secondary centers and the timing of each one is specific to the individual bone.

## Key features

- Blood vessels migrate into the spaces left by the dead chondrocytes, carrying osteoprogenitor
  cells that deposit bone onto the columns of calcified cartilage, and that vascular invasion in the
  diaphysis is the primary center of ossification.
- Cartilage keeps being added at both ends while cartilage is converted to bone centrally and the
  bone is then resorbed, and the net result is a bone that grows longer.
- Because the process moves along the bone, a single histological section shows the whole temporal
  sequence laid out spatially.

> Vascular invasion is the step that converts calcified cartilage into bone, which is why no ossification center appears until blood vessels arrive.

Library: Growth plate closure and the end of longitudinal growth
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Knee radiographs from childhood through adulthood](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s05.jpg)

## Identify it

- The hyaline cartilage left between the diaphysis and the epiphysis is the epiphyseal (growth)
  plate.
- The plate closes when bone deposition catches up with chondrocyte proliferation, the remaining
  hyaline cartilage disappears, and no further increase in bone length can occur.

## Key features

- Closure is a race between two rates, not a timer. As long as the chondrocytes proliferate faster
  than the advancing bone consumes the plate, the bone keeps lengthening.
- Appositional growth on the periosteal and endosteal surfaces of the diaphysis continues to
  increase the diameter of the bone, and that is a separate process from lengthening at the physis.

> Length comes from the growth plate and width comes from appositional growth on the periosteal and endosteal surfaces.

Library: Appositional growth and the woven to lamellar conversion

## Identify it

- Remodeling then replaces that woven bone with lamellar bone, and the same conversion happens again

## Key features

Library: Achondroplasia as a growth plate disease

Library: Myositis ossificans: mechanism and classic scenarios

## Identify it

- Myositis ossificans is a benign, solitary, self limiting and often self resolving ossifying soft

- The most common presentation is limited range of motion of an adjacent joint, in roughly 20

Library: Fracture healing: the five step sequence

![Six panel fracture healing figure](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s12.jpg)

## Identify it

- Fracture repair is endochondral ossification run on demand, and the lecture lists it as five
  ordered events.

## Key features

1. Blood vessels crossing the fracture line rupture and form a hematoma, with an acute inflammatory
   response.
2. Fibroblasts and mesenchymal cells migrate in and granulation tissue forms around the fracture
   ends.
3. Chondroblasts and osteoblasts proliferate under the periosteum and in the endosteum, and a
   fibrocartilaginous soft callus forms.
4. The soft callus is remodeled into primary woven bone, the hard callus, by endochondral
   ossification. The chondrocytes hypertrophy and die, the cartilage calcifies, blood vessels
   infiltrate, and the calcified cartilage is broken down and turned over into bone.
5. Woven bone is remodeled into lamellar bone and the callus shrinks, because lamellar bone is
   stronger and needs less mass to hold the same stability.

> The soft callus is cartilage and the hard callus is woven bone, so the step between them is exactly the hypertrophy, calcification and vascular invasion of endochondral ossification.

## Identify it

- The objective asks for the timing as well as the sequence, and the figure gives a duration for
  every stage.

## Key features

| Stage | Timing |
|---|---|
| Hematoma | Within hours to days |
| Inflammatory | Begins within 48 hours |
| Granulation tissue | 2 to 12 days |
| Soft callus | One week to several months |
| Hard callus | One week to several months |

Library: Naming the fracture line: transverse, oblique, longitudinal and spiral
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Slide on fracture line direction](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s14.jpg)

## Identify it

- The first classification is by the direction the fracture line takes relative to the long axis of
  the bone.

## Key features

| Pattern | Line relative to the long axis | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Transverse | Perpendicular | Force applied perpendicular to the shaft |
| Diagonal or oblique | Diagonal | Force applied in the same direction as the long axis |
| Longitudinal or linear | Along the axis | Force along the bone |
| Spiral | Winds around the shaft | Torque, such as planting a foot in a hole while running |

- Oblique and spiral are the pair most often confused. An oblique line is a single flat plane cut at
  an angle, while a spiral line twists around the circumference of the shaft.

> A twisting mechanism makes a spiral fracture, while the lecture attributes the oblique one to a force applied in the same direction as the long axis, so the history often names the pattern before the radiograph does.

Library: Incomplete and crush fracture patterns
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Slide on greenstick, torus and compression fractures](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s15.jpg)

## Identify it

- Greenstick and torus fractures are incomplete and are more common in children, and compression
  fractures crush the bone and are common in osteoporosis.

## Key features

| Pattern | What you see | Typical patient |
|---|---|---|
| Greenstick | Incomplete, one cortex broken and the other merely bent | Children |
| Torus or buckle | Buckled cortex, distinct fracture lines often not seen | Children |
| Compression | Bone crushed, looks wider or flatter | Older adults with osteoporosis |

- Torus is the harder of the two paediatric patterns to spot, because there is a bulge in the cortex
  rather than a lucent line.
- Both paediatric patterns reflect the fact that immature bone bends before it breaks.

Library: Describing the position of the distal fragment
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Slide on the relationship of distal to proximal fragment](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s16.jpg)

## Identify it

- This classification is always written from the distal fragment's point of view relative to the
  proximal one.
- Most fractures display more than one of these abnormalities of position at the same time.

## Key features

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Displacement | Amount of off-set of the distal relative to the proximal fragment |
| Angulation | The angle away from normal that the distal fragment makes with the proximal |
| Shortening | Overlapping of the ends of the fracture fragments, described by the length of overlap |

  different projections.

> Displacement is an off-set, shortening is an overlap, and the two can coexist in the same fracture.

## Identify it

- A simple fracture has two fragments and a comminuted fracture has more than two.

## Key features

- The count is of bone fragments, not of fracture lines, and it says nothing about whether the skin

- Comminution usually means a higher energy injury, and the small intermediate fragments are the

Library: Fractures that communicate with the atmosphere
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Slide on closed and open fractures](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s18.jpg)

## Identify it

- A closed fracture has no communication with outside air, and an open or compound fracture
  communicates with outside air because the skin is broken.
- The bone may or may not be visible through the wound in an open fracture.

## Key features

- This classification is about the soft tissue envelope rather than the bone itself, so a
  two fragment fracture can be open and a comminuted one can be closed.
- The clinical photograph on the slide shows an open wound over the fracture, but a small puncture
  wound counts just as much, since what matters is that the fracture communicates with outside air.

> Open and compound are the same thing, and what defines them is continuity with outside air rather than how badly the bone is broken.

Library: Reduction and immobilization after a fracture
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Slide on fracture treatment options](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s19.jpg)

## Identify it

- A fracture must first be reduced, which restores the periosteum and endosteum and makes sure no
  soft tissue is trapped in the fracture, and then the fragments are immobilized until healing is
  done.

## Key features

| Option | What it is |
|---|---|
| Cast immobilization | Plaster or fiberglass cast secures the reduced position |
| Functional cast, splint or brace | Allows limited or controlled movement of nearby joints |
| Traction | Gentle steady pull to align fragments, often temporary |
| External fixation | Pins or screws above and below the fracture connected to a bar or ring outside the skin |
| Open reduction and internal fixation | Screws and plates attached to the bone, or a rod through the marrow space |

- External fixation is the option chosen when the soft tissues are too badly damaged to operate on
  immediately, and it can hold the position until surgery is tolerated.
- Internal fixation is the option that produces the rigid stability under which a fracture can heal
  by intramembranous ossification.

> Reduction restores the periosteum and endosteum and clears soft tissue from the fracture, and everything after it is a method of holding that reduction still.

Library: Vertebral compression fractures: burden and presentation
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Slide on vertebral compression fractures](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s20.jpg)

## Identify it

- Vertebral compression fractures are the most common fragility fracture, with roughly 700,000 per
  year in the United States and 70,000 hospitalizations, at an annual cost around 15 billion
  dollars.
- They affect up to 25 percent of people over 70 and 50 percent of those over 80.

## Key features

- Pain is usually localized to the level of the fracture, and it may wrap around the rib cage when
  the corresponding dermatome is involved.
- Focal tenderness means pain on deep palpation of the spinous processes.
- Local kyphosis follows the collapse of the anterior vertebral body, which is the hunchbacked
  posture listed earlier among fracture deformities.

> Pain that wraps around the rib cage from a thoracic compression fracture is following a dermatome, not spreading through the chest.

Library: Vertebral compression fractures: diagnosis, prognosis and treatment
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Slide on diagnosis and treatment of vertebral compression fractures](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s21.jpg)

## Identify it

- The diagnosis is radiographic, with loss of anterior, middle or posterior vertebral height by 20
  percent or at least 4 mm. CT and MRI are used much less commonly.

## Key features

- The associated condition the lecture names is compromised pulmonary function, which follows from
  the thoracic kyphosis.
- One year mortality is around 15 percent and two year mortality around 20 percent, which the
  lecture notes is equivalent to hip fracture.
- Treatment runs from observation, bracing and medical management, through vertebroplasty and
  kyphoplasty, to surgical decompression and stabilization, which is rare.

> A vertebral compression fracture carries roughly the same two year mortality as a hip fracture, which is why it is not treated as a minor injury.

Library: Vertebroplasty compared with kyphoplasty
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Slide on vertebroplasty](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s22.jpg)

## Identify it

- Both procedures put bone cement into a fractured vertebral body, and they differ in whether a
  cavity is made first.

## Key features

| | Vertebroplasty | Kyphoplasty |
|---|---|---|
| Cavity | None, cement goes straight into the fractured bone | A balloon is inflated to create one |
| Injection pressure | Higher | Lower |

- The pressure difference follows directly from the cavity. Cement forced into intact trabecular

Library: Components of a diarthrodial joint
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Histology and diagram of a synovial joint](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s25.jpg)

## Identify it

- Synovial joints, also called diarthroses, allow extensive movement, and the lecture asks you to
  identify which tissues are part of the joint and which are part of the joint cavity.
- The compact bone at each articulating end is covered with articular hyaline cartilage, which has
  no perichondrium.

## Key features

- The joint capsule is a layer of dense connective tissue on the outside, thickened in places to
  form ligaments.
- The capsule is lined internally by the synovial membrane.
- The joint cavity is a connective tissue space enclosed by the capsule and the articular surfaces,
  filled with synovial fluid.
- Articular cartilage is avascular, so damage to it does not heal well.

> Articular cartilage carries no perichondrium and no blood supply, which is why it cannot repair itself the way most connective tissues can.

Library: Inside the joint cavity: synovial fluid and its lubricants
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Micrograph of synovium and articular surface](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s25.jpg)

## Identify it

- The synovial fluid in the joint cavity contains hyaluronic acid and the glycoprotein lubricin,
  and both of them reduce friction.

## Key features

- Low friction is produced by two things together, the smooth surface of the hyaline cartilage and
  the lubricating proteoglycans and glycoproteins in the fluid.
- The lecture gives the friction coefficients that follow: cartilage on cartilage is about 0.002,
  far lower than anything manufactured.

> Hyaluronic acid and lubricin are the named lubricants in synovial fluid, and cartilage on cartilage reaches a coefficient of friction of about 0.002.

Library: Osteoarthritis: scope, tissues involved and risk factors
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Slide on osteoarthritis](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s26.jpg)

## Identify it

- Osteoarthritis, also called degenerative joint disease, affects about 31 million adults in the
  United States and is the most common form of arthritis and the most common cause of disability.
- It damages cartilage, synovium, capsule and subchondral bone, so it is a disease of the whole
  joint rather than of cartilage alone.

## Key features

- It occurs most frequently in the hands, hips and knees, but it can occur in any joint.
- The risk factors listed are joint injury or overuse, advancing age, female sex, obesity and
  genetics.
- It is the second most common reason for patients to visit a physician, lifetime risk is 46 percent
  for the knee and 25 percent for the hip, and the risk of developing knee osteoarthritis has
  doubled since the Second World War.
- Around 70 percent of adults with knee osteoarthritis are women, and women tend to have more severe
  cartilage destruction and more pain while being less likely to receive total joint arthroplasty.

> Despite the name, osteoarthritis is not primarily inflammatory, and the damage extends to the synovium, capsule and subchondral bone as well as the cartilage.

Library: What happens to the cartilage matrix in osteoarthritis
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Bone Growth, Formation, and Fractures

![Normal and osteoarthritic articular cartilage histology](library/images/msk-week-15/bonegrow-s26.jpg)

## Identify it

- The cartilage changes are a decrease in proteoglycans, a decrease in water content and a decrease
  in type II collagen fibrils.
- With the matrix depleted the surface cannot be maintained and develops numerous tears.

## Key features

- Pathological change also occurs in the joint capsule, the synovial membrane and the subchondral
  bone, and osteophytes (bone spurs) and subchondral cysts arise.
- Progression is certain and largely irreversible, which is why conservative treatment rarely
  succeeds in the long term.
- On histology the normal articular surface is smooth and the osteoarthritic surface is fibrillated,
  with clefts running into the matrix.

> Proteoglycan loss pulls water out of the matrix, the cartilage loses its resilience, and the surface tears.

Library: Total joint arthroplasty and the friction problem

## Identify it

- Most conservative treatments for osteoarthritis lack long term success, so severe disease is
  corrected with total joint arthroplasty.
- A replacement has a metal stem and articulating surfaces of metal, ceramic or plastic.

## Key features

Library: Radius and ulna: orientation, features, and the interosseous membrane
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Anterior and posterior views of the paired forearm bones](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s03.jpg)

## Identify it

- The radius is the lateral bone and the shorter of the two. The ulna is the medial bone and the longer of the two.
- The ulna does not reach the wrist. That one fact decides most of what follows at the distal end of the forearm.
- Working proximal to distal on the ulna: olecranon, trochlear notch, coronoid process, ulnar tuberosity, radial notch, then the head and the styloid process distally.
- Working proximal to distal on the radius: head, neck, radial tuberosity, then the broad distal end with its own styloid process.

## Key features

| Bone | Side | Proximal end | Distal end |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radius | Lateral, shorter | Head, neck, radial tuberosity | Broad, carries the styloid process, reaches the wrist |
| Ulna | Medial, longer | Olecranon, trochlear notch, coronoid process, radial notch | Head and styloid process, does not reach the wrist |

- The interosseous membrane is a tough fibrous sheet running between the shafts of the radius and the ulna.
- It makes a radio-ulnar syndesmosis, a fibrous joint, and it is perforated by an opening that lets neurovasculature pass from the anterior to the posterior side.
- Note which bone carries a head at which end. The radial head is proximal, at the elbow, while the ulnar head is distal, at the wrist, which is the reverse of what most students expect.

> The ulna is longer but stops short of the carpus, so the radius alone carries the hand, and a fall transmits force through the radius first.

## Identify it

Library: Proximal radio-ulnar joint and the annular ligament
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

## Identify it

- The head of the radius articulates with the radial notch of the ulna.

Library: Radial head subluxation (nursemaid's elbow)

## Identify it

- Dislocation of the radial head, also called nursemaid's elbow.
- It happens when a child is suddenly lifted by the upper limb while the arm is pronated.
- The traction pulls the radial head out from the ring formed by the annular ligament and the radial notch.

Library: Distal radio-ulnar joint and the triangular fibrocartilage complex

Library: Fascia and compartments of the forearm
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Cross sections of the arm and the forearm showing the fascial envelope and the divisions within it](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s12.jpg)

## Identify it

- The arm and the forearm each have an anterior and a posterior compartment, but what separates them is different in each.
- In the arm the brachial fascia wraps the whole segment, and the medial and lateral intermuscular fascia run inward from it to the humerus to split anterior from posterior.
- In the forearm the antebrachial fascia wraps the whole segment, and the interosseous membrane running between the radius and the ulna is what splits anterior from posterior.

## Key features

| Segment | Outer envelope | What divides the compartments |
|---|---|---|
| Arm (brachium) | Brachial fascia | Medial and lateral intermuscular fascia |
| Forearm (antebrachium) | Antebrachial fascia | Interosseous membrane |

- The interosseous membrane is doing two jobs at once, holding the two bones together and acting as the partition between the flexor and extensor groups.

> In the arm the partition is fascia running in from the sleeve, and in the forearm the partition is a membrane running between two bones.

|---|---|---|

Library: Pronator teres

## Identify it

- Distal attachment: lateral surface of the radius.
- Innervation: median nerve (C6, C7).
- Actions: pronates and flexes the forearm.

## Key features

- It is the only muscle in the superficial group that attaches to the radius rather than running on to the hand, and that is what lets it rotate the radius across the ulna.
- Because it crosses the elbow as well, it adds flexion at the elbow to its pronation.
- Its two proximal heads matter clinically, because the median nerve passes between them on its way into the forearm.

> Pronator teres pulls the radius across the ulna, so it pronates, and because it also crosses the elbow it flexes.

Library: Flexor carpi radialis
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Flexor carpi radialis shown on the skeleton of the upper limb](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s19.jpg)

## Identify it

- Proximal attachment: medial epicondyle of the humerus.
- Distal attachment: base of the second metacarpal.
- Innervation: median nerve (C6, C7).
- Actions: flexes and abducts the hand.

## Key features

- The name reports both halves of the action. Flexor gives the flexion, and carpi radialis places the pull on the radial side of the carpus, which abducts the hand.
- It runs past the wrist to reach a metacarpal, so it acts on the hand as a whole rather than on any individual digit.
- Pair it against flexor carpi ulnaris. Both flex, and their side to side components oppose each other, so acting together they flex the wrist with no deviation.

> A muscle that crosses the wrist on the radial side both flexes the hand and pulls it into abduction.

Library: Palmaris longus
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Palmaris longus and its continuation into the palm](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s21.jpg)

## Identify it

- Proximal attachment: medial epicondyle of the humerus.
- Distal attachment: flexor retinaculum and palmar aponeurosis.
- Innervation: median nerve (C7, C8).
- Actions: flexes the hand and tenses the palmar aponeurosis.

## Key features

- It is the only one of the four superficial muscles that does not reach a bone distally. It ends in the fascia of the palm, which is why its second action is tensing rather than moving.
- In dissection it appears as a thin tendon crossing the wrist superficial to the flexor retinaculum before fanning out into the apex of the palmar aponeurosis.
- Palmaris longus may or may not be present in a given donor, and it is becoming more rare over time.

> Palmaris longus is the superficial muscle that may simply be absent, and the one that inserts into fascia instead of bone.

Library: Flexor carpi ulnaris
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Flexor carpi ulnaris shown on the skeleton of the upper limb](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s23.jpg)

## Identify it

- Proximal attachment: medial epicondyle of the humerus and the olecranon of the ulna.
- Distal attachment: pisiform, hook of the hamate, and the fifth metacarpal.
- Innervation: ulnar nerve (C7, C8).
- Actions: flexes and adducts the hand.

## Key features

- This is the one superficial muscle the median nerve does not supply, and it is the most medial of the four.
- It has three distal attachments rather than one, running from the pisiform through the hook of the hamate to the base of the fifth metacarpal.
- The ulnar nerve passes under its tendon as it travels down the forearm, which ties the nerve and the muscle together anatomically as well as functionally.

> Flexor carpi ulnaris is the ulnar exception in the superficial group, and the ulnar nerve runs beneath its tendon.

Library: Medial epicondylitis (golfer's elbow)

## Identify it

- An overuse injury of the common flexor tendon at the medial epicondyle of the humerus.
- It develops during repeated wrist flexion.
- Pain is at the medial epicondyle, which is the shared origin rather than any one muscle belly.

## Key features

- The lesion sits at the single tendon that all four superficial muscles arise from, so the provoking movement is wrist flexion in general and not the action of one named muscle.

Library: Flexor digitorum superficialis

## Identify it

- Proximal attachment: medial epicondyle of the humerus, coronoid process of the ulna, and the anterior border of the radius.
- Distal attachment: middle phalanges of all but the first digit, so digits two through five.
- Innervation: median nerve (C7, C8, T1).

## Key features

- It is the only muscle in the intermediate layer, sitting alone between the four superficial and the three deep muscles.

Library: Deep flexor group and the anterior interosseous nerve
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

## Identify it

- Three muscles lie in the deep layer: flexor digitorum profundus, flexor pollicis longus, pronator quadratus.
- Their general innervation is the anterior interosseous nerve, which is a branch of the median nerve.
- The anterior interosseous nerve supplies pronator quadratus, flexor pollicis longus, and the lateral part of flexor digitorum profundus.

## Key features

- The nerve runs down on the interosseous membrane, which is where its name comes from and which is why it reaches the deep layer and nothing more superficial.
- One of the three is split. The medial part of flexor digitorum profundus goes to the ulnar nerve instead, so the anterior interosseous claims only the lateral part.
- Unlike the median nerve as a whole, the anterior interosseous nerve carries no cutaneous territory in this lecture, so everything it does here is motor.

> The anterior interosseous nerve is a purely deep layer nerve: pronator quadratus, flexor pollicis longus, and the lateral part of flexor digitorum profundus.

Library: Flexor digitorum profundus and its divided innervation
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

## Identify it

- Proximal attachment: anterior surface of the ulna and the interosseous membrane.
- Distal attachment: distal phalanges of all but the first digit. The medial part goes to digits four and five, the lateral part to digits two and three.
- Innervation is split: the medial part by the ulnar nerve (C8, T1), the lateral part by the median nerve (C7, C8).
- Action: flexes the distal phalanges of all but the first digit.

## Key features

| Part | Digits | Nerve |
|---|---|---|
| Medial | 4 and 5 | Ulnar |
| Lateral | 2 and 3 | Median |

- This is the only muscle in the compartment that two different nerves supply, and the split follows the medial to lateral line that the rest of the compartment follows.
- It reaches the distal phalanx, one joint further than flexor digitorum superficialis, so it is the muscle behind flexion of the distal interphalangeal joints.
- It takes no attachment from the humerus at all, arising instead from the ulna and the interosseous membrane.

> One muscle, two nerves: the ulnar nerve drives the ring and little fingers and the median nerve drives the index and middle.

Library: Flexor pollicis longus
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Flexor pollicis longus running to the thumb](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s29.jpg)

## Identify it

- Proximal attachment: anterior surface of the radius and the interosseous membrane.
- Distal attachment: distal phalanx of the thumb, the first digit.
- Innervation: anterior interosseous nerve (C7, C8).
- Action: flexes the thumb.

## Key features

- Pollicis names the thumb, and this is the only muscle in the anterior forearm that acts on it.
- It arises from the radius while flexor digitorum profundus arises from the ulna, which matches where each one is heading. The radial side muscle goes to the thumb.
- Flexion of the thumb at its distal joint depends on this muscle alone, so loss of the anterior interosseous nerve shows up there immediately.

> Flexor pollicis longus is the thumb's long flexor, it comes off the radius, and its nerve is the anterior interosseous.

Library: Pronator quadratus
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Pronator quadratus bridging the distal radius and ulna](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s30.jpg)

## Identify it

- Proximal attachment: distal anterior surface of the ulna.
- Distal attachment: distal anterior surface of the radius.
- Innervation: anterior interosseous nerve (C7, C8).
- Action: pronates the forearm.

## Key features

- It is a short square muscle running straight across from ulna to radius at the distal end, which is the shortest route for producing pronation.
- It is the second of the two pronators. Pronator teres works at the proximal end and also flexes the elbow, while pronator quadratus works at the distal end and does nothing else.
- Because it crosses no other joint, pronation is its only action, which is what sets it apart from pronator teres.

|---|---|---|---|
| Pronator teres | Proximal forearm | Median | Flexes the forearm |
| Pronator quadratus | Distal forearm | Anterior interosseous | None |

> Two pronators, one at each end of the forearm, and only the distal one is pure.

Library: Three layers reviewed: counts, shared origins, and reach
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Review figure tracing each muscle of the three layers from origin to insertion](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s31.jpg)

## Identify it

- The counts are four, one, and three, moving from superficial to deep.
- The shared origin belongs to the superficial group alone. All four come off the common flexor attachment on the medial epicondyle.
- The deep group shares a different pattern: all three arise from the forearm bones and the interosseous membrane, with no humeral attachment at all.

## Key features

| Layer | Muscles | Where they arise |
|---|---|---|
| Superficial | Pronator teres, flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus, flexor carpi ulnaris | Common flexor attachment on the medial epicondyle |
| Intermediate | Flexor digitorum superficialis | Medial epicondyle, coronoid process, anterior border of the radius |
| Deep | Flexor digitorum profundus, flexor pollicis longus, pronator quadratus | Ulna, radius, and the interosseous membrane |

- The intermediate muscle straddles the pattern, taking a humeral attachment like the superficial group and forearm attachments like the deep group.
- A muscle that arises below the humerus cannot act on the elbow at all, which is why nothing in the deep group has an elbow action. Of the muscles that do take a humeral attachment, pronator teres is the only one this lecture credits with an elbow action, flexion of the forearm.

> Count down four, one, three, and remember that the deep group never touches the humerus, so none of it can act on the elbow.

Library: Median nerve in the forearm
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Course of the median nerve through the anterior forearm](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s33.jpg)

## Identify it

- Brachial plexus origin: the medial and lateral cords, carrying C5 through C8 and T1.
- It enters the forearm alongside the brachial artery and then continues down with the ulnar artery.
- Motor supply: every muscle of the anterior forearm except flexor carpi ulnaris and the medial half of flexor digitorum profundus.

## Key features

- It draws from both the medial and the lateral cord, which is why its root value spans C5 through T1.
- Six of the eight anterior compartment muscles answer to it directly or through its anterior interosseous branch, so a proximal median lesion takes out most of the flexor compartment at once.
- The two muscles it does not supply are both on the medial side of the forearm, matching the side the ulnar nerve occupies.

> The median nerve runs the anterior forearm, and the two muscles it skips sit on the ulnar side and belong to the ulnar nerve.

Library: Common sites of median nerve injury
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Three circled points along the course of the median nerve where it is commonly injured](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s35.jpg)

## Identify it

- Three sites, running proximal to distal.
- Running past the elbow.
- Passing between the two heads of pronator teres, which produces pronator syndrome.
- Running under the flexor retinaculum, which produces carpal tunnel syndrome.

## Key features

| Site | Named syndrome |
|---|---|
| Past the elbow | Not separately named in this lecture |
| Between the two heads of pronator teres | Pronator syndrome |
| Under the flexor retinaculum | Carpal tunnel syndrome |

- All three sites are places where the nerve is held against something firm, either a bone, a muscular arch, or a fibrous roof, which is the pattern behind compression generally.
- The level of the lesion decides how much is lost. A lesion at the elbow is upstream of every anterior forearm branch, while one at the flexor retinaculum is downstream of all of them.
- Pronator syndrome is a compression at the elbow region caused by the muscle the nerve passes through, so pronation activity can provoke it.

> The further proximal the median nerve lesion, the more of the anterior forearm goes with it, which is why the site is the first thing to establish.

Library: Anterior interosseous syndrome
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Side by side comparison of pinch on an affected and an unaffected hand](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s36.jpg)

## Identify it

- Injury to the anterior interosseous nerve.
- The result is reduced flexion of flexor digitorum profundus and flexor pollicis longus.
- The sign is visible when the patient tries to pinch. The injured side cannot bend the tip of the thumb or the tip of the index finger, so the pinch comes out flat and triangular instead of round.

## Key features

- The two muscles that fail are the ones that reach the distal phalanges of the thumb and of the index finger, which are exactly the two surfaces used in a tip pinch.
- Pronator quadratus is on the same nerve and weakens too, but it is harder to see because pronator teres still pronates the forearm.
- There is no sensory loss to look for here, because this branch is motor to the deep layer.

> A pinch that comes out as a flat triangle rather than a circle points to the anterior interosseous nerve, because flexor pollicis longus and the lateral flexor digitorum profundus cannot bend the tips.

Library: Ulnar nerve in the forearm
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Course of the ulnar nerve down the medial side of the forearm](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s37.jpg)

## Identify it

- Brachial plexus origin: the medial cord, carrying C7, C8 and T1.
- In the forearm it passes under the tendon of flexor carpi ulnaris.
- Motor supply in the forearm: flexor carpi ulnaris, and the ulnar part of flexor digitorum profundus.

## Key features

- Two muscles only, and both of them are the medial ones. Everything else in the compartment belongs to the median nerve.
- The nerve runs on the medial side the whole way down, which is why both the muscles it claims and the skin territory it serves sit on that side.
- Because it passes beneath the tendon of flexor carpi ulnaris, the muscle it supplies is also the structure it travels under, which makes that tendon a landmark for finding it.

> In the forearm the ulnar nerve owns exactly two muscles, flexor carpi ulnaris and the medial half of flexor digitorum profundus, which is why isolated weakness of ring and little finger tip flexion points to it.

Library: Peripheral cutaneous nerves of the forearm
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Peripheral cutaneous nerve territories across the upper limb](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s38.jpg)

## Identify it

- A peripheral cutaneous nerve distribution is innervation from multiple spinal nerves carried through one peripheral nerve.
- The lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm covers the lateral strip. It comes from the musculocutaneous nerve.
- The medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm covers the medial strip. It comes directly off the medial cord.

## Key features

| Territory | Nerve | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Lateral forearm skin | Lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm | Musculocutaneous nerve |
| Medial forearm skin | Medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm | Medial cord of the brachial plexus |

- Neither of these is the median or the ulnar nerve, so sensory loss over the forearm itself does not come from the two nerves that run the muscles there.
- The medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm is unusual in branching straight from a cord rather than from a terminal branch, which is worth noting when tracing a lesion back to the plexus.

> The nerves that supply the skin of the forearm are not the nerves that supply its muscles, so forearm numbness and forearm weakness point to different places.

Library: Upper limb dermatomes in the forearm and hand
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Anterior view of the upper limb with the segmental skin fields labeled](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s39.jpg)

## Identify it

- A dermatome is cutaneous innervation by a single spinal nerve.
- On the anterior view the bands run in sequence: C6 down the lateral forearm to the thumb, C7 across the middle of the hand, C8 along the medial forearm to the ring and little fingers, and T1 over the medial side of the arm above it.
- Compare this against the peripheral cutaneous map, which is built the opposite way, from one nerve carrying several roots.

## Key features

| Field | Where it lands on the limb |
|---|---|
| C6 | Lateral forearm and thumb |
| C7 | Middle of the hand |
| C8 | Medial forearm, ring and little fingers |
| T1 | Medial arm |

- The two maps answer different questions. A dermatome chart localizes a root or a spinal level, and a peripheral cutaneous chart localizes a named nerve.
- The sequence runs round the limb in order, so a band of numbness that follows a stripe down the forearm fits a root, while one that stops at a nerve boundary does not.

> One spinal nerve equals one dermatome, one peripheral nerve equals several spinal nerves blended together, and the two maps will not overlay.

Library: Arteries of the anterior forearm: ulnar and radial
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![The brachial artery dividing into the ulnar and radial arteries below the elbow](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s47.jpg)

## Identify it

- In the forearm the brachial artery gives rise to two vessels, the ulnar artery and the radial artery.
- The ulnar artery runs down the medial side, passing across the flexor digitorum profundus, and the median nerve continues with it after entering the forearm alongside the brachial artery.
- The radial artery runs down the lateral side.

## Key features

- The division happens below the elbow, so the medial epicondyle is the landmark used to orient the split on the figure.
- The ulnar and radial arteries are named for the bones they parallel, the same convention the muscles follow, so the ulnar artery is the medial one.
- The median nerve enters the forearm alongside the brachial artery and then continues with the ulnar artery, which puts a major nerve and a major vessel in the same medial corridor.

> One artery enters the forearm and two leave the elbow region, the ulnar medially and the radial laterally, with the median nerve following the ulnar.

Library: Interosseous and recurrent branches in the forearm
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Branching pattern of the ulnar and radial arteries in the forearm](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s48.jpg)

## Identify it

- The ulnar artery gives the ulnar recurrent arteries and the common interosseous artery.
- The common interosseous artery then divides into the anterior interosseous artery and the posterior interosseous artery.
- The radial artery gives the radial recurrent arteries.

## Key features

| Parent vessel | Branches |
|---|---|
| Ulnar artery | Ulnar recurrent arteries, common interosseous artery |
| Common interosseous artery | Anterior interosseous artery, posterior interosseous artery |
| Radial artery | Radial recurrent arteries |

- The two interosseous arteries travel on either face of the interosseous membrane, which is how each compartment gets its own supply, and it matches the way the anterior interosseous nerve runs with its artery on the front of the membrane.

- Only the ulnar artery produces a common trunk. The radial artery contributes a recurrent branch and otherwise continues on.

> Recurrent branches run the wrong way on purpose, heading proximally to join descending vessels so that blood can still get past a joint that bends.

Library: Superficial veins and lymphatic drainage of the forearm
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Superficial venous pattern of the forearm in anterior and posterior view](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s42.jpg)

## Identify it

- Two named superficial veins run up the forearm, the basilic vein on the medial side and the cephalic vein on the lateral side.
- They are fed distally by the superficial venous palmar arch and the dorsal venous network of the hand.
- Lymph follows a parallel route: the lymphatic plexus of the hand drains into superficial lymphatic vessels, which carry it to the axillary and apical lymph nodes.

## Key features

| System | Starts at | Runs through | Ends at |
|---|---|---|---|
| Superficial venous | Palmar and dorsal networks of the hand | Basilic vein medially, cephalic vein laterally | Deeper veins of the arm |

> Veins and lymphatics of the forearm both start in the hand and both run superficially, so anything in the hand travels up the same two routes.

Library: Condyle of the humerus and what each landmark articulates with
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![Anterior view of the humeral condyle with its four landmarks numbered](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s44.jpg)

## Identify it

- Four landmarks on the anterior aspect of the condyle, and one more behind it.
- The capitulum is lateral and takes the head of the radius.
- The trochlea is medial and takes the trochlear notch of the ulna.
- Above them sit two hollows: the radial fossa laterally, which receives the head of the radius, and the coronoid fossa medially, which receives the coronoid process of the ulna.

## Key features

| Landmark on the humerus | Side | Articulates with |
|---|---|---|
| Capitulum | Lateral | Head of the radius |
| Trochlea | Medial | Trochlear notch of the ulna |

- The fossae are receiving spaces rather than articular surfaces. The two in front take the forearm bones in full flexion, and the one behind takes the olecranon in full extension.

Library: The anterior forearm and elbow on radiographs
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Anterior Forearm and Elbow

![AP and lateral radiographs of the forearm with structures labeled](library/images/msk-week-15/forearm-s46.jpg)

## Identify it

- Two standard views of the same forearm, AP and lateral, each showing the elbow at one end and the wrist at the other.
- Proximally: the humerus with its trochlea and capitulum, the olecranon and trochlear notch of the ulna, and the head of the radius with the radial tuberosity just below it.
- Distally: the styloid process of the radius and the styloid process of the ulna, with the carpal bones beyond them.

## Key features

- The AP is the view that lays the two bones out side by side. It separates the olecranon from the head of the radius at the elbow and separates the two shafts along their length, which is why both styloid processes, the trochlea, the capitulum and the radial tuberosity are all labeled on that view.

- The radial tuberosity is the landmark that confirms which bone is the radius on a film where both shafts look similar.
- The same landmarks learned on the dry bone appear here, which is the point of the exercise. The radial head sits against the capitulum and the trochlear notch wraps the trochlea.

> Identify the radial tuberosity first on a forearm film, because it fixes which shaft is the radius and the rest of the landmarks follow from there.

Library: Radiocarpal Joint and the Articular Disc
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Coronal dorsal view of the right wrist joint](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s03.jpg)

## Identify it

- A condyloid type of synovial joint between the distal end of the radius, the articular disc of the distal radio-ulnar joint, and the scaphoid, lunate and triquetrum.
- The distal end of the radius articulates with the scaphoid and lunate, while the articular disc articulates with the triquetrum.

## Key features

- The ulna does not directly articulate with any carpal bone. The articular disc sits between the head of the ulna and the triquetrum and keeps them apart.
- That disc is the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) of the distal radio-ulnar joint. It binds the distal ends of the radius and ulna together and stabilises the wrist region by transmitting and distributing load from the hand to the ulna.
- The distal radio-ulnar joint itself is a pivot synovial joint between the head of the ulna and the ulnar notch of the radius, and it is where pronation and supination happen, with the radius moving around a relatively fixed distal ulna.
- The TFCC can be damaged by ulnar-sided wrist trauma or by chronic repetitive movement, which the lecture lists as golf, gymnastics and racquet sports.

> Load from the hand reaches the ulna only through the articular disc, because the ulna has no direct contact with the carpus.

## Identify it

- Four named ligaments strengthen the wrist joint: the palmar radiocarpal ligament, the dorsal radiocarpal ligament, the radial collateral ligament of the wrist, and the ulnar collateral ligament of the wrist.

## Key features

| Movement | Note |

Library: Wrist Sprains and Scapholunate Ligament Injury
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Slide on wrist sprains with radiographs of the carpus](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s07.jpg)

## Identify it

- A wrist sprain is a stretch or tear of any of the ligaments supporting the wrist, most often from a fall on an outstretched hand (FOOSH).
- The scapholunate ligament, between the scaphoid and lunate, is the one most commonly injured or torn.

## Key features

- The two complications the lecture names for a wrist sprain are avascular necrosis of the lunate and compression of the median nerve in the carpal tunnel.
- Both of those follow from the same event. Damage to the scapholunate ligament can force the lunate to dislocate anteriorly, and the anteriorly displaced lunate both loses its blood supply and crowds the carpal tunnel.
- A sprain is a ligamentous injury, so the initial radiograph can be unremarkable for bone while the carpal alignment is already abnormal.

> A patient who develops carpal tunnel symptoms days after a FOOSH has told you something about carpal alignment, not just about soft tissue swelling.

Library: Anterior Dislocation of the Lunate
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

## Identify it

- The lunate is the carpal bone most commonly dislocated, and it dislocates anteriorly, into the floor of the carpal tunnel.

## Key features

- The mechanism is the same forced loading that sprains the wrist, with the scapholunate ligament giving way first.
- Two consequences follow directly from where the bone goes. Anteriorly it presses on the median nerve inside the carpal tunnel, producing acute carpal tunnel syndrome. Having lost its ligamentous attachments it can also undergo avascular necrosis.
- On a PA radiograph the dislocated lunate loses its normal quadrilateral outline and overlaps the bones around it, which is why the injured and uninjured sides are compared.

> Of the eight carpal bones, the scaphoid is the one most often fractured and the lunate is the one most often dislocated.

Library: Carpal Bones, Their Rows and Their Articulations
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Palmar view of the carpal bones in two rows](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s08.jpg)

## Identify it

- Eight carpal bones in two rows. Proximal row from lateral to medial: scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum, pisiform. Distal row from lateral to medial: trapezium, trapezoid, capitate, hamate.
- The lecture's mnemonic runs Some Lovers Try Positions That They Can't Handle.

## Key features

| Bone | Shape and articulations the lecture gives |
|---|---|
| Scaphoid | Boat shaped, articulates with the radius |
| Lunate | Crescent shaped, between scaphoid and triquetrum, articulates with the radius |
| Triquetrum | Three sided, articulates with the disc of the distal radio-ulnar joint |
| Pisiform | Pea shaped, sits on the palmar surface of the triquetrum |
| Trapezium | Four sided, articulates with metacarpals 1 and 2, scaphoid and trapezoid |
| Trapezoid | Wedge shaped, articulates with metacarpal 2, trapezium, capitate and scaphoid |
| Capitate | The largest carpal bone, articulates with metacarpal 3, trapezoid, scaphoid, lunate and hamate |
| Hamate | Wedge shaped with a hook, articulates with metacarpals 4 and 5, capitate and triquetrum |

- Only the scaphoid, lunate and triquetrum take part in the wrist joint proper. The pisiform sits on the palmar surface of the triquetrum rather than in the articular line.
- The flexor retinaculum and the hypothenar muscles attach to the hook of the hamate, and the superficial and deep branches of the ulnar nerve pass medial to it.

> The pisiform is a sesamoid sitting on the triquetrum, which is why it is in the proximal row by position but not part of the wrist joint.

Library: Fracture of the Scaphoid
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Slide on scaphoid fracture with a radiograph](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s11.jpg)

## Identify it

- The most commonly fractured carpal bone, usually from a fall on an outstretched hand.
- Pain is felt on the lateral aspect of the wrist, in the anatomical snuffbox.

## Key features

- The fracture may not be visible on a radiograph until 10 to 14 days later, once bone resorption at the fracture line has occurred. A normal looking film on the day of injury therefore does not exclude it.
- Disunion of the two fragments can cause avascular necrosis of the proximal fragment, meaning death of that part of the bone from loss of its blood supply.
- The radial artery passes dorsally around the scaphoid and trapezium along the floor of the snuffbox, which is why that hollow is both the tender spot and a vascular landmark.

> Snuffbox tenderness after a FOOSH is treated as a scaphoid fracture until proven otherwise, because the film that would prove it may be two weeks away.

Library: Fracture of the Hook of the Hamate
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

## Identify it

- A fracture through the hook of the hamate (the hamulus), which projects into the medial side of the palm.

## Key features

- The hypothenar muscles attach to the hook, so when it fractures their traction can pull the fragments apart.
- The ulnar nerve and ulnar artery lie in close proximity to the hook and can be injured by the fracture.
- The lecture gives the functional consequence of that nerve injury as decreased grip strength of the hand, which follows from losing the intrinsic muscles the ulnar nerve supplies.
- The ulnar canal is formed by the pisiform and the hamate with the ligament overlying them, so the hook is one of the two bony walls of the space the nerve and artery run through, and a fracture there breaks that wall.

> A fractured hamate hook threatens the ulnar nerve not because the nerve runs through the bone, but because it runs just medial to it.

Library: Bones and Joints of the Hand
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Slide showing the joints of the hand](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s13.jpg)

## Identify it

- Five metacarpals, each with a base, shaft and head, and fourteen phalanges. The thumb has two phalanges and the other digits have three, graded proximal (largest), middle and distal (smallest).

## Key features

| Joint | Type | Movements |
|---|---|---|
| Carpometacarpal, digits 2 to 5 | Plane synovial | Limited gliding |
| Carpometacarpal of the thumb | Saddle synovial | Flexion and extension, abduction and adduction, circumduction |
| Metacarpophalangeal (MCP) | Condyloid synovial | Flexion and extension, abduction and adduction, circumduction in digits 2 to 5 |
| Proximal interphalangeal (PIP) | Hinge | Flexion and extension |
| Distal interphalangeal (DIP) | Hinge | Flexion and extension |

- The phalanges are the insertion site for several anterior forearm muscles. Flexor pollicis longus inserts on the base of the distal phalanx of the thumb.
- The flexor digitorum superficialis tendons split to insert on the sides of the middle phalanx, which allows the flexor digitorum profundus tendons to pass through them and attach to the base of the distal phalanx.
- Most lymph from the upper limb travels in superficial lymphatic vessels that follow the superficial veins and drain to the axillary nodes.

> The saddle shape of the thumb carpometacarpal joint is what gives the thumb a range the other four digits do not have.

Library: Movements of the Thumb
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Photographs of the five thumb movements](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s14.jpg)

## Identify it

- Five movements are named for the thumb: flexion, extension, abduction, adduction and opposition.

## Key features

- Opposition is the one movement of the five that is not simply the reverse of another. It is listed with rotation of the thumb, and it is what brings the pad of the thumb across toward the other digits.
- Opposition is the movement that is lost outright when the median nerve is injured, because opponens pollicis is supplied by the recurrent branch.
- Adduction of the thumb is the odd one out among thumb movements, since adductor pollicis is supplied by the deep branch of the ulnar nerve rather than the median.

> Learn which nerve supplies each thumb movement, because the pattern of movements lost is how a median lesion is told apart from an ulnar one.

Library: Surface Anatomy of the Palm
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Palm showing the thenar and hypothenar eminences](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s15.jpg)

## Identify it

- The thenar eminence is the muscular region of the palm at the base of the thumb.
- The hypothenar eminence is the muscular region at the base of the little finger.

## Key features

- Each eminence is made by three short muscles, and the two compartments are mirror images of each other in their organisation: an abductor, a short flexor and an opponens.
- Because the muscles are superficial, loss of bulk in either eminence is visible on inspection, which is why the lecture opens its nerve lesion section with a photograph of two palms side by side.
- The thenar eminence is flattened by a median nerve lesion and the hypothenar eminence by an ulnar nerve lesion.
- The recurrent branch of the median nerve lies subcutaneously over the thenar eminence, so a superficial cut there can paralyse the thenar muscles.

> Comparing one palm with the other is the examination, because wasting is obvious side by side and easy to miss alone.

Library: Palmar Aponeurosis
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Palmar aponeurosis and the palmaris longus tendon](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s16.jpg)

## Identify it

- A thick triangular sheet of longitudinally oriented fibres running from the distal edge of the flexor retinaculum to the medial four digits, where it becomes continuous with the fibrous tendon sheaths of the digits.

## Key features

- The palmaris longus tendon inserts into the proximal portion of the aponeurosis, which is why that tendon is the surface landmark for the structures underneath.
- Extensions, or septa, from the aponeurosis pass deeply and divide the hand into compartments.
- The superficial palmar arch lies immediately deep to the aponeurosis, so the aponeurosis is what protects the arch from a superficial palmar laceration.
- The aponeurosis covers the medial four digits only. The thumb has its own thenar fascia instead.

> The palmar aponeurosis is both a protective layer over the arch and the structure that pathologically shortens in Dupuytren contracture.

Library: Dupuytren Contracture
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Clinical photographs of palmar nodules and fixed finger flexion](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s17.jpg)

## Identify it

- Progressive, unexplained shortening and thickening of the collagen fibres of the palmar aponeurosis.
- Nodules (small lumps) and cords appear in the palm, and the fingers are drawn into permanent partial flexion.

## Key features

- The fourth and fifth digits are usually the ones affected, and they are drawn into flexion at the MCP and PIP joints.
- It is frequently bilateral and is described in men over the age of fifty. The cause is unknown, though genetics is thought to play a role.
- The deformity is fixed rather than a weakness, because the problem is in the fascia and not in the muscles or nerves. Sensation is normal.
- Treatment depends on severity and may involve surgically removing the thickened fibres and nodules, or breaking them apart with collagenase injection or needling, so the patient can open the palm.

> A finger that cannot be passively straightened points to the fascia, while a finger that cannot be actively straightened points to nerve or tendon.

Library: Flexor Retinaculum and the Contents of the Carpal Tunnel
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Carpal tunnel in cross section and on MRI](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s19.jpg)

## Identify it

- The flexor retinaculum, also called the transverse carpal ligament, is a fibrous band extending between the outer carpal bones. It forms the roof of the carpal tunnel, and the arch of the carpal bones forms the floor and walls.

## Key features

- Four things pass deep to the retinaculum, inside the tunnel: the four tendons of flexor digitorum superficialis, the four tendons of flexor digitorum profundus, the tendon of flexor pollicis longus, and the median nerve. That is nine tendons and one nerve.
- Three things pass superficial to the retinaculum and so are outside the tunnel: the ulnar nerve and ulnar artery, the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve, and the palmaris longus tendon when it is present.
- The median nerve is the most superficial of the contents, lying just deep to the retinaculum, which is why it is the structure compressed when the space is reduced. The flexor pollicis longus tendon lies radial to it and deeper.
- On an axial MRI the retinaculum is the dark band arching over the dark cluster of flexor tendons, with the median nerve sitting immediately deep to it.

> The ulnar nerve passes over the retinaculum and not through the tunnel, which is the single fact that separates carpal tunnel syndrome from ulnar canal syndrome.

Library: Carpal Tunnel Syndrome
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

## Identify it

- Compression of the median nerve within the carpal tunnel, producing both sensory and motor deficits.
- It results from any lesion that reduces the size of the tunnel or increases the size of its contents, such as inflammation of the tendons or their synovial sheaths.

## Key features

- Sensory loss involves the palmar aspect of the lateral three and a half digits and the distal dorsal aspects of those digits including the nail beds.
- The skin of the central palm is spared, because the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve arises proximal to the carpal tunnel and passes superficial to the retinaculum.
- The motor loss is confined to the thenar muscles supplied by the recurrent branch, so the patient loses opposition of the thumb and weakens thumb abduction and flexion.
- Anterior dislocation of the lunate is one of the causes the lecture names, since the displaced bone reduces the space from the floor.

> Sensation preserved over the central palm with numb lateral fingers localises the lesion to the tunnel rather than to the nerve higher up.

Library: Carpal Tunnel Release
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Carpal tunnel surgery](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s21.jpg)

## Identify it

- A partial or complete surgical division of the flexor retinaculum, performed to relieve the symptoms of carpal tunnel syndrome.

## Key features

- The operation works by cutting the roof of the tunnel, which allows the contents to expand and takes pressure off the median nerve. Nothing is removed from inside the tunnel.
- The incision runs in the palm over the retinaculum, which puts the recurrent branch of the median nerve at risk, since it runs superficially across the thenar eminence.
- The ulnar nerve and artery lie superficial to the retinaculum on its medial side and are also close to the operative field.
- Division of the retinaculum does not restore muscle bulk that has already been lost, which is why the thenar wasting of a long standing lesion may persist.

> Releasing the tunnel means dividing its roof, so the structures at risk are the ones lying on top of that roof rather than inside the tunnel.

Library: Ulnar Canal and Ulnar Canal Syndrome
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Ulnar canal formed between the pisiform and hamate](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s22.jpg)

## Identify it

- The ulnar canal, also called Guyon's canal or tunnel, is a fibro-osseous arch stretching between the pisiform and the hook of the hamate with an overlying ligament.
- Two structures pass through it: the ulnar artery and the ulnar nerve.

## Key features

- The nerve enters the hand lateral to the pisiform and divides into superficial and deep branches.
- Ulnar canal syndrome produces hypoesthesia in the medial one and a half digits together with weakness of the intrinsic hand muscles.
- Clawing of the fourth and fifth fingers may occur, but unlike a proximal ulnar injury the ability to flex those digits is unaffected and there is no radial deviation of the hand.
- Handlebar neuropathy is compression of the ulnar nerve here in people who ride with the hands extended on the grips, putting pressure on the hook of the hamate. It is described in long distance cyclists and produces sensory loss on the medial hand with weakness of the intrinsic muscles.

> The canal sits superficial to the flexor retinaculum, so a procedure that opens the carpal tunnel does nothing for the nerve inside this one.

Library: Thenar Muscles
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Thenar muscles and their attachments](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s24.jpg)

## Identify it

- The palm is organised into four muscular compartments: thenar (three muscles), hypothenar (three muscles), central (the lumbricals), and the adductor and interosseous compartment. The thenar and hypothenar groups are mirror images of each other.

## Key features

| Muscle | Proximal attachment | Distal attachment | Innervation | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Abductor pollicis brevis | Flexor retinaculum, scaphoid, trapezium | Lateral side of base of proximal phalanx of thumb | Recurrent branch of median n. | Abducts thumb |
| Flexor pollicis brevis | Flexor retinaculum, scaphoid, trapezium | Lateral side of base of proximal phalanx of thumb | Superficial head: recurrent branch of median n. Deep head: deep branch of ulnar n. | Flexes thumb |
| Opponens pollicis | Flexor retinaculum, scaphoid, trapezium | Lateral side of the first metacarpal | Recurrent branch of median n. | Opposition and rotation of thumb |

- All three share the same proximal attachment, which makes the flexor retinaculum a muscular origin as well as the roof of the tunnel.
- The deep head of flexor pollicis brevis is the exception to the rule that the thenar muscles are median, since it is supplied by the deep branch of the ulnar nerve.
- Opponens pollicis is the only one of the three that inserts on the metacarpal rather than the phalanx, which is what lets it rotate the whole thumb column.

> Three thenar muscles, all from the flexor retinaculum, all median except the deep head of flexor pollicis brevis.

Library: Hypothenar Muscles
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Hypothenar muscles and their attachments](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s27.jpg)

## Identify it

- Three muscles in the hypothenar compartment, mirroring the thenar group: abductor digiti minimi, flexor digiti minimi brevis and opponens digiti minimi.

## Key features

| Muscle | Proximal attachment | Distal attachment | Innervation | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Abductor digiti minimi | Pisiform | Medial side of base of proximal phalanx of digit 5 | Deep branch of ulnar n. | Abducts digit 5 |
| Flexor digiti minimi brevis | Flexor retinaculum and hook of hamate | Medial side of base of proximal phalanx of digit 5 | Deep branch of ulnar n. | Flexes digit 5 |
| Opponens digiti minimi | Flexor retinaculum and hook of hamate | Medial border of the fifth metacarpal | Deep branch of ulnar n. | Opposition and rotation of digit 5 |

- All three are supplied by the deep branch of the ulnar nerve, with no exception to parallel the deep head of flexor pollicis brevis.
- Abductor digiti minimi is the one that arises from the pisiform rather than the hook of the hamate, which is worth holding onto because the other two share the hamate origin.
- As on the thumb side, the opponens is the one inserting on the metacarpal, and it is the one that cups the palm.

> A hamate hook fracture pulls on the hypothenar muscles that attach to it, which is why the fragments separate.

Library: Lumbrical Muscles
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Lumbricals arising from the flexor digitorum profundus tendons](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s31.jpg)

## Identify it

- Four worm-like muscles in the central compartment of the palm. They arise from the tendons of flexor digitorum profundus and insert on the lateral side of the extensor expansion of digits 2 to 5.

## Key features

- The first and second lumbricals arise from the lateral two profundus tendons; the third and fourth arise from the medial three tendons.
- Innervation is split: lumbricals 1 and 2 by the median nerve, lumbricals 3 and 4 by the deep branch of the ulnar nerve.
- Their action is the one that looks contradictory until you see where they attach. They flex the fingers at the MCP joints and extend them at the PIP and DIP joints, which the lecture calls the Z movement.
- They can do both because they pass anterior to the MCP joint but attach to the extensor expansion, which runs behind the IP joints.
- They are tested by having the patient turn the palm upward and flex the MCP joints against resistance while keeping the interphalangeal joints extended.

> The lumbricals are the only muscles arising from a tendon rather than from bone, and the only ones that flex one joint while extending the next.

Library: Adductor Pollicis and Froment Sign
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Adductor pollicis and the paper test](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s33.jpg)

## Identify it

- A two-headed muscle of the adductor compartment. The oblique head arises from the bases of the second and third metacarpals, the capitate and adjacent carpals; the transverse head arises from the shaft of the third metacarpal.
- Both heads insert on the medial side of the base of the proximal phalanx of the thumb.

## Key features

- It is supplied by the deep branch of the ulnar nerve, and its action is to adduct the thumb and assist opposition.
- Froment sign tests it. The patient holds a sheet of paper between the palm and the thumb, and the examiner tries to pull it away.
- A patient with an intact adductor pollicis holds the paper with the thumb flat and the thumbnail at a right angle to the palm. One who has lost the muscle substitutes flexor pollicis longus, which is median supplied, and the thumb flexes at the interphalangeal joint to grip.
- A positive sign therefore points to the ulnar nerve, even though the movement being tested belongs to the thumb.

> Froment sign is a substitution test: it is positive when the patient recruits a median-supplied muscle to do an ulnar-supplied job.

Library: Interosseous Muscles and How They Are Tested
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Palmar and dorsal interossei](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s34.jpg)

## Identify it

- Three palmar interossei and four dorsal interossei, all in the deepest layer of the palm, all supplied by the deep branch of the ulnar nerve.

## Key features

| Group | Attachments | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Palmar interossei (3) | Palmar surfaces of metacarpals 2, 4 and 5 to the bases of the proximal phalanges and extensor expansions of digits 2, 4 and 5 | Adduct those digits toward the midline of the third digit (PAD) |
| Dorsal interossei (4) | Adjacent sides of two metacarpals to the bases of the proximal phalanges and extensor expansions of digits 2 to 4 | Abduct digits 2 to 4 from the midline (DAB) |

- Both groups also assist the lumbricals in flexing the MCP joints and extending the IP joints, because they too reach the extensor expansion.
- The dorsal interossei are tested by having the patient spread digits 2 and 3 apart against the examiner's resistance.
- The palmar interossei are tested by having the patient hold a sheet of paper between adjacent fingers and resist its being pulled away.
- The counts are four dorsal and three palmar, and the two mnemonics carry the actions: PAD for palmar adduction and DAB for dorsal abduction.

> Every interosseous muscle in the hand is ulnar, so loss of finger spread and finger squeeze together is an ulnar sign.

Library: Intrinsic Compared With Extrinsic Muscles of the Hand
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

## Identify it

- Intrinsic muscles have both attachments within the hand: the thenar group, the hypothenar group, the lumbricals, adductor pollicis and the interossei.
- Extrinsic muscles have their bellies in the forearm and reach the hand by long tendons.

## Key features

| | Intrinsic | Extrinsic |
|---|---|---|
| Belly | In the hand | In the forearm |
| Job | Fine positioning, spread, opposition, the MCP flex with IP extend combination | Gross power flexion of wrist and digits |
| Flexion of the wrist | None | Flexor carpi ulnaris, palmaris longus, flexor carpi radialis |
| Flexion of the digits | At MCP via lumbricals and interossei | Flexor pollicis longus, flexor digitorum profundus, flexor digitorum superficialis |

- The split matters clinically because a lesion at the wrist spares the extrinsic muscles, whose bellies lie proximal to the injury, while taking out the intrinsics.
- A lesion at the elbow takes out both, which is why the same nerve produces a different hand at the two levels.
- The lumbricals sit on the boundary, being intrinsic muscles that arise from an extrinsic tendon.

> Where the belly sits relative to the lesion decides what the hand can still do, which is the whole logic of wrist versus elbow injuries.

Library: Fibrous and Synovial Digital Sheaths
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Digital sheaths and the common flexor sheath](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s36.jpg)

## Identify it

- Fibrous digital sheaths are osseofibrous canals that bind the long flexor tendons closely to the phalanges and function as a series of pulleys.
- Within them, synovial sheaths containing synovial fluid allow the tendons to glide, lubricating them and decreasing friction.

## Key features

- The tendons of digits 2, 3 and 4 have separate synovial sheaths that do not communicate with anything proximal.
- The synovial sheath of digit 5 is continuous with the common flexor sheath, which extends proximally through the carpal tunnel into the anterior forearm.
- The thumb has its own continuous sheath along flexor pollicis longus.
- The pulley function is why the tendons do not bowstring away from the phalanges when the digit flexes.

> The anatomy of the sheaths, not the virulence of the organism, decides how far an infection in a finger can travel.

Library: Flexor Tenosynovitis and the Spread of Hand Infection
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

## Identify it

- Inflammation of a flexor tendon and its synovial sheath, usually following an injury to the finger that seeds the sheath.
- The finger swells and becomes painful to move.

## Key features

- Because digits 2 to 4 have separate synovial sheaths, infection there is usually confined to the infected digit.
- Infection in digit 5 is the dangerous one. Its sheath is continuous with the common flexor sheath, so pus can spread through the palm and the carpal tunnel into the anterior forearm.
- Spread into the carpal tunnel also increases the volume of the tunnel's contents, which is one of the mechanisms the lecture gives for compressing the median nerve.
- The swelling is fusiform along the whole digit rather than localised, because it follows the length of the sheath.

> A small puncture wound of the little finger can end as a forearm infection, and the route is the common flexor sheath.

Library: Ulnar Artery in the Hand
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Arteries of the hand](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s37.jpg)

## Identify it

- The ulnar artery enters the hand superficial to the flexor retinaculum, passing between the pisiform and the hook of the hamate through the ulnar canal, alongside the ulnar nerve.

## Key features

- It divides into the superficial palmar arch, which is its main continuation, and a deep branch that dives through the hypothenar compartment to join the deep palmar arch.
- The ulnar artery is the main contributor to the superficial palmar arch, which the radial artery completes.
- Because the artery runs with the nerve through the ulnar canal, a hamate hook fracture or sustained pressure on the hook threatens both at once.
- Distally the superficial arch is protected by passing deep to the palmar aponeurosis.

> The ulnar artery is the one vessel that shares a confined passage with a nerve at the wrist, which is why the two are injured together.

Library: Radial Artery in the Hand
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

## Identify it

- The radial artery reaches the hand by passing dorsally around the scaphoid and trapezium, along the floor of the anatomical snuffbox, then entering the palm by diving between the heads of the first dorsal interosseous muscle.

## Key features

- Before it leaves the front of the wrist it gives a small superficial palmar branch that contributes to the superficial palmar arch.
- In the palm it gives the princeps pollicis artery, which divides and passes along the sides of the thumb, and the radialis indicis artery, which runs along the lateral side of the second digit.
- It ends by anastomosing with the deep branch of the ulnar artery to form the deep palmar arch, to which it is the main contributor.
- Its course across the floor of the snuffbox puts it at risk in a scaphoid fracture and explains why the proximal scaphoid fragment can lose its supply.

> The radial artery supplies the thumb and index finger directly through the princeps pollicis and radialis indicis before it ever reaches the arch.

Library: Superficial and Deep Palmar Arterial Arches
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![The two palmar arterial arches](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s38.jpg)

## Identify it

- Two arterial arches cross the palm. The superficial arch lies immediately deep to the palmar aponeurosis; the deep arch lies across the metacarpals just distal to their bases.

## Key features

| | Superficial arch | Deep arch |
|---|---|---|
| Main contributor | Ulnar artery | Deep branch of the radial artery |
| Completed by | Superficial palmar branch of the radial artery | Deep branch of the ulnar artery |
| Position | Immediately deep to the palmar aponeurosis | Across the metacarpals just distal to their bases |
| Branches | Common palmar digital arteries, which branch into proper palmar digital arteries | Three palmar metacarpal arteries |

- Blood reaching a fingertip has usually travelled ulnar artery, superficial palmar arch, common palmar digital artery, proper palmar digital artery.
- The deep arch is the more proximal of the two despite its name referring to depth, so the superficial arch lies distal to it in the palm.
- Because each arch is fed from both sides, either the radial or the ulnar artery can usually supply the whole hand on its own.

> Two arches fed from two arteries is the anatomical reason a hand can survive losing one of them, and the reason the Allen test exists.

Library: Allen Test
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

## Identify it

- A bedside test of collateral blood flow from the radial and ulnar arteries to the hand, which specifically tests for the presence of complete superficial and deep palmar arches.

## Key features

- It is performed before procedures that have the potential to disrupt flow in either vessel, such as arterial puncture or cannulation, wrist surgery and cardiac catheterisation.
- The procedure: compress both the ulnar and radial arteries, have the patient clench and unclench the hand until the palm blanches, then release one artery and watch how long the palm takes to flush, paying attention to the thumb and thenar eminence. The test is then repeated for the other artery.
- A palm that flushes promptly after one artery is released shows that the released vessel alone can perfuse the hand through the arches.
- A palm that stays blanched means the arches are incomplete and the other vessel cannot be sacrificed safely.

> The test does not measure how good an artery is; it measures whether the other one can take over.

Library: Median Nerve in the Hand
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Branches of the median nerve in the hand](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s41.jpg)

## Identify it

- The median nerve (C5 to T1) enters the hand through the carpal tunnel, deep to the flexor retinaculum, and has three named branches here.

## Key features

| Branch | Where it arises | What it supplies |
|---|---|---|
| Palmar cutaneous branch | Proximal to the carpal tunnel, passing superficial to the retinaculum | Skin of the central palm |
| Recurrent branch | In the palm, running superficially back over the thenar eminence | Abductor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis, flexor pollicis brevis (superficial head) |
| Medial and lateral branches | Distal to the tunnel, continuing as common then proper palmar digital branches | First and second lumbricals, and sensation to the palmar aspect and distal dorsal halves of the lateral three and a half digits including the nail beds |

- The palmar cutaneous branch is the one that matters most for localising a lesion, because it leaves the nerve before the tunnel and therefore survives carpal tunnel syndrome.
- The recurrent branch is dangerously superficial. A relatively minor laceration of the thenar eminence can sever it.
- The sensory territory includes the nail beds of the lateral three and a half digits, which is dorsal skin supplied by a nerve that reaches the hand on the palmar side.

> Three branches, three different levels, and each one tells you something different about where the nerve was injured.

Library: Ulnar Nerve in the Hand
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Branches of the ulnar nerve in the hand](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s43.jpg)

## Identify it

- The ulnar nerve (C8, T1) enters the hand through the ulnar canal, lateral to the pisiform and superficial to the flexor retinaculum, then divides into a superficial and a deep branch.

## Key features

- The deep branch is almost entirely motor and supplies opponens digiti minimi, abductor digiti minimi, flexor digiti minimi, the lumbricals of digits 4 and 5, all the dorsal and palmar interossei, adductor pollicis and the deep head of flexor pollicis brevis. It also supplies the metacarpal bones and proprioception to the joints.
- The superficial branch continues as common palmar digital and then proper palmar digital branches, supplying the anterior skin of the medial one and a half digits and the distal dorsal aspects including the nail beds.
- The ulnar nerve supplies most of the intrinsic muscles of the hand. The median nerve, through its recurrent branch, supplies abductor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis, the superficial head of flexor pollicis brevis and the first two lumbricals. Flexor pollicis brevis is split between the two nerves, its deep head going to the deep branch of the ulnar nerve.
- Because the nerve is superficial to the retinaculum, it is not compressed in carpal tunnel syndrome and is not decompressed by carpal tunnel release.

> The ulnar nerve is the motor nerve of the hand, which is why ulnar lesions wreck fine movement while median lesions wreck the thumb and sensation.

Library: Peripheral Nerve Territories of the Hand
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Cutaneous nerve territories of the hand](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s44.jpg)

## Identify it

- Three nerves share the skin of the hand: median, ulnar and radial.

## Key features

| Nerve | Palmar surface | Dorsal surface |
|---|---|---|
| Median | Lateral three and a half digits and adjacent palm | Distal dorsal halves and nail beds of the lateral three and a half digits |
| Ulnar | Medial one and a half digits and adjacent palm | Medial one and a half digits, including the nail beds, and the medial dorsum |
| Radial | Little or none | The lateral dorsum of the hand and proximal dorsal digits |

- The median and ulnar nerves wrap around onto the dorsum distally to take the nail beds, which is why the dorsal and palmar maps do not match each other.
- The dividing line runs down the fourth digit, splitting it between median and ulnar territory on both surfaces.
- The central palm belongs to the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve, which is a separate territory from the digital branches and is spared in carpal tunnel syndrome.

> Numbness of the little finger and the medial half of the ring finger is ulnar wherever the lesion sits, because no other nerve supplies that skin.

Library: Median Nerve Lesion at the Wrist
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Comparison of both palms showing thenar wasting](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s45.jpg)

## Identify it

- Lacerations at the wrist often injure the median nerve because the nerve is relatively close to the surface there, and the carpal tunnel is the commonest site of median nerve lesions overall.
- The visible sign is decreased muscle mass in the thenar compartment and the distal palm, best seen by comparing the two hands.

## Key features

- Paralysis and wasting affect the thenar muscles and the first two lumbricals. Abductor pollicis brevis gives weakened thumb abduction, flexor pollicis brevis gives weakened thumb flexion, and opponens pollicis gives no opposition of the thumb at all.
- Fine control movements of the second and third digits are impaired through the loss of the first two lumbricals.
- Sensory loss covers the lateral palm and the palmar aspect of the lateral three and a half digits with their nail beds. The central palm is the exception, since its branch leaves the nerve proximal to the wrist.
- Flexion of the digits is preserved, because the long flexors are supplied in the forearm above the level of the injury.

> Opposition is the movement that is lost outright rather than weakened, and it is the one that makes the hand useless for grip.

Library: Median Nerve Lesion at the Elbow and the Hand of Benediction
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Patient asked to make a fist](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s47.jpg)

## Identify it

- A lesion in the elbow region produces everything a wrist lesion produces and adds the forearm muscles, because the branches to those muscles arise distal to the injury.
- Asked to make a fist, the patient cannot flex the first three digits, and the hand takes the posture called the hand of benediction.

## Key features

- Paralysed muscles: pronator teres, flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus, flexor digitorum superficialis, flexor pollicis longus, the lateral part of flexor digitorum profundus, pronator quadratus, the thenar muscles and the lateral two lumbricals.
- The functional deficits the lecture lists are loss of flexion at the PIP joints (flexor digitorum superficialis) and at the DIP joints of digits 2 and 3 (lateral flexor digitorum profundus), weakened wrist and finger flexion, weakened forearm pronation, and difficulty making a fist.
- Flexion of digits 4 and 5 is only weakened rather than lost, because the medial part of flexor digitorum profundus is ulnar supplied.
- The nerve is also at risk where it passes between the two heads of pronator teres, which the lecture names as pronator syndrome.

> The index and middle fingers stay extended during an attempted fist because their long flexors are median and their neighbours' are not.

Library: Recurrent Branch Injury in the Thenar Eminence
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

## Identify it

- An isolated injury to the recurrent branch of the median nerve, caused by a relatively minor laceration of the thenar eminence, where the branch lies subcutaneously.

## Key features

- The result is paralysis of the three thenar muscles supplied by that branch: abductor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis and the superficial head of flexor pollicis brevis.
- Sensation is entirely normal, because the recurrent branch is purely motor and the digital branches are untouched.
- This is the pattern that separates it from carpal tunnel syndrome and from a wrist laceration, both of which take sensation as well.
- The lecture is explicit that the branch must be repaired or the thenar muscles will atrophy.

> A patient who cannot oppose the thumb but has completely normal sensation has a recurrent branch injury, not carpal tunnel syndrome.

Library: Ulnar Nerve Lesion at the Wrist and the Ulnar Claw Hand
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Patient asked to open the hand](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s50.jpg)

## Identify it

- Compression or laceration of the ulnar nerve at the wrist, in the ulnar canal. Asked to open the hand, the patient cannot extend the interphalangeal joints of digits 4 and 5, so those two fingers stay curled while their metacarpophalangeal joints sit in hyperextension.
- That posture is the ulnar claw hand.

## Key features

- The muscles responsible for the lost movement are lumbricals 3 and 4, which normally flex the MCP joints while extending the IP joints.
- The claw is produced by the unopposed action of the long extensors at the MCP joints and of flexor digitorum profundus at the IP joints.
- Other deficits: weakness and atrophy of the intrinsic muscles (lumbricals 3 and 4, the palmar and dorsal interossei, adductor pollicis, the hypothenar muscles), and loss of abduction and adduction of the digits.
- Sensory loss is on the medial hand and the medial one and a half digits.

> At the wrist the ability to flex digits 4 and 5 is unaffected and there is no radial deviation of the hand, and that pair of findings is what separates this lesion from one at the elbow.

Library: Ulnar Nerve Lesion at the Elbow
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

![Normal and lesioned wrist flexion compared](library/images/msk-week-15/wrist-s52.jpg)

## Identify it

- A proximal ulnar nerve lesion, most commonly posterior to the medial epicondyle in the cubital tunnel, or between the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris.
- It produces everything the wrist lesion produces and adds the forearm muscles the nerve supplies.

## Key features

- Wrist flexion is impaired through loss of flexor carpi ulnaris and the medial part of flexor digitorum profundus.
- When the patient tries to flex the wrist, the hand is drawn to the lateral side, because flexor carpi radialis is still working and has nothing opposing it.
- Making a fist is difficult, since in the absence of opposition the MCP joints hyperextend and the patient cannot flex digits 4 and 5 at the DIP joints.
- Sensory loss covers the medial hand on both palmar and dorsal sides and the medial one and a half fingers, the same territory as a wrist lesion.

> Radial deviation on attempted wrist flexion places the lesion at the elbow, because that sign needs flexor carpi ulnaris to be denervated.

Library: Comparing Median and Ulnar Lesions at the Wrist and the Elbow
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Wrist and Palm of Hand

## Identify it

- Four lesions, two nerves at two levels, distinguished by which muscles lie distal to the injury and by one or two signs unique to each.

## Key features

| Lesion | Posture or sign | Muscles lost beyond the hand | Sensory loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Median at the wrist | Thenar wasting, no opposition | None | Lateral 3.5 digits, central palm spared |
| Median at the elbow | Hand of benediction on attempted fist | Pronators, flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus, flexor digitorum superficialis, flexor pollicis longus, lateral flexor digitorum profundus | Lateral 3.5 digits and lateral palm |
| Ulnar at the wrist | Clawing of digits 4 and 5 may occur, but flexion of those digits is unaffected and there is no radial deviation | None | Medial 1.5 digits |
| Ulnar at the elbow | The characteristic ulnar claw hand, with the hand drawn to the lateral side on attempted wrist flexion | Flexor carpi ulnaris, medial flexor digitorum profundus | Medial 1.5 digits, palmar and dorsal |

- The benediction hand and the claw hand look similar in a photograph but arise from opposite situations. Benediction appears when the patient tries to make a fist and the median digits will not flex; the claw appears when the patient tries to open the hand and the ulnar digits will not straighten.
- The two ulnar levels are separated by what the forearm muscles are still doing. At the wrist the ability to flex digits 4 and 5 is unaffected and the hand does not deviate on wrist flexion, because flexor carpi ulnaris and the medial part of flexor digitorum profundus take their branches above the injury. At the elbow both are denervated, so flexion of those digits is impaired and the hand is drawn to the lateral side.
- Only two of the four take a sign from the forearm: the pronation weakness of a proximal median lesion and the radial deviation of a proximal ulnar lesion.

> Ask what the patient was trying to do when the photograph was taken, because the same hand means different things making a fist and opening out.

Library: Rheumatism and the rheumatologic approach to joint pain
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Historical illustration of the four humors](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s02.jpg)

## Identify it

- Rheumatism is any painful disorder of joints, muscles or connective tissue. The word comes from an older idea that disease followed a flow of humors through the body.
- The lecture builds one diagnostic habit and then applies it twice. First decide where the pain comes from, then decide whether the process is inflammatory, then describe it by timing, pattern and distribution.

## Key features

- The six objectives of the lecture are to distinguish articular from non-articular disease processes, differentiate inflammatory from non-inflammatory musculoskeletal disorders, compose a framework based on timing, pattern and distribution, apply that framework to rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis, explain the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis, and list the notable extra-articular manifestations of rheumatoid arthritis.
- Two diseases carry the whole lecture, rheumatoid arthritis and primary osteoarthritis, and almost every comparison in the deck is drawn between those two.

> Slides with a gold border and stars are flagged by the lecturer as the summary information that is most high yield for examination. Four slides carry that border, and all four are comparison tables.

Library: Articular and periarticular structures
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Labeled diagram of a synovial joint and its surrounding structures](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s07.jpg)

## Identify it

- Articular means the joint itself, which the lecture defines as five structures: subchondral bone, cartilage, synovial fluid, synovium and capsule.
- Periarticular means around the joint, which the lecture defines as five more: tendon, tenosynovium, enthesis, bursa and ligament.

## Key features

- The articular list is the one to memorize first, because an effusion can only come from a structure on that list, and the lecture states flatly that effusion means arthritis.
- The periarticular list explains the common mimics of arthritis. Tendinitis, tenosynovitis, enthesitis and bursitis all hurt near a joint without being a disease of the joint.
- Enthesis means the point where a tendon or ligament inserts into bone, and it is the structure that links this list to the spondyloarthropathies named later in the deck.

> Problems of articular structures produce pain through the whole joint, while problems of periarticular structures produce pain over one part of it.

Library: Nonarticular and referred sources of musculoskeletal pain
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Diagram showing articular, periarticular, nonarticular and referred pain sources](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s09.jpg)

## Identify it

- Nonarticular means away from the joint but still within the musculoskeletal system, and the lecture lists two structures, muscle and bone.
- Referred means away from the musculoskeletal system altogether, and the lecture lists three sources, visceral, vascular and neurologic.

## Key features

- The four categories are built up one at a time across slides 6 through 9, and by slide 9 the full map is articular, periarticular, nonarticular and referred.
- Muscle and bone pain is the nonarticular category, so a diffuse myalgia or a bone lesion is classified here rather than as arthritis.
- Visceral, vascular and neurologic sources are grouped together because none of them originate in the musculoskeletal system, even though the patient feels the pain there.

> Getting this classification right is the first objective of the lecture, and the rest of the diagnostic framework only applies once the pain has been placed in the articular box.

Library: Localizing the source of pain on examination
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Table comparing history and physical findings by source of pain](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s13.jpg)

## Identify it

- The lecture gives a history finding and a physical finding for each of the four sources, and the discriminating feature on examination is the relationship between active and passive range of motion.

## Key features

| Source | History | Physical examination |
|---|---|---|
| Articular | Pain in the joint area, pain in all directions of movement | Tenderness with or without swelling of the entire joint line, limited or painful range of motion in all directions, pain with active range equal to passive range, effusion means arthritis |
| Periarticular | Pain localized near the joint, pain with some movements | Tenderness with or without swelling over part of the joint, limited or painful range of motion in some directions, pain with active range greater than passive range |
| Nonarticular | Hurts all over, diffuse, unrelated to joint range of motion, history does not fit a pattern | Non-articular examination findings, pain unrelated to joint range of motion |
| Referred, neurologic | Numbness, paresthesia, burning, painless weakness | Weakness, sensory loss, reflex changes |

- Active range of motion loads the tendons and muscles, while passive range of motion does not, so a structure outside the joint hurts more when the patient moves the joint themselves.
- Painless weakness and sensory loss point to a neurologic referred source rather than to anything in the joint.

> Pain with active range of motion that exceeds pain with passive range of motion localizes the problem to a periarticular structure, while equal pain in both localizes it to the joint.

Library: Inflammatory versus noninflammatory joint disease: the history
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Table comparing inflammatory and noninflammatory features](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s16.jpg)

## Identify it

- This is the second objective, and the lecturer flags the comparison table with a gold border and stars, which marks it as summary information that is most high yield for examination.

## Key features

| Feature | Inflammatory | Noninflammatory |
|---|---|---|
| Morning stiffness | Greater than 1 hour | 30 minutes or less |
| Fatigue | Significant | Minimal |
| Activity | Improves pain and stiffness | Worsens pain |
| Rest | Worsens, often causes significant gelling | Often improves symptoms, gelling under 20 minutes |
| Systemic involvement (extra-articular manifestations, fever, weight loss) | Yes | No |
| Corticosteroid responsiveness | Yes | No |
| ESR and CRP | Often elevated | Normal |

- Gelling is the stiffness that develops after a period of immobility, and the lecture separates the two processes by how long it lasts and whether rest relieves or aggravates the underlying pain.
- The five cardinal signs of inflammation introduced on the preceding slide are what the examination is looking for in the joint itself.

> The single most useful historical discriminator is the direction the symptoms move with use, since inflammatory pain improves with activity and noninflammatory pain worsens with it.

Library: Inflammatory versus noninflammatory arthritis: the physical examination
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Clinical photographs of two hands with accompanying examination findings](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s17.jpg)

## Identify it

- Inflammatory arthritis on examination shows tenderness with painful or limited range of motion, fusiform swelling of the involved joints that feels spongy or boggy to palpation, and erythema with warmth.
- Noninflammatory arthritis shows tenderness with painful or limited range of motion, bony thickening and enlargement, an effusion that may be present but with minimal warmth, and crepitus.

## Key features

- Fusiform means the whole joint is swollen into a spindle shape, which is what soft tissue synovitis looks like from the outside.
- The texture of the swelling is the discriminator that the lecture emphasizes. Synovitis is spongy or boggy, while osteophytes and bony remodeling are hard.
- Both processes can produce an effusion, so the presence of fluid alone does not separate them. The warmth, the erythema and the quality of the swelling do.
- Crepitus belongs to the noninflammatory column and reflects the irregular cartilage and bone surfaces of a mechanically damaged joint.

> Palpating the swelling is the step that settles the question at the bedside, because boggy soft tissue means synovitis and hard enlargement means bone.

Library: Synovial fluid analysis: what to send and how the classes are defined
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Photograph of five synovial fluid samples with a classification table](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s19.jpg)

## Identify it

- Every joint aspirate is sent for four studies: cell count with differential, crystal analysis, Gram stain, and aerobic and anaerobic bacterial culture.

## Key features

| Class | Appearance | WBC per mm3 | Percent PMNs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | Clear, viscous, pale yellow | Under 200 | Under 10 |
| Non-inflammatory | Clear to slightly turbid yellow, viscous | 200 to 2,000 | Under 20 |
| Inflammatory | Cloudy, yellow, watery | 2,000 to 50,000 | Over 50 |
| Septic or pyarthrosis | Turbid, purulent | Over 50,000 | Over 75 |
| Hemorrhagic | Bloody | High RBC, WBC equivalent to peripheral blood | Not applicable |

- Viscosity falls as the fluid becomes more inflammatory, so a clear viscous sample and a cloudy watery sample sit at opposite ends of the same scale.
- A hemorrhagic aspirate is defined by its red cells rather than by its white cell count, since the white count matches peripheral blood.
- The 2,000 cell threshold is the one the lecture reuses later, where rheumatoid fluid is inflammatory with more than 2,000 cells and no crystals, and osteoarthritic fluid is non-inflammatory with fewer than 2,000.

> All four studies are sent on every aspirate, because septic arthritis and crystal arthritis can look identical on the day the patient presents.

Library: Polarized microscopy and the red plate compensator
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Diagram of birefringence under a red plate compensator](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s20.jpg)

## Identify it

- Positive birefringence means the crystal appears blue when it lies parallel to the axis of the red plate compensator and yellow when it lies perpendicular.
- Negative birefringence is the reverse, yellow when parallel to the compensator axis and blue when perpendicular.

## Key features

- The compensator axis is marked on the slide by an arrow, and the color is only interpretable relative to that arrow, which is why the lecture shows paired images of the same crystal in two orientations.
- Under ordinary light a crystal is visible but colorless, so the polarizer and the compensator are what turn shape into a diagnosis.
- The direction of the color change, not the brightness, is what defines the sign. Brightness describes whether the birefringence is strong or weak.

> Blue when parallel is positive and yellow when parallel is negative, and that one sentence is enough to work out every crystal on the next slide.

Library: Crystals in synovial fluid: shape, birefringence and disease
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Polarized micrographs of four crystal types with a comparison table](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s21.jpg)

## Identify it

| Crystal | Shape | Birefringence | Disease |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monosodium urate | Needle shaped | Strongly negative | Gout |
| Calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate | Rhomboid or rod shaped | Weakly positive | Acute and chronic CPPD arthritis |
| Cholesterol | Notched, rectangular plates | Positive and negative | Chronic inflammatory joint effusion |
| Steroid | Flat, variably shaped plates | Positive and negative | After intraarticular steroid injection |

## Key features

- Monosodium urate and calcium pyrophosphate differ in every column, so shape alone usually settles the question and birefringence confirms it.
- Cholesterol and steroid crystals both show mixed birefringence, so shape is the only reliable discriminator between them. Cholesterol plates are notched and rectangular, while steroid plates are flat and irregular.
- A steroid crystal in the fluid tells you the joint was injected, not that there is a crystal arthropathy.

> Needles that are strongly negative are gout, and rhomboids that are weakly positive are CPPD, which is the pairing worth being able to produce without thinking.

Library: Common forms of arthritis: the inflammatory and noninflammatory lists
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Slide listing inflammatory and noninflammatory arthritides](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s25.jpg)

## Identify it

- Inflammatory arthritis divides into autoimmune or autoinflammatory disease, crystal-induced arthritis, and infection-associated arthritis.
- Noninflammatory arthritis in this lecture means osteoarthritis, either primary or secondary.

## Key features

- The autoimmune group holds rheumatoid arthritis and the seronegative spondyloarthropathies, which are non-radiographic axial spondyloarthropathy, ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, reactive arthritis and arthritis associated with inflammatory bowel disease.
- The crystal-induced group holds gout and calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease.
- Secondary osteoarthritis has five listed causes: traumatic from prior joint injury, inflammatory from longstanding inflammatory arthritis in that joint, congenital, metabolic and neuropathic.
- Longstanding rheumatoid arthritis can therefore end in secondary osteoarthritis of the same joint, which is the one place the two halves of the list meet.

> This list is repeated three times in the deck, which marks it as the frame the lecturer wants in place before either disease is discussed in detail.

Library: Describing an arthritis: timing, pattern and distribution
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Slide defining timing, pattern and distribution of joint involvement](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s24.jpg)

## Identify it

- Timing asks four questions: acute (under 6 weeks) or chronic (over 6 weeks), intermittent or episodic versus persistent, migratory or additive, and rapid or insidious onset.
- Pattern asks how many joints and whether the involvement is symmetric. Monoarticular is 1 joint, oligoarticular is 4 or fewer, and polyarticular is more than 4.
- Distribution asks whether small or large joints are favored, whether the axial skeleton (sacroiliac joints and spine) is involved, and exactly which joints are affected.

## Key features

- Migratory means the inflammation leaves one joint as it appears in the next, while additive means new joints are added without the earlier ones improving. Both rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis are additive.
- The six week line separates acute from chronic, and both of the diseases this lecture diagnoses are chronic by that definition.
- The last item under distribution, exactly which joints are affected, is what actually separates rheumatoid arthritis from osteoarthritis, because the two diseases have almost complementary joint maps.

> This is the framework named in the third objective, and the next sections apply the same three headings to osteoarthritis and then to rheumatoid arthritis in the same order.

Library: Primary osteoarthritis: mechanism of joint failure
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Histology and arthroscopic images of degenerating articular cartilage](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s27.jpg)

## Identify it

- Primary osteoarthritis is mechanical destruction of articular cartilage and then of the underlying bone, which the lecture calls wear and tear arthritis.
- Inadequate repair leads to new bone formation, and that new bone is the osteophyte.

## Key features

- The labeled findings on the slide are normal cartilage, fraying cartilage, a fissure or cleft, focal cartilage destruction with loss, and the osteophyte.
- Cartilage damage is focal rather than uniform, which is why the joint space narrows asymmetrically on radiographs.
- The sequence runs cartilage first, then subchondral bone, then attempted repair. Nothing in this sequence is immune mediated, which is the distinction the comparison table later draws against rheumatoid arthritis.

> Osteoarthritis is simultaneously destructive and productive, losing cartilage in one place while building osteophytes at the margins.

Library: Eburnation and the gross appearance of osteoarthritis
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Gross specimen of a femoral head](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s28.jpg)

## Identify it

- Eburnation is subchondral bone that has been polished by direct bone on bone contact after the overlying articular cartilage has worn away, and the comparison table describes the result as a polished ivory appearance.

## Key features

- The femoral head specimen shows articular cartilage degeneration with an area of exposed, smooth, hardened bone where cartilage no longer covers the surface.
- Eburnation requires full thickness cartilage loss, so it appears late and marks advanced disease.
- Subchondral bone thickening accompanies eburnation, and on radiographs the same process appears as subchondral sclerosis with subchondral cysts.

> Eburnation is the macroscopic endpoint of the mechanism on the previous page, and it is specific to the noninflammatory, mechanical pathway.

Library: Primary osteoarthritis: symptoms
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Slide listing the symptom pattern of primary osteoarthritis](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s29.jpg)

## Identify it

- The osteoarthritic symptom set is noninflammatory joint pain that is worse with activity, relieved with rest, usually worst at the end of the day, with 30 minutes or less of morning stiffness and no associated systemic symptoms.

## Key features

- Every item maps onto the noninflammatory column of the gold bordered comparison table, so this page is that table applied to one disease.
- Worst at the end of the day is the mirror image of rheumatoid arthritis, where the morning is the worst time.
- The absence of systemic symptoms matters because fever, weight loss and significant fatigue all argue for an inflammatory process instead.

> Pain that builds through the day with use and settles with rest is the osteoarthritis history, and it is the opposite of the rheumatoid history in both directions.

Library: Primary osteoarthritis: timing, pattern and joint distribution
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Slide showing the timing, pattern and joint distribution of osteoarthritis](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s30.jpg)

## Identify it

- Timing is chronic (over 6 weeks), persistent, additive and insidious in onset.
- Pattern is mono-, oligo- or polyarticular, and asymmetric.
- Distribution involves both large and small joints, with axial involvement common in the cervical and lumbar spine.
- The presentation slide crowds the distribution bullets into the pattern column for space, so read them against the framework on the approach slide rather than against the column they sit in.

## Key features

- Osteoarthritis involves the knees, hips, cervical and lumbar spine, first carpometacarpal joint, first metatarsophalangeal joint, PIPs and DIPs.
- Osteoarthritis spares the MCPs and the wrists.
- The sparing list is the part that is tested, because the MCPs and wrists are exactly the joints rheumatoid arthritis attacks, and the first carpometacarpal joint is exactly the one rheumatoid arthritis spares.
- Asymmetry is also discriminating, since rheumatoid arthritis is symmetric.

> A hand with involvement at the DIPs and the base of the thumb but normal MCPs and wrists is osteoarthritis by distribution alone.

Library: Osteoarthritis of the hands: Heberden and Bouchard nodes
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Clinical photograph of hands with labeled osteoarthritic changes](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s31.jpg)

## Identify it

- Heberden nodes are bony hypertrophy at the DIP joints and Bouchard nodes are bony hypertrophy at the PIP joints.
- The other labeled findings are squaring of the first carpometacarpal joint, interosseous muscle wasting and sparing of the MCPs.

## Key features

- The nodes are hard and fixed because they are bone, and the comparison table states that osteoarthritic hands show no soft tissue swelling.
- Squaring at the base of the thumb comes from first carpometacarpal involvement, which is a joint rheumatoid arthritis spares.
- Interosseous muscle wasting appears in both diseases and therefore does not help discriminate between them.

> Heberden is distal and Bouchard is proximal, and the DIP involvement alone rules against rheumatoid arthritis, which spares the DIPs.

Library: Erosive osteoarthritis and the gull wing deformity
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Bilateral hand radiographs showing erosive changes](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s32.jpg)

## Identify it

- Erosive osteoarthritis produces a central erosion with lateral osteophytes, and the combination gives the gull wing deformity on radiographs.

## Key features

- The other radiographic features named on the slide are angulation and asymmetric joint space narrowing.
- The erosion sits in the center of the joint surface, which is what distinguishes it from the marginal erosions of rheumatoid arthritis that start at the edges where bare bone meets synovium.
- Erosive osteoarthritis keeps the osteoarthritic distribution, so the findings cluster at the interphalangeal joints rather than at the MCPs and wrists.

> Central erosion with lateral osteophytes is erosive osteoarthritis, while marginal erosion without osteophytes is rheumatoid arthritis.

Library: Rheumatoid arthritis: epidemiology
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

## Identify it

- Prevalence is 0.5 to 1 percent of the worldwide population, which is 2 to 4 million people in the United States.
- Peak incidence falls in the fourth to sixth decade, and the ratio of women to men is 3 to 1.

## Key features

- Female sex is listed again as a risk factor on the gold bordered comparison table, alongside smoking and the HLA-DR4 haplotype.
- A peak in the fourth to sixth decade means most new cases appear between roughly age 30 and age 60, which is earlier than the older age that characterizes primary osteoarthritis.
- The 3 to 1 ratio is the same direction as osteoarthritis, where female sex is also a risk factor, so sex alone does not separate the two diseases.

Library: Citrullination and the environmental triggers of rheumatoid arthritis
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Diagram of the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s36.jpg)

## Identify it

- Citrullination is a single amino acid change from arginine to citrulline, carried out by a peptidylarginine deiminase, and it happens to self-proteins at mucosal surfaces.
- The presence of citrullinated self-proteins is hypothesized to lead to a break in self-tolerance.

## Key features

- Three environmental risk factors are named, all of them acting at a mucosal surface: smoking, periodontal disease and the gut microbiome.
- Genetics contributes MHC class II alleles plus more than 100 other loci, and epigenetics contributes histone modification and DNA methylation.
- The reason this mechanism is worth knowing in detail is that the antibody test used clinically, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide, is directed at the product of this exact reaction.

> The disease starts at a mucosal surface rather than in the joint, and smoking, gum disease and the gut flora are all ways of inflaming a mucosal surface.

Library: The shared epitope and its synergy with smoking
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Chart of rheumatoid arthritis risk by smoking exposure and shared epitope status](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s37.jpg)

## Identify it

- The shared epitope is a group of HLA-DRB1 alleles with similar coding of 5 amino acids at positions 70 to 74 in the third hypervariable region of the antigen binding domain, and the named alleles are HLA-DRB1*04 and HLA-DRB1*01.
- It carries the highest genetic susceptibility to rheumatoid arthritis.

## Key features

- The hypothesis stated on the slide is that the provoking antigen interacting with the shared epitope is a citrullinated self-protein, which ties the genetic risk directly to the mechanism on the previous page.
- Smoking is highly synergistic with genetic predisposition at the shared epitope, meaning the combined risk is greater than the sum of the separate risks.
- Risk rises with pack-years and with gene dose, so the highest odds ratios on the chart belong to heavy smokers who carry two copies of the shared epitope.
- HLA-DR4 positivity appears again later as a risk factor for extra-articular disease.

> The shared epitope is a stretch of five amino acids in the peptide binding groove, which is why different HLA-DRB1 alleles can confer the same risk.

Library: Rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP: sensitivity, specificity and interpretation

## Identify it

| Antibody | Sensitivity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|
| Rheumatoid factor | 60 to 80 percent | 80 to 86 percent |
| Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide, also called ACPA | 57 to 67 percent | 93 to 99 percent |

## Key features

- Rheumatoid factor is an IgM antibody that targets the Fc region of IgG, and the comparison table labels it nonspecific while labeling anti-CCP specific.
- Other causes of a positive rheumatoid factor are other autoimmune diseases, neoplasm, infections including endocarditis, hepatitis C with cryoglobulinemia, and other organ-specific chronic inflammatory diseases.

Library: Synovitis, pannus and the cellular events of the rheumatoid joint
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Diagram of the cellular events in the rheumatoid synovium](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s40.jpg)

## Identify it

- Synovitis is inflammation of the synovium, and synovial hyperplasia is pannus.
- Four events are listed: influx and proliferation of immune cells, production of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, release of collagenases and metalloproteinases, and activation of osteoclasts.

## Key features

- Each event explains one finding. Immune cell influx and proliferation explain the thickened synovium, collagenases and metalloproteinases explain cartilage loss, and osteoclast activation explains bone erosion.
- The labeled diagram shows increased angiogenesis and an expanded synovial sublining, which is how the tissue supports its own growth.
- RANKL links the inflamed synovium to the osteoclast, and IL-6, IL-1 and TNF are the cytokines shown driving the loop.
- Periarticular osteoporosis, increased synovial fluid, loss of cartilage and erosion are the four joint-level consequences labeled on the accompanying figure.

> Pannus is the structure that does the damage, since it is the inflamed synovium growing across and then into the cartilage and bone.

Library: Normal joint and active rheumatoid joint under the microscope
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Four histology panels comparing a normal joint with an active rheumatoid joint](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s41.jpg)

## Identify it

- The normal synovial lining is 1 to 3 cells thick, and on low power the normal diarthrodial joint shows a smooth cartilage surface over regular subchondral bone.
- The active rheumatoid joint shows pannus, which appears as a thick, cellular, lymphocyte rich tissue advancing over the bone and cartilage margin.

## Key features

- The normal synovium sits on loose tissue with adipocytes and has no appreciable inflammatory infiltrate.
- Pannus is recognizable at high power by the density of mononuclear cells, and at low power by the loss of the clean interface between synovium, cartilage and bone.
- The lecture gives the normal lining thickness as a number because it is the easiest way to call hyperplasia on a slide.

> A synovial lining more than a few cells thick, with a dense lymphocytic infiltrate underneath it, is pannus.

Library: Pannus in gross specimens of the knee and patella

## Identify it

- In gross specimens the pannus appears as shaggy, irregular, frond-like tissue covering and replacing the smooth articular surface.

Library: Rheumatoid arthritis versus osteoarthritis: pathogenesis, risk factors and autoantibodies
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

## Identify it

- This is one of the four gold bordered, starred slides, which the lecturer marks as the summary information that is most high yield for examination.

Library: Rheumatoid arthritis: symptoms

## Identify it

- The rheumatoid symptom set is inflammatory joint pain with morning stiffness lasting more than 1 hour, improving with activity, worse with rest, usually worst in the morning, and possibly accompanied by systemic symptoms.

## Key features

Library: Rheumatoid arthritis: timing, pattern and joint distribution
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Slide showing the timing, pattern and joint distribution of rheumatoid arthritis](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s46.jpg)

## Identify it

- Timing is chronic (over 6 weeks), persistent, additive and insidious in onset.
- Pattern is polyarticular and symmetric.
- Distribution favors small joints over large, with axial involvement limited to C1-C2.

## Key features

- Rheumatoid arthritis involves the PIPs, MCPs, wrists, elbows, shoulders, hips, knees, ankles, tarsus, MTPs and C1-C2.
- Rheumatoid arthritis spares the DIPs, the thoracic and lumbar spine, the sacroiliac joints and the first carpometacarpal joint.
- The timing line is word for word the same as osteoarthritis, which means timing alone cannot separate the two diseases and the work is done by pattern and distribution.
- Symmetric and polyarticular is the pattern, against asymmetric and variable joint count in osteoarthritis.

Library: The rheumatoid hand on examination: fusiform swelling and DIP sparing
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Clinical photograph of a hand with active rheumatoid arthritis](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s47.jpg)

## Identify it

- Active rheumatoid arthritis shows fusiform swelling at the PIPs and MCPs that is spongy, boggy and painful, with interosseous muscle wasting and sparing of the DIPs.

## Key features

- Fusiform swelling is spindle shaped because the synovium surrounds the whole joint, so the finger is widest at the joint and narrows on either side.
- The soft, boggy texture is the finding that separates this from the hard nodes of osteoarthritis, and the comparison table records that osteoarthritic hands have no soft tissue swelling.
- Interosseous wasting reflects disuse and appears as hollowing between the metacarpals on the dorsum of the hand.
- This is the early, active appearance. The deformities on the following pages belong to late disease.

> Swelling at the PIPs and MCPs with normal DIPs is the rheumatoid hand, and the sparing of the DIPs is as diagnostic as the swelling itself.

Library: Radiographic findings in rheumatoid arthritis
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Radiographs of hands showing rheumatoid changes](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s48.jpg)

## Identify it

- Four radiographic findings are labeled: symmetric soft tissue swelling, marginal erosions, juxta-articular (periarticular) osteopenia, and symmetric joint space narrowing.

## Key features

- Erosions are marginal because the bare area at the joint margin, where synovium meets bone without covering cartilage, is where pannus reaches bone first.
- Osteopenia is juxta-articular, meaning it surrounds the inflamed joint rather than affecting the whole skeleton, and it reflects local inflammation.
- Joint space narrowing is symmetric across the joint surface, unlike osteoarthritis where cartilage loss is focal and narrowing is uneven.
- The comparison table gives the osteoarthritic counterpart as osteophytes, asymmetric joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis and cysts. There are no osteophytes in the rheumatoid list.

> Erosions with osteopenia and no osteophytes is rheumatoid arthritis, while osteophytes with sclerosis and no osteopenia is osteoarthritis.

Library: Ulnar drift in rheumatoid arthritis
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Clinical photograph of hands with ulnar drift](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s49.jpg)

## Identify it

- Ulnar drift is a combination of two deviations in opposite directions, radial deviation at the wrist and ulnar deviation at the MCPs.

## Key features

- The wrist component follows wrist inflammation, which weakens the ulnar collateral ligament and the extensor carpi ulnaris and allows the carpal bones to displace.
- The MCP component follows MCP inflammation, which weakens the joint capsule and lets the extensor tendons dislocate off the tops of the metacarpal heads.
- The deformity is therefore a consequence of soft tissue failure rather than of bone destruction, which is why it appears in longstanding disease.

> Ulnar drift is a zigzag, with the wrist angling one way and the fingers angling the other.

Library: Swan neck and boutonniere deformities
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Tendon diagrams and clinical photographs of two finger deformities](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s50.jpg)

## Identify it

- Swan neck deformity is hyperextension at the PIP with flexion at the DIP.
- Boutonniere deformity is flexion at the PIP with hyperextension at the DIP.

## Key features

- The two deformities are exact opposites at both joints, so naming the position of the PIP is enough to identify either one.
- Boutonniere means buttonhole in French, and the name describes the proximal phalanx head pushing through a defect in the central slip of the extensor tendon the way a button passes through its hole.
- Swan neck is named for the curve of the finger in profile, with the PIP arched back and the fingertip dropped.
- Both are late deformities of chronic synovitis and both appear in the late row of the gold bordered comparison table alongside ulnar deviation.

> If the PIP is hyperextended it is swan neck, and if the PIP is flexed it is boutonniere.

Library: Thumb Z deformity and the piano key sign
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Clinical photograph of hands showing thumb and wrist deformities](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s51.jpg)

## Identify it

- Z deformity of the thumb, also called hitchhiker thumb, is flexion at the first MCP with hyperextension at the IP joint.
- The piano key sign is a prominent ulnar styloid that can be depressed and springs back, caused by destruction of the ulnar collateral ligament leading to instability of the ulnar styloid.

## Key features

- The slide also labels interosseous muscle wasting and volar finger subluxation on the same hands.
- The thumb deformity is the thumb equivalent of boutonniere, since the proximal joint flexes and the distal joint hyperextends.

> These are deformities of late disease, and like the others they come from ligament and tendon failure rather than from bone loss.

Library: Atlantoaxial subluxation and the C1-C2 rule
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Lateral cervical radiographs in flexion with a labeled diagram of the atlas](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s52.jpg)

## Identify it

- C1-C2 is the only spinal joint that is ever involved in rheumatoid arthritis.
- Weakening of the transverse ligament allows horizontal subluxation, which can compress the spinal cord and sometimes the vertebral artery. An atlantodental interval greater than 3 mm is abnormal.

## Key features

- Symptoms are neck and occipital pain or stiffness, a shock-like sensation running from the neck down the back with neck flexion, painless sensory loss in the hands and feet, drop attacks, dizziness, syncope and sudden death.
- Diagnosis is by flexion and extension radiography of the cervical spine, and the lecture states that rheumatoid patients need these films both for symptoms and before surgery or intubation.
- Treatment is surgical fusion.
- The transverse ligament of the atlas holds the odontoid against the anterior arch of the atlas, so when it fails the odontoid moves back toward the cord.

> Low back pain should never be attributed to rheumatoid arthritis activity, because the thoracic spine, lumbar spine and sacroiliac joints are all on the spare list.

Library: Methotrexate in rheumatoid arthritis: dosing, mechanism and adverse effects
Folder: MSK / Week 15 / Introduction to Rheumatologic Disease

![Clinical photographs of methotrexate adverse effects](library/images/msk-week-15/rheum-s64.jpg)

## Identify it

- Methotrexate is first line treatment for rheumatoid arthritis if there is no contraindication, dosed at 15 to 25 mg every 7 days, in oral and subcutaneous formulations.
- The mechanism of action at rheumatoid arthritis dosing is inhibition of AICAR transformylase, which increases adenosine.

## Key features

- It must be given with daily folic acid to mitigate some of the adverse effects.
- Common adverse effects are nausea and gastrointestinal upset, oral ulcers and mucositis, leukopenia, alopecia and hepatotoxicity.
- Rare but important adverse effects are accelerated rheumatoid nodulosis, teratogenicity and pneumonitis.
- Accelerated rheumatoid nodulosis is the one that causes diagnostic confusion, because new nodules appearing on treatment look like worsening disease when they are a drug effect.
- The weekly schedule matters clinically, since daily dosing of the same amount is a recognized cause of serious toxicity.

> The dose is weekly, not daily, and the increase in adenosine rather than antifolate activity is what explains the anti-inflammatory effect at these doses.
