Library: Nucleolus and ribosomes
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Cell Structure and Intracellular Organelles

## Key findings

- The nucleolus is the site of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) transcription, pre-RNA processing, and ribosome subunit assembly
- Cells with high protein synthesis, such as cancer cells and liver cells, tend to have more nucleoli

## Ribosome sizes

| Cell type | Whole ribosome | Small subunit | Large subunit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prokaryotic | 70S | 30S | 50S |
| Eukaryotic | 80S | 40S | 60S |

## Why it matters

Several antibiotics act on the prokaryotic subunits. Aminoglycosides and tetracyclines bind the 30S subunit, while macrolides, clindamycin, and linezolid bind the 50S subunit.

> Prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S (30S + 50S); eukaryotic ribosomes are 80S (40S + 60S).

Library: Lysosomes vs. peroxisomes
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Cell Structure and Intracellular Organelles

## Key findings

| Feature | Lysosomes | Peroxisomes |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Golgi body | Not from the Golgi body |
| Where enzymes are made | Rough ER, then passed to the Golgi | Free ribosomes (like mitochondria) |

- Lysosomes are highly acidic
- Peroxisomes carry out beta oxidation of long-chain fatty acids and alpha oxidation of branched-chain fatty acids
- Peroxisomal catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water

## Related disorders

| Disorder | Defect | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| I-cell disease | NAG-1 phosphotransferase (Golgi) | Enzymes aren't recognized by the M6P receptor, so lysosomes are empty |
| Zellweger syndrome | Peroxisomes | Very-long-chain and branched-chain fatty acids accumulate |
| Refsum disease | Alpha oxidation | Phytanic acid can't be metabolized |
| Adrenoleukodystrophy | Peroxisomal acyl-CoA synthetase | Very-long-chain fatty acids build up; X-linked recessive |

> Lysosomal enzymes are made on the rough ER and sent through the Golgi; peroxisomal enzymes are made on free ribosomes.

## Key findings

- In non-dividing cells, centrioles attach to the inner side of the plasma membrane to form a basal body
- A basal body can form the base of a flagellum or a beating (motile) cilium
- In all other cells, the basal body forms a nonmotile primary cilium

> In most cells, the basal body forms a nonmotile primary cilium.

## Heteroplasmy

The severity of a mitochondrial mutation changes from one child to another in the same family.

## Microsomal fraction

Vesicles derived from the smooth and rough ER, the Golgi body, and the plasma membrane that form during centrifugation.

> Heteroplasmy explains why siblings with the same mitochondrial mutation can be affected to different degrees.

## Pathway

Alcohol → (alcohol dehydrogenase) → acetaldehyde → (aldehyde dehydrogenase) → acetic acid

## Key findings

| Drug or condition | Target | Type | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disulfiram | Aldehyde dehydrogenase | Irreversible inhibitor | Acetaldehyde builds up and worsens hangover symptoms; used to treat alcoholism |

> Disulfiram blocks aldehyde dehydrogenase, so acetaldehyde accumulates.

Library: Formins and Arp2/3: linear vs. branched actin
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Actin

## Key findings

| Nucleator | Filament | Structures |
|---|---|---|
| Formins | Linear | Microvilli, filopodia |
| Arp2/3 complex | Branched at a 70-degree angle | Lamellipodia |

## Formin structures

- Microvilli are stable, uniform structures. Bundling proteins cross-link the parallel filaments, a stable cap at the plus end stops growth, and the minus end attaches to the terminal web of the cortical actin cytoskeleton
- Filopodia grow and retract rapidly because their cap is less stable. Their filaments are cross-linked by fascin

## Arp2/3 structures

- Lamellipodia can contract using myosin. Actomyosin contraction creates shape changes, as in epithelial wound healing and folding
- ActA is a bacterial scaffold protein that induces rapid Arp2/3-based assembly

## Pathogens

Some bacterial and fungal pathogens hijack the cortical actin cytoskeleton, using a comet tail of rapid assembly behind them for propulsion. Listeria moves with actin rockets.

> Formins make linear filaments; the Arp2/3 complex starts branches at a 70-degree angle.

Library: Actin toxins: phalloidin, jasplakinolide, and cytochalasin D
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Actin

## Key findings

| Toxin | Source | Effect on actin |
|---|---|---|
| Phalloidin | Death cap mushrooms | Binds three monomers and blocks both assembly and disassembly: hyperstabilizes subunits and blocks ADP release |
| Jasplakinolide | Sea sponges | Blocks disassembly, so filaments only grow ("actin goes on a journey") |
| Cytochalasin D | Fungi | Caps the plus end, so filaments shorten until they're gone |

## Death cap mushroom poisoning

- The toxicity comes from alpha-amanitin, which inhibits RNA polymerase II and stops mRNA synthesis, causing massive hepatocyte death
- The liver is the main target; listed symptoms include extreme hunger, jaundice, and seizures or coma

> Death cap toxicity comes from alpha-amanitin, which inhibits RNA polymerase II.

Library: Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Actin

## Mechanism

Muscular dystrophy comes from mutations in the protein complex that secures contracting muscle cells to the extracellular matrix. Dystrophin helps attach actin fibers to the ECM by anchoring to the cortical actin cytoskeleton.

## Duchenne muscular dystrophy

- Severe
- Mutations in the actin-complex binding domains of dystrophin
- Frameshift or nonsense mutations leave dystrophin truncated or absent
- X-linked recessive
- Progressive muscle weakness and calf pseudohypertrophy

## Becker muscular dystrophy

- Mutations in the spacer domains of dystrophin
- These don't directly disrupt protein-protein interactions

> Duchenne mutations hit dystrophin's actin-binding domains; Becker mutations hit the spacer domains.

Library: Microtubule structure and dynamic instability
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Cytoskeleton and Microtubules

## Key findings

- The basic unit is a dimer of alpha and beta tubulin. Beta is at the plus end and alpha is at the minus end
- Each microtubule has 13 protofilaments
- Gamma tubulin sits on the MTOC and helps start microtubule formation
- Microtubule-associated proteins such as tau can modify tubulin tails

## Dynamic instability

- GTP-bound tubulin: growth (rescue)
- GTP hydrolysis: shrinkage (catastrophe)
- The GTP cap is on the plus (beta) end

## Drug link

Paclitaxel stabilizes microtubules so spindle fibers can't pull apart, arresting cells in M phase. Docetaxel, vincristine, and vinblastine are also grouped as M-phase microtubule inhibitors.

> GTP-bound tubulin grows (rescue); GTP hydrolysis leads to shrinkage (catastrophe).

Library: Tau and Alzheimer disease
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Cytoskeleton and Microtubules

## Key findings

- Tau is a microtubule-associated protein
- Neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) are aggregates of tau
- Hyperphosphorylated tau no longer binds microtubules and forms aggregates
- It's still an open question whether the disease comes from losing tau's normal function or from the toxicity of the aggregates

> Neurofibrillary tangles in Alzheimer disease are aggregates of hyperphosphorylated tau.

Library: Kinesin and dynein motors

## Kinesin

- Two polypeptide heads
- Needs cargo to be activated, which keeps it from wasting ATP

## Dynein

- Two types: cytoplasmic and axonemal
- Much larger than kinesin
- Can't function by itself; it needs activators, cargo, and cargo adaptors
- Activators: dynactin and Lis1

## Motor disorders

| Disorder | Defect | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Perry syndrome | Dynactin 1 (DCTN1) | Parkinson-type symptoms that don't respond to medication; tremor, weight loss, depression; autosomal dominant |
| Spinal muscular atrophy and ALS | Bicaudal-D, a dynein cargo adaptor | Dynein becomes hyperactive |
| Lissencephaly | Lis1 (Dcx in the X-linked form) | Neurons fail to migrate to the outer cortex; smooth brain |

> Bicaudal-D mutations make dynein hyperactive.

Library: Motile vs. nonmotile cilia

## Key findings

| Feature | Motile cilia | Nonmotile (primary) cilia |
|---|---|---|
| Arrangement | 9+2 | 9+0 |

Library: SGLT1 and SGLT2
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Membrane Transport

## Key findings

| Feature | SGLT1 | SGLT2 |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Intestine | Kidney |
| Substrates | D-glucose and D-galactose | Glucose only |
| Affinity | Higher than SGLT2 | Lower than SGLT1 |

- Active transport happens on the apical side (external milieu); passive transport happens on the basolateral side (internal milieu)
- Sodium is pumped out on the basolateral side, and the symporter brings sugar in with sodium as sodium moves down its concentration gradient

## Clinical links

- Glucose-galactose malabsorption: an SGLT1 defect leaves children unable to absorb glucose and galactose, causing osmotic diarrhea
- SGLT2 inhibitors (phlorizin analogs such as Jardiance, Farxiga, and Invokana) treat type 2 diabetes. Glucose isn't reabsorbed into the bloodstream, and the extra water lost with it can cause dehydration. They also raise the chance of yeast infections

> SGLT2 inhibitors make the kidney spill glucose, which brings dehydration and yeast infections as side effects.

## Key findings

| Disorder | Defect | Key findings |
|---|---|---|
| GLUT1 deficiency syndrome | GLUT1 | Seizures, developmental delay, hypotonia, low CSF glucose (hypoglycorrhachia), because the blood-brain barrier depends on GLUT1 |

## FDG imaging

18FDG is a substrate of all GLUTs and enters every cell in the body. It has to be injected because the GI tract absorbs glucose through SGLTs. GLUT1 is overexpressed in cancer cells.

> GLUT1 deficiency presents with seizures and developmental delay because the brain depends on GLUT1.

Library: FPHHI and sulfonylureas
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Membrane Transport

## Mechanism

In pancreatic beta cells, restricting potassium exit keeps the membrane more positive. Calcium channels then open, and insulin is released.

## Key findings

- Familial persistent hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia of infancy (FPHHI): mutations restrict K+ exit from beta cells, so they release too much insulin. Autosomal recessive
- Mutations that increase K+ release have the opposite effect and lower insulin levels
- Sulfonylureas work by essentially the same mechanism as FPHHI. They block K+ exit so calcium channels open and insulin is released, which is how they treat type 2 diabetes

> Sulfonylureas mimic FPHHI: blocking K+ exit from beta cells triggers insulin release.

Library: Amino acid transport disorders
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Membrane Transport

## Key findings

Amino acids are moved by secondary active transporters, and most individual amino acids are carried by more than one transporter.

| Disorder | Defect | Key findings |
|---|---|---|
| Hartnup disease | Intestinal and renal transport of neutral amino acids | More neutral amino acids lost in urine; tryptophan loss causes niacin deficiency; cerebellar ataxia; autosomal recessive |
| Cystinuria | Luminal absorption of cationic amino acids and cystine (COLA: cystine, ornithine, lysine, arginine) | Positive cyanide-nitroprusside test; yellow, waxy kidney stones; urine smells like rotten eggs; autosomal recessive |
| Cystinosis | Lysosomal transporter that moves cystine out of the lysosome | Cystine accumulates in lysosomes of nearly every organ; eventually leads to Fanconi syndrome |

> Cystinuria is a gut and kidney transport defect; cystinosis is a lysosomal transport defect. They're easy to confuse.

Library: Drug targets, occupancy, and KD
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Pharmacodynamics

## Drug targets

- Small-molecule drugs act on GPCRs, enzymes, ligand- and voltage-gated ion channels, transcription factors, nuclear receptors, transport and structural proteins, and RNA
- GPCRs account for about 12% of targets and ion channels about 19%
- Biologics include monoclonal antibodies, growth factors, and allergens

## Occupancy

- Drugs work by occupying their target, and binding is reversible
- KD is the concentration of drug needed to bind half of the targets

> KD is the drug concentration that occupies half of the targets.

Library: Agonists, antagonists, and inverse agonists
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Pharmacodynamics

## Two-state model

GPCRs and ion channels exist in inactive and active forms, and drugs shift the equilibrium one way or the other.

| Ligand | Effect |
|---|---|
| Full agonist | Shifts toward the active conformation |
| Partial agonist | Shifts partially toward the active conformation |
| Competitive antagonist | Occupies the site and competes with the agonist for binding; higher KD |
| Inverse agonist | Shifts toward the inactive conformation |

## Look-alikes

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Potency | The concentration of drug needed to bind the target (KD) |
| Efficacy | The maximal physiological effect |

> An inverse agonist shifts the receptor toward the inactive state; a competitive antagonist only occupies the site.

Library: Biased and allosteric ligands
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Pharmacodynamics

## Ligand bias

- Ligand bias means favoring one state over another, a more complex model than the two-state model
- Many receptors have multiple signal transduction pathways, and some ligands favor one pathway over another. Those ligands are "biased" toward that pathway

## Allosteric ligands

- Allosteric ligands bind a site other than the one occupied by the natural ligand (the orthosteric site)
- They can be positive or negative allosteric modulators
- They fine-tune a response by modulating affinity, efficacy, and agonism

> Allosteric ligands bind away from the orthosteric site and fine-tune the response.

Library: Therapeutic index
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Pharmacodynamics

## Key findings

- Therapeutic index = TD50 / ED50 (toxic dose / effective dose)
- A larger number is better, because the effective and toxic doses are farther apart and less likely to overlap
- Vancomycin is an example of a drug with a narrow therapeutic index that requires drug monitoring

> A larger therapeutic index means a wider gap between the effective and toxic doses.

Library: cAMP and IP3/calcium pathways
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Cellular Signaling

## AC/cAMP/PKA pathway

Gαs → adenylyl cyclase → cyclic AMP (second messenger) → PKA

## PLC/IP3/calcium pathway

1. Gαq activates phospholipase C (PLC)
2. PLC splits PIP2 into DAG and IP3
3. IP3 activates calcium channels
4. Calcium activates calmodulins

## Clinical link

Bacillus anthracis and Bordetella pertussis share a mechanism: both raise cAMP levels, which causes edema.

> Phospholipase C splits PIP2 into DAG and IP3.

Library: Smooth muscle contraction signaling
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Cellular Signaling

## Contraction

- Gα12/13 → RhoA-GTP → ROCK → inhibits myosin light chain phosphatase (MLCP), which normally allows relaxation
- Gαq → IP3 → calcium release → activates myosin light chain kinase (MLCK)

## Relaxation

- Nitric oxide → cGMP → PKG1, which activates MLC phosphatase
- PKG2 inhibits calcium release

> Contraction comes from turning on MLCK (Gαq, calcium) or turning off MLCP (Gα12/13, RhoA, ROCK).

Library: Nitric oxide, cGMP, and PDE-5
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Cellular Signaling

## Pathway

Nitric oxide (NO) → soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) → cGMP → PKG1 and PKG2

## Key findings

- PKG1 activates MLC phosphatase, causing relaxation
- PKG2 inhibits calcium release, also causing relaxation
- PDE-5 converts cGMP into GMP, ending the signal. Drugs are made to block PDE-5

> PDE-5 breaks down cGMP, so blocking it prolongs NO-driven relaxation.

Library: Growth factor signaling: RAS-MAPK, PI3K-AKT, and Wnt
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Cellular Signaling

## RAS-MAPK pathway

Receptor tyrosine kinase is phosphorylated → Grb2 (scaffolding protein) → SOS (guanine nucleotide exchange protein) → RAS → RAF → MEK → ERK → transcription factors

## PI3K-AKT pathway

- The survival pathway
- PI3K converts PIP2 to PIP3 (second messenger), which binds and activates AKT
- Drives survival, protein synthesis, and metabolism
- PTEN is the phosphatase that opposes PI3K

## Wnt/beta-catenin pathway

Beta-catenin is normally destroyed by the destruction complex. When Wnt binds, beta-catenin is no longer degraded.

## Clinical link

HER2 amplification signals along the MEK and AKT pathways and increases cell proliferation. It's amplified in about 30% of breast cancers.

> PTEN is the phosphatase that opposes PI3K in the survival pathway.

Library: Drug absorption and metabolism
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Pharmacokinetics

## Absorption

- Absorption is getting a drug into circulation
- Permeation through the lipid bilayer depends on size, hydrophobicity, and the lipid-water coefficient
- P-glycoprotein (P-GP) pumps foreign substances, including drugs, out of cells

## Metabolism

- Cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes carry out phase I reactions in drug metabolism
- They usually add a hydroxyl group, making the drug more hydrophilic so the kidneys can excrete it

## Clinical links

- Rifampin is a CYP inducer, and fluconazole inhibits CYP450
- Barbiturates, alcohol, and sulfonamides induce P450, which can precipitate attacks of acute intermittent porphyria

> CYP450 phase I reactions usually add a hydroxyl group so the drug can be excreted by the kidneys.

Library: Bioavailability, volume of distribution, and clearance
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Pharmacokinetics

## Key findings

| Parameter | Meaning | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Bioavailability (F) | How much of the drug reaches systemic circulation | F = AUC oral / AUC IV |
| Volume of distribution (Vd) | The apparent volume in which a quantity of drug is distributed | Vd (mL) = amount (mg) / concentration (mg/mL) |
| Clearance (Cl) | Volume of plasma cleared of a drug per unit of time | Not given as a formula |

> Bioavailability compares the oral AUC to the IV AUC.

Library: Loading and maintenance doses
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Pharmacokinetics

## Key findings

- Loading dose = Vd × target concentration
- Maintenance dose = Cl × target concentration, or (Cl × target concentration) / F when bioavailability is factored in

> The loading dose depends on volume of distribution; the maintenance dose depends on clearance.

Library: Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 2: Cellular, Molecular, and Genetic Foundations / Patterns of Inheritance

## Key findings

- p is the frequency of allele A, and q is the frequency of allele a
- p² is the frequency of AA
- 2pq is the frequency of Aa
- q² is the frequency of aa

> 2pq is the frequency of heterozygotes (Aa).
