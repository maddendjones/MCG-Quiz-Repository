## Key findings

A dry tap on marrow aspiration means the marrow space is occupied or fibrosed.

| Entity | Mechanism | Other clues |
|---|---|---|
| Primary myelofibrosis | Fibrosis from megakaryocyte-derived cytokines; confirmed with reticulin stain | Teardrop cells, massive splenomegaly |

Library: Sites of hematopoiesis

## Key findings

| Site | Window | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Yolk sac | Day 18-19 to week 8 | Primitive wave in "blood islands": outer cells become endothelium, inner cells become the first nucleated red cells with embryonic hemoglobin |
| AGM region | Weeks 4-6 | The first true self-renewing HSCs bud from hemogenic endothelium of the dorsal aorta |
| Liver | Week 6 to birth | Dominant source through mid-gestation |
| Spleen | Weeks 10-28 | Transient secondary site, with the thymus; shuts down around birth |
| Bone marrow | Week 18 to adult | Permanent site; the only significant source by birth |

- Memory hook: Young Liver Synthesizes Blood
- The lecture slides give a more granular timeline, with marrow starting in the clavicle around week 10 and dominant by week 28

## Adult marrow

- Red marrow (active) fills all bones in infants but is limited in adults to the axial skeleton (skull, sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis) and the proximal humerus and femur
- Yellow marrow (fat) replaces red marrow in the limb long bones with age, and it can revert to red marrow under extreme stress such as severe bleeding or chronic hemolysis

> The AGM region, not the yolk sac, is where the first true self-renewing HSCs arise.

## Marrow compartments

| Compartment | Contents |
|---|---|
| Endosteum | Bone-lining surface with osteoblasts; quiescent HSCs are held here, protected from mutation and chemotherapy |
| Hematopoietic cords | Developing cells in stromal meshwork; erythroblastic islands around a central macrophage; megakaryocytes against the sinusoid wall |
| Sinusoids | Thin-walled vessels draining to the central vein; mature cells cross here into circulation |

- Normal adult myeloid:erythroid ratio is 2:1 to 4:1
- A left shift means immature granulocytes are being released early under demand

## The hematopoietic stem cell

- Self-renewal, multipotency, quiescence, and rarity (about 1 in 10,000 to 100,000 nucleated marrow cells)
- Can't be identified by morphology, only by surface markers: CD34+, CD38-, lineage-negative
- CD34 is used clinically to select and count stem cell grafts
- G-CSF mobilizes HSCs out of the marrow niche into the blood so they can be collected by apheresis instead of a surgical marrow harvest

> HSCs are CD34+ CD38- Lin-, and G-CSF mobilizes them into blood for collection.

## Key findings

HSC → multipotent progenitor (MPP) → two progenitor lines:

| Progenitor | Gives rise to |
|---|---|
| Common myeloid progenitor (CMP) → granulocyte-monocyte progenitor (GMP) | Neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, monocytes and macrophages |
| Common myeloid progenitor (CMP) → megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitor (MEP) | Red cells (via EPO); megakaryocytes and platelets (via TPO) |
| Common lymphoid progenitor (CLP) | B cells (mature in marrow), T cells (mature in thymus), NK cells (no thymic education) |

## Older names still seen on boards

- CFU-GEMM: multipotent myeloid colony-forming unit
- BFU-E and CFU-E: early and late erythroid progenitors

> EPO drives the erythroid line and TPO drives megakaryocytes, both from the MEP.

Library: B and T cell origin and maturation
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hematopoiesis and the Lymphoid System

## Lymphoid organs

| Type | Organs | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Primary ("schools") | Bone marrow, thymus | Where lymphocytes are born and educated; they don't fight infection |
| Secondary ("battlefields") | Lymph nodes (filter lymph), spleen (filters blood), MALT (tonsils, Peyer's patches, adenoids) | Where mature lymphocytes meet antigen |

## B cells

- Arise and mature in the marrow: pro-B → pre-B → immature B → mature naive B
- The heavy chain rearranges first and is tested at the pre-B receptor checkpoint, then the light chain rearranges, giving surface IgM
- Central tolerance: B cells that bind self too strongly undergo receptor editing (a new light chain) or are deleted
- One clone, one specificity; B cells need T cell help; plasma cells are terminally differentiated B cells built to secrete antibody

## T cells

- Recognize antigen only as processed peptide on MHC from an antigen-presenting cell, never in native form
- Helper T cells coordinate the response; cytotoxic T cells kill infected cells
- Precursors leave the marrow early and finish maturing in the thymus

> B cells mature in the marrow; T cells are born in the marrow but mature in the thymus.

## Zones

| Zone | Contents |
|---|---|
| Cortex (B cell zone) | Primary follicles (dormant B cells) and secondary follicles with pale germinal centers, where B cells proliferate, isotype switch, and undergo affinity maturation |
| Paracortex (T cell zone) | T cells and high endothelial venules (HEVs), where lymphocytes leave the blood |
| Medulla | Medullary cords (plasma cells and macrophages) and medullary sinuses carrying lymph to the exit |

## Circulation

- Lymph enters through several afferent lymphatics → subcapsular sinus → trabecular sinus → medullary sinus
- Lymphocytes enter from blood through HEVs in the paracortex
- Everything leaves through one efferent lymphatic at the hilum

## Clinical links

- DiGeorge syndrome (thymic aplasia): no T cells, so the paracortex is underdeveloped and looks empty
- Removing lymph nodes (such as axillary nodes in mastectomy) can cause lymphedema of the limb

> The paracortex is the T cell zone, which is empty in DiGeorge syndrome.

Library: GALT and tonsils

## GALT

- Not encapsulated; antigen crosses the epithelium directly, so there are no afferent lymphatics
- Peyer's patches are aggregated follicles in the ileal mucosa and submucosa
- M (microfold) cells in the overlying epithelium sample antigen from the gut lumen
- Output: dimeric IgA with a secretory component that protects it from proteolysis

## Tonsils (Waldeyer's ring)

| Tonsil | Location | Epithelium | Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Palatine | Paired, oropharynx | Stratified squamous | Deep crypts that trap bacteria |
| Pharyngeal (adenoid) | Single midline mass, roof of nasopharynx | Respiratory (pseudostratified ciliated columnar) | Folds, not crypts |
| Lingual | Posterior third of tongue | Stratified squamous | |

- The adenoid is largest in childhood; adenoid hypertrophy can block the nasopharynx and eustachian tube, causing mouth breathing and recurrent otitis media

> M cells in Peyer's patches sample gut antigen, and the output is secretory IgA.

Library: Spleen structure and function
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hematopoiesis and the Lymphoid System

## Location

Left hypochondrium, deep to ribs 9-11, intraperitoneal; the hilum faces the stomach and touches the pancreatic tail.

## Structure

| Region | Share | Contents and role |
|---|---|---|
| Red pulp | 75% | Mechanical filtering of red cells. Splenic cords (of Billroth) trap old or stiff cells for macrophages; sinusoids have barrel-hoop basement membranes |
| White pulp | 25% | Immune response. PALS (periarteriolar lymphatic sheath of T cells around the central artery), B cell follicles, and the marginal zone (macrophages and specialized B cells that trap polysaccharide antigens) |

## Red cell circulation

Red cells enter the open circulation of the red pulp and must squeeze through 1-3 μm slits between the stave cells of the sinus wall to get back into circulation. Rigid cells, such as spherocytes and sickled cells, fail this deformability test and are eaten by red pulp macrophages. This is why splenectomy helps in hereditary spherocytosis and why extravascular hemolysis happens in the spleen.

> Red cells must squeeze through 1-3 μm slits in the splenic sinuses, and rigid cells fail.

Library: Asplenia and Howell-Jolly bodies
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hematopoiesis and the Lymphoid System

## Key findings

- The spleen makes the IgM and opsonins needed to clear encapsulated organisms: S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, N. meningitidis
- Asplenic patients carry a lifelong risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI)
- Howell-Jolly bodies are nuclear remnants the spleen normally removes, so seeing them means no spleen or a nonfunctioning one
- Functional autosplenectomy from sickle cell disease gives the same infection profile
- Asplenia is the single greatest severity risk factor for babesiosis

## Management

- Vaccinate against encapsulated organisms before an elective splenectomy
- Fever in an asplenic patient is a medical emergency

## Therapeutic splenectomy

Hereditary spherocytosis, warm AIHA, and hypersplenism in thalassemia.

> Howell-Jolly bodies mean asplenia, and fever in an asplenic patient is an emergency.

Library: Thymus structure and T cell selection
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hematopoiesis and the Lymphoid System

## Key findings

The thymus sits in the anterior mediastinum and comes from the third pharyngeal pouch (Thymus = Third).

| Region | Appearance | Event |
|---|---|---|
| Cortex | Dark, immature thymocytes | Positive selection: cells that recognize self-MHC survive; the rest die by apoptosis |
| Medulla | Pale, mature T cells | Negative selection: cells that attack self-antigen too strongly die |

- AIRE (autoimmune regulator) makes medullary epithelial cells express tissue-restricted self-antigens (from pancreas, thyroid, and so on), so reactive T cells are deleted before leaving
- Hassall's corpuscles are onion-skin layers of epithelial reticular cells found only in the medulla; concentric pink swirls on a slide mean thymus
- About 98% of developing thymocytes die during selection

## Involution

The thymus is largest in childhood and is replaced by fat after puberty, with T cell output falling steeply. An adult thymus can still regenerate a T cell repertoire when needed, such as after bone marrow transplant.

## Look-alike

Hassall's corpuscles vs. germinal centers: both look pale and organized at low power, but only Hassall's corpuscles show onion-skin layering.

> Positive selection happens in the thymic cortex; negative selection, driven by AIRE, happens in the medulla.

Library: Heme structure and heme proteins
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Heme Synthesis and Porphyria

## Structure

- Heme is a porphyrin (four pyrrole rings linked by methine bridges) holding an iron atom (Fe²⁺)
- Planar, with pyrrole nitrogens facing inward to bind iron
- Amphipathic: methyl and vinyl groups on the hydrophobic side, propionate groups on the hydrophilic side, so it fits into protein pockets

## Heme proteins

| Protein | Function | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Hemoglobin | Oxygen transport | Four heme groups |
| Myoglobin | Oxygen storage in muscle | One chain, one heme |
| Cytochromes | Electron transport chain | ATP production |
| Cytochrome P450 | Drug metabolism | Liver smooth ER |
| Catalase | Breaks down hydrogen peroxide | Protects against oxidative damage |
| Nitric oxide synthase | Makes nitric oxide | Vasodilation signaling |
| Tryptophan pyrrolase | Tryptophan breakdown | Serotonin pathway |

- Heme is red, and porphyria intermediates can turn urine port-wine red or tea-colored

> Heme is a porphyrin ring holding ferrous iron.

Library: Heme synthesis pathway
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Heme Synthesis and Porphyria

The pathway has eight enzymes: it starts in the mitochondria, moves to the cytosol, and finishes in the mitochondria.

## Key steps

| Step | Location | Enzyme | Cofactor or inhibitor | Deficiency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Succinyl-CoA + glycine → delta-ALA | Mitochondria | ALA synthase (rate-limiting) | Vitamin B6 | Sideroblastic anemia (B6 deficiency, as with isoniazid) |
| 2 delta-ALA → porphobilinogen | Cytosol | ALA dehydratase | Zinc; very sensitive to lead | Lead raises ALA without raising PBG |
| PBG → hydroxymethylbilane | Cytosol | PBG deaminase (HMB synthase) | | Acute intermittent porphyria |
| Uroporphyrinogen III → coproporphyrinogen III | Cytosol | Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase (UROD) | | Porphyria cutanea tarda |
| Protoporphyrin IX + Fe²⁺ → heme | Mitochondria | Ferrochelatase | Inhibited by lead | Erythropoietic protoporphyria |

## Where symptoms come from

- Early intermediates (ALA, PBG) are neurotoxic: abdominal pain, neuropathy, psychiatric symptoms
- Later intermediates (porphyrins) are phototoxic: blisters and photosensitivity

> ALA synthase is the rate-limiting step and needs vitamin B6.

Library: Regulation of heme synthesis: ALAS1 vs. ALAS2
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Heme Synthesis and Porphyria

## Key findings

| Feature | Liver (ALAS1) | Bone marrow (ALAS2) |
|---|---|---|
| Heme made for | Cytochrome P450 | Hemoglobin (about 85% of body heme) |
| Regulation | Negative feedback: heme acts as a co-repressor, blocking ALAS1 transcription and its transport into mitochondria | Not controlled by heme feedback; stimulated by EPO; iron controls translation through an iron response element (low iron blocks it) |

## The porphyria attack

1. Barbiturates, alcohol, or sulfonamides induce cytochrome P450
2. P450 consumes free heme
3. Low heme releases the brake on ALAS1, and its activity surges
4. With a downstream block (as in AIP), ALA and PBG build up

> P450-inducing drugs use up hepatic heme, which releases ALAS1 and triggers porphyria attacks.

Library: Acute intermittent porphyria
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Heme Synthesis and Porphyria

## Key findings

- PBG deaminase deficiency; autosomal dominant
- Delta-ALA and PBG (both neurotoxic) accumulate
- No photosensitivity, because the block is upstream of the phototoxic porphyrins
- Diagnosis: high urinary PBG during an attack

## The 5 P's

1. Painful abdomen, severe and unexplained
2. Port-wine urine (PBG oxidizes to dark red in light)
3. Polyneuropathy
4. Psychological disturbances (hallucinations, anxiety)
5. Precipitated by drugs (P450 inducers), alcohol, or starvation

## Treatment

1. Stop the precipitating drug
2. Glucose loading (high-carbohydrate diet or IV dextrose), which inhibits ALAS expression
3. IV hemin, which supplies heme and restores negative feedback on ALAS1

Neither fixes the enzyme defect; both stop overproduction of the toxic intermediates. Phlebotomy is wrong for AIP.

> Treat an AIP attack with IV hemin and glucose to shut down ALAS1.

Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Heme Synthesis and Porphyria

## Key findings

| Feature | Porphyria cutanea tarda (PCT) | Erythropoietic protoporphyria (EPP) |
|---|---|---|
| Enzyme | Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase | Ferrochelatase; autosomal dominant |
| Accumulates | Uroporphyrin, deposited in skin | Protoporphyrin IX |
| Skin | Blistering photosensitivity with hyperpigmentation on sun-exposed hands and face | Immediate burning pain on sun exposure, then swelling and redness; no blisters |
| Labs | High urinary uroporphyrin; tea-colored urine | High RBC protoporphyrin |
| Triggers | Alcohol (inhibits UROD), hepatitis C, HIV, estrogen, iron overload | |
| Treatment | Avoid sun and alcohol; phlebotomy to remove iron; low-dose chloroquine | Beta-carotene (scavenges free radicals) and sun avoidance |

- PCT is the most common porphyria
- In a vignette, blistering vs. neurovisceral symptoms decides between PCT and AIP, and phlebotomy is right for PCT but wrong for AIP

> PCT blisters; EPP burns without blisters.

Library: Lead poisoning
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Heme Synthesis and Porphyria

## Mechanism

Lead inhibits ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase, so delta-ALA (neurotoxic) and red cell protoporphyrin rise.

## LEAD

- Lead lines on the gums (Burton lines) and at the metaphyses of long bones
- Encephalopathy (headache, memory loss) and erythrocyte basophilic stippling
- Abdominal colic and anemia (microcytic, sideroblastic)
- Drops: wrist drop and foot drop from neuropathy

## Labs

- Microcytic anemia
- Basophilic stippling: ribosomal aggregates, because lead blocks rRNA degradation
- Ringed sideroblasts: iron trapped in mitochondria

## Treatment

Remove the exposure and give chelation.

> Basophilic stippling in a microcytic anemia points to lead poisoning.

Library: Red cell production: iron, B12, folate, and EPO
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Anemia

## Ingredients

Iron (the core of heme), vitamin B12 and folate (for DNA synthesis), and erythropoietin (the growth signal).

## Iron absorption and transport

1. Non-heme iron (Fe³⁺) is reduced to Fe²⁺ by duodenal cytochrome B
2. It enters the enterocyte through DMT1 (divalent metal transporter 1)
3. It leaves into the blood through ferroportin, which is controlled by hepcidin from the liver
4. In blood, iron (Fe³⁺) rides on transferrin to the marrow

- High hepcidin, as in chronic inflammation, closes ferroportin and traps iron in cells

## B12 and folate

- B12 needs intrinsic factor from gastric parietal cells for absorption in the terminal ileum
- Deficiency of either causes nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony (the nucleus lags behind the cytoplasm), leading to macrocytosis

> Hepcidin controls iron release through ferroportin.

Library: Defining and classifying anemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Anemia

## Definition (WHO)

| Population | Hemoglobin |
|---|---|
| Men | Under 13.0 g/dL |
| Women | Under 12.0 g/dL |
| Pregnant women | Under 11.0 g/dL (physiologic plasma volume expansion) |

## Classification

| Size | Causes |
|---|---|
| Microcytic (MCV under 80) | TAILS: thalassemia, anemia of chronic disease, iron deficiency, lead poisoning, sideroblastic anemia |
| Normocytic, high reticulocytes | Hemolysis: hereditary spherocytosis, G6PD deficiency, sickle cell, AIHA, MAHA |
| Normocytic, low reticulocytes | Aplastic anemia, chronic kidney disease |
| Macrocytic (MCV over 100), megaloblastic | B12 or folate deficiency; hypersegmented neutrophils |
| Macrocytic, non-megaloblastic | Alcoholism, liver disease, hypothyroidism; no hypersegmented neutrophils |

## Why microcytic anemias are small

Hemoglobin is heme plus globin, and heme is iron plus protoporphyrin. A shortage of any of these makes the red cell divide an extra time to keep its hemoglobin concentration, so it ends up smaller.

> The microcytic anemias are TAILS: thalassemia, ACD, iron deficiency, lead, sideroblastic.

Library: Systematic approach to anemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Anemia

## Step 1: reticulocyte count

| Corrected reticulocyte count | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Over 2% | The marrow is working; the problem is destruction (hemolysis) or blood loss |
| Under 2% | The marrow is failing; it's a production problem |

## Step 2: MCV

- Microcytic (under 80 fL): usually a hemoglobin synthesis defect
- Normocytic (80-100 fL): marrow failure or mixed causes
- Macrocytic (over 100 fL): DNA synthesis defect or membrane problem

## Step 3: targeted testing

- Microcytic: iron studies
- Macrocytic: B12, folate, and methylmalonic acid
- High reticulocytes: hemolysis labs and smear

## Signs that point to a cause

| Sign | Points toward |
|---|---|
| Pallor, tachycardia | Any anemia |
| Jaundice | Hemolysis |
| Koilonychia (spoon nails) | Iron deficiency (specific) |
| Pica, especially ice | Iron deficiency |
| Glossitis (smooth, beefy red tongue) | B12, folate, or iron deficiency |
| Paresthesias, gait abnormality | B12 deficiency (subacute combined degeneration), specific |

> Classify first with the reticulocyte count, then the MCV, before ordering iron studies.

## Key findings

| Condition | MCV | Serum iron | Ferritin | TIBC | Saturation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Iron deficiency | Low | Low | Low (most sensitive early marker) | High | Low |
| Anemia of chronic disease | Low or normal | Low | High | Low | Low |
| Thalassemia | Very low | Normal | Normal or high | Normal | Normal |
| Sideroblastic anemia | Low | High | High | Normal or low | High |

## The logic of TIBC

TIBC is a stand-in for transferrin. In iron deficiency the liver makes more transferrin to catch any available iron, so TIBC rises. In anemia of chronic disease ferritin is already high, so the liver makes less transferrin and TIBC falls. This opposite behavior is the cleanest way to separate the two.

## Thalassemia trait

Target cells with normal or high iron and ferritin separate thalassemia from iron deficiency. The Mentzer index (MCV / RBC count) is under 13 in thalassemia and over 13 in iron deficiency, and hemoglobin electrophoresis makes the diagnosis.

> TIBC is high in iron deficiency and low in anemia of chronic disease.

Library: Iron deficiency anemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Anemia

## Causes

Chronic blood loss (GI bleeding, heavy menstrual bleeding) or poor intake and malabsorption (celiac disease).

## Key findings

- Microcytic, hypochromic cells with central pallor over one-third of the cell
- Pica (especially ice) and koilonychia (spoon nails)
- Low iron, low ferritin, high TIBC, low saturation

## Treatment

- Oral ferrous sulfate 325 mg
- Take it with vitamin C, since an acidic environment helps absorption
- Don't take it with milk or antacids, since calcium blocks absorption

> Take oral iron with vitamin C, not with milk or antacids.

Library: Anemia of chronic disease
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Anemia

## Mechanism

Chronic inflammation (rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, cancer) raises IL-6, which increases hepcidin. Hepcidin degrades ferroportin, locking iron inside macrophages and blocking gut absorption. Iron is present but hidden from red cell precursors, a functional iron deficiency.

## Key findings

- Microcytic or normocytic
- Low serum iron, high ferritin, low TIBC, low saturation

## Treatment

Treat the underlying inflammatory condition; iron supplements are largely ineffective while hepcidin is high.

> Iron is trapped in macrophages by hepcidin, so oral iron doesn't help.

Library: Sideroblastic anemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Anemia

## Mechanism

A defect in protoporphyrin synthesis leaves iron accumulating in the mitochondria of erythroid precursors.

## Causes

| Cause | How |
|---|---|
| Alcohol | Mitochondrial poison |
| Lead | Inhibits ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase |
| Isoniazid | Causes B6 deficiency, and B6 is the ALA synthase cofactor |

## Key findings

- High serum iron and ferritin
- Ringed sideroblasts on Prussian blue stain of the marrow

## Look-alike

Ring sideroblasts also appear in MDS. In an older patient with cytopenias and dysplasia in other lines, they point to MDS rather than lead or B6 deficiency.

> Ringed sideroblasts with high iron point to sideroblastic anemia from alcohol, lead, or isoniazid.

Library: B12 vs. folate deficiency
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Anemia

## Shared findings

Megaloblastic anemia (MCV over 100), hypersegmented neutrophils (over 5 lobes), and high homocysteine.

## Key differences

| Feature | B12 deficiency | Folate deficiency |
|---|---|---|
| Methylmalonic acid | High (only B12 converts methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA) | Normal |
| Neurologic symptoms | Yes: subacute combined degeneration, paresthesias, gait problems | No |
| Causes | Pernicious anemia, vegan diet, Crohn disease | Poor diet and alcohol use; increased demand in pregnancy |
| Treatment | IM cyanocobalamin 1 mg weekly for 4 weeks, then monthly; oral works for maintenance or purely dietary causes | Oral folic acid 1 mg/day |

## Never give folate alone in B12 deficiency

Folate enters DNA synthesis downstream of B12, so it bypasses the folate trap and corrects the anemia but doesn't restore B12's role in myelin. The neurologic damage keeps progressing and can become irreversible while the normal blood count hides the deficiency.

## Pernicious anemia (Addison-Biermer disease)

- Autoantibodies against parietal cells or intrinsic factor, so B12 can't be absorbed in the ileum
- Associated with other autoimmune disease (Hashimoto thyroiditis, vitiligo)
- Higher risk of gastric carcinoma
- Treated with IM B12, or very high-dose oral B12 that bypasses intrinsic factor by passive diffusion

> High methylmalonic acid is specific for B12 deficiency.

Library: Red cell life cycle and normal destruction
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hemolytic Anemias

## Key findings

- A normal red cell lives about 120 days
- It needs membrane resiliency, stable reduced hemoglobin (Fe²⁺), and working enzymes that make ATP and NADPH
- With age, enzymes decay, hemoglobin oxidizes into Heinz bodies, and phosphatidylserine flips outward as an "eat me" signal; the stiff cell is trapped in the spleen and eaten by macrophages

## Recycling

| Part | Fate |
|---|---|
| Globin | Broken down to amino acids and reused |
| Iron | Recycled on transferrin |
| Protoporphyrin | Converted to unconjugated bilirubin, which rides on albumin to the liver for conjugation |

## In hemolytic anemia

The system is overwhelmed. Excess unconjugated bilirubin causes jaundice and raises the risk of calcium bilirubinate (pigmented) gallstones.

## Hallmark of hemolysis

An elevated reticulocyte count in the face of anemia, with high LDH, high indirect bilirubin, and low haptoglobin.

> Excess bilirubin from hemolysis causes jaundice and pigmented gallstones.

Library: Intravascular vs. extravascular hemolysis
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hemolytic Anemias

## Key findings

| Feature | Extravascular ("the spleen eater") | Intravascular ("the vessel burst") |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Splenic macrophages bite off antibody-coated or misshapen cells | Cells burst inside vessels from mechanical trauma, complement, or toxins |
| Findings | Spherocytes, splenomegaly, jaundice, high reticulocytes | Hemoglobinemia, hemoglobinuria (dark or red urine), hemosiderinuria days later |
| Haptoglobin | Slightly low or normal | Near zero (the most specific sign) |

## The haptoglobin sponge

Haptoglobin circulates waiting to soak up spilled hemoglobin. In intravascular hemolysis, lots of hemoglobin spills into plasma, the complex is cleared by the liver, and free haptoglobin drops to near zero. In extravascular hemolysis the spill happens inside the macrophage, so haptoglobin barely falls.

> Undetectable haptoglobin is the most specific sign of intravascular hemolysis.

Library: Hereditary spherocytosis
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hemolytic Anemias

## Mechanism

Defects in spectrin, ankyrin, or band 3, which tether the membrane to the cytoskeleton. Membrane blebs are bitten off by the spleen, the cell loses surface area and becomes a rigid sphere, and it's trapped and destroyed in the splenic sinuses.

## Diagnosis

- Spherocytes: small, round, no central pallor
- High MCHC, because the shrinking cell concentrates hemoglobin
- Positive osmotic fragility test (spherocytes burst in hypotonic saline)
- Negative Coombs test
- Jaundice, splenomegaly, and pigment gallstones; often a family history

## Treatment

Splenectomy stops the hemolysis by removing the site of destruction, though spherocytes persist. Vaccinate first, and expect Howell-Jolly bodies afterward.

> Spherocytes, high MCHC, positive osmotic fragility, and a negative Coombs mean hereditary spherocytosis.

Library: G6PD deficiency hemolytic crisis
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hemolytic Anemias

## Mechanism

G6PD makes NADPH, which glutathione needs to neutralize hydrogen peroxide. Without it, hemoglobin is oxidized and precipitates as Heinz bodies, and splenic macrophages bite them out, leaving bite cells (degmacytes).

## Key findings

- X-linked recessive, so it affects males
- Triggers: infection (the most common), fava beans, sulfa drugs (TMP-SMX), and antimalarials (primaquine)
- Sudden back pain and hemoglobinuria days after exposure

## Management

Avoid triggers and give supportive care during a crisis.

## Malaria link

G6PD deficiency partly protects against P. falciparum but makes primaquine and tafenoquine dangerous, so check G6PD status before giving them.

> Bite cells and Heinz bodies after a sulfa drug or infection mean G6PD deficiency.

Library: Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hemolytic Anemias

## Mechanism

ADAMTS13 normally cuts very large vWF multimers down to size. When it's deficient (or blocked by an antibody), huge vWF multimers trap platelets, microthrombi form in small vessels, and passing red cells are sheared into schistocytes (helmet cells). Finding schistocytes is an emergency until proven otherwise.

## FAT RN

Fever, anemia (hemolytic), thrombocytopenia, renal failure (mild), neurologic symptoms (confusion, seizures).

## Criteria that fit TTP

- Platelets under 30,000
- Hemolysis: high reticulocytes and bilirubin, low haptoglobin
- Creatinine under 2.0 (renal failure is mild in TTP and severe in HUS)
- Normal PT and PTT, which separates it from DIC

## Management

- Start plasma exchange (PLEX) immediately; don't wait for ADAMTS13 levels. PLEX removes the antibody and replaces the enzyme
- Don't transfuse platelets; it adds fuel to the fire

> Schistocytes, low platelets, and confusion with normal PT/PTT mean TTP: start plasma exchange now.

Library: Hemoglobin structure and the globin genes
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hemoglobinopathies

## Structure

- Hemoglobin is a tetramer of four globin chains and four heme groups: two alpha-like and two beta-like chains in adults
- Oxygen binds only ferrous iron (Fe²⁺); oxidized ferric iron (Fe³⁺) makes methemoglobin, which can't bind oxygen

## Normal adult hemoglobin

| Hemoglobin | Chains | Share | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| HbA | α2β2 | 96-98% | Dominant form |
| HbA2 | α2δ2 | 2.5-3.5% | Elevated in beta-thalassemia trait |
| HbF | α2γ2 | Under 1% | Higher oxygen affinity than HbA |

## Globin genes

| Cluster | Chromosome | Genes | Copies per person |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 16 | Zeta, alpha | 4 alpha genes (2 per chromosome) |
| Beta | 11 | Epsilon, gamma, delta, beta | 2 beta genes (1 per chromosome) |

- Four alpha genes give redundancy, which is why alpha-thalassemia comes in four severities and beta-thalassemia in three

> Alpha genes are on chromosome 16 (four copies); beta genes are on chromosome 11 (two copies).

Library: Hemoglobin switching
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hemoglobinopathies

## Key findings

| Site | Window | Hemoglobin |
|---|---|---|
| Yolk sac | 0-8 weeks | Embryonic: Gower-1 (ζ2ε2), Gower-2 (α2ε2), Portland (ζ2γ2) |
| Liver | 6 weeks to birth | Main site of HbF (α2γ2) |
| Spleen | 10-28 weeks | Transient |
| Bone marrow | 18 weeks to adult | Main site of HbA (α2β2) |

## The switch

Around birth, gamma-globin falls and beta-globin rises, and HbA becomes dominant about 2 months after birth. Because HbF has no beta chains, it protects infants with sickle cell disease or beta-thalassemia major, so symptoms don't appear until about 6 months, as HbF declines. The same fact is the target of hydroxyurea and BCL11A gene editing.

> HbF has no beta chains, which delays symptoms of beta-chain disorders until about 6 months.

## Qualitative (structural)

The globin structure is changed, usually by a missense mutation, while the amount is normal.

| Variant | Result |
|---|---|
| HbS | Sickle cell disease |
| HbC | Hemoglobin C disease |
| HbE | Hemoglobin E disease |
| Unstable hemoglobins | Precipitate as Heinz bodies (congenital Heinz body anemia) |
| High-affinity hemoglobins | Hold oxygen too tightly; tissue hypoxia drives EPO and polycythemia |
| Low-affinity hemoglobins | Release oxygen too easily; pseudo-cyanosis with adequate oxygenation |

## Quantitative (thalassemia)

The structure is normal, but too little of a chain is made: alpha- and beta-thalassemia.

## Which test

Electrophoresis separates structural variants by charge; HPLC quantifies the fractions to diagnose thalassemia.

> Structural variants change the chain; thalassemias reduce the amount of chain.

Library: Sickle cell disease
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hemoglobinopathies

## Molecular pathogenesis

- Point mutation in beta-globin (chromosome 11): glutamic acid (hydrophilic) → valine (hydrophobic) at position 6 (GAG → GTG)
- Deoxygenated HbS fits valine into a pocket on a neighboring molecule and stacks into rigid polymers ("glue replaced by Velcro")

## What drives polymerization

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Deoxygenation | Only deoxygenated HbS polymerizes; hypoxia, acidosis, and dehydration trigger sickling |
| Hemoglobin concentration | Higher MCHC (dehydration) speeds polymerization |
| HbF | Strongly inhibits polymerization |
| HbA | Inhibits polymerization, so trait carriers are asymptomatic |
| HbC | Takes part in polymerization, so HbSC disease is significant but milder than HbSS |

## Two mechanisms

- Vaso-occlusion: rigid cells block small vessels, causing ischemia and pain
- Hemolysis: extravascular (anemia, jaundice) and intravascular, where free hemoglobin scavenges nitric oxide, causing pulmonary hypertension and priapism

## Clinical manifestations

- Vaso-occlusive crisis: bone pain; dactylitis in infants
- Acute chest syndrome: the leading cause of death in adults
- Autosplenectomy: infections with encapsulated organisms
- Osteomyelitis: Salmonella is the classic pathogen, though S. aureus is most common overall
- Aplastic crisis: parvovirus B19 temporarily halts red cell production
- Stroke: high risk in children; screened with transcranial Doppler

> HbS is valine at position 6, and dehydration (high MCHC) promotes sickling.

Library: Beta-thalassemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hemoglobinopathies

## Genetics

Point mutations on chromosome 11 (promoter, splicing, or nonsense). Beta-zero makes no beta chain; beta-plus makes less.

## Mechanism

Too few beta chains lowers HbA. The key problem is that excess unpaired alpha chains are toxic: they precipitate, damage the membrane, and kill precursors in the marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis).

## Forms

| Form | Genotype | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Minor (trait) | Heterozygous | Asymptomatic microcytic anemia; high HbA2 |
| Intermedia | Variable | Can keep hemoglobin over 7 g/dL without transfusion |
| Major (Cooley anemia) | Homozygous | Severe transfusion-dependent anemia starting at 6 months |

## Complications of the major form

- Skeletal: crew-cut skull and chipmunk facies from massive marrow expansion driven by ineffective erythropoiesis
- Iron overload from transfusions and increased absorption (low hepcidin): heart failure, cirrhosis, bronze diabetes

> Excess alpha chains destroy precursors in the marrow, driving the bone changes of beta-thalassemia major.

Library: Alpha-thalassemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hemoglobinopathies

## Genetics

Gene deletions on chromosome 16; normal is four genes (αα/αα).

| Deletions | Genotype | Entity | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | -α/αα | Silent carrier | Asymptomatic |
| 2, cis | --/αα | Trait, common in Asians | Mild; higher risk of passing severe forms to children |
| 2, trans | -α/-α | Trait, common in Africans | Mild |
| 3 | --/-α | HbH disease | Excess beta chains form β4 tetramers; moderate to severe hemolytic anemia |
| 4 | --/-- | Hb Barts, hydrops fetalis | Excess gamma chains form γ4; incompatible with life without intrauterine transfusion |

## Hb Barts

Gamma-4 has extremely high oxygen affinity, like myoglobin, with a hyperbolic curve and no cooperativity. It holds oxygen and won't release it, causing the severe hypoxia of hydrops fetalis.

## Why beta-thalassemia is worse for the marrow

In beta-thalassemia, the excess alpha chains are insoluble and destroy precursors in the marrow. In alpha-thalassemia, the excess beta or gamma chains are soluble and form tetramers that cause hemolysis in the blood with far less marrow destruction.

> Hb Barts (γ4) binds oxygen so tightly that tissues starve, causing hydrops fetalis.

## Diagnosis

|---|---|
| CBC | Thalassemias cause microcytosis out of proportion to the anemia; Mentzer index (MCV / RBC) under 13 suggests thalassemia, over 13 suggests iron deficiency |

|---|---|---|---|---|

## Inheritance example

A mother with sickle cell trait (HbA and HbS, with more than half HbA) and a father who is HbC homozygous (100% HbC) will have children who are 50% HbAC (trait, asymptomatic) and 50% HbSC disease.

## Key findings

|---|---|---|

## Quantitative: immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)

IgG autoantibodies against GpIIb/IIIa; splenic macrophages eat the coated platelets. Low platelets, normal PT/PTT, more megakaryocytes in the marrow.

## Qualitative

| Disorder | Defect | Features | Ristocetin |
|---|---|---|---|

vWD is the one primary hemostasis disorder that can prolong the PTT, because vWF carries and protects factor VIII from clearance.

## The cascade

The goal is to turn fibrinogen (factor I) into fibrin using thrombin (factor IIa).

| Pathway | Trigger | Factors | Test | Drug |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Intrinsic | Negatively charged surfaces (collagen, glass) | 12 → 11 → 9 → 8 | PTT | Heparin |
| Extrinsic | Tissue factor (III) from injured endothelium | 7 | PT/INR | Warfarin |
| Common | Where they meet | 10 → 5 → 2 (thrombin) → 1 (fibrin) | Both | |

## Vitamin K-dependent proteins

- Factors 2, 7, 9, 10, protein C, and protein S
- Vitamin K is needed for gamma-carboxylation of glutamic acid residues, which lets these proteins bind calcium and phospholipid
- Warfarin inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR), blocking vitamin K recycling

## Warfarin skin necrosis

Proteins C and S have shorter half-lives than factors 2 and 10, so starting warfarin depletes the anticoagulant proteins first. This causes warfarin skin necrosis and venous gangrene in HIT.

> The vitamin K-dependent proteins are factors 2, 7, 9, 10, protein C, and protein S.

## Mixing study

Mix the patient's plasma 1:1 with normal plasma and repeat the test.

| Result | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Corrects | A missing factor was supplied: factor deficiency (such as hemophilia) |
| Doesn't correct | Something blocks clotting: factor inhibitor (anti-factor VIII antibody, lupus anticoagulant) |

## Pattern recognition

- Isolated prolonged PTT that corrects, in a male with deep tissue bleeding: hemophilia until proven otherwise
- Both PT and PTT prolonged: common pathway defect, vitamin K deficiency, or DIC

> A mixing study that corrects means a missing factor; one that doesn't means an inhibitor.

## Hemophilias

X-linked recessive disorders with deep tissue bleeding (hemarthrosis, muscle hematoma).

| Feature | Hemophilia A | Hemophilia B (Christmas disease) |
|---|---|---|
| Missing factor | 8 (A = eight) | 9 |
| Labs | High PTT, normal PT | High PTT, normal PT |

## Vitamin K deficiency

- Newborns (no gut flora), malabsorption (cystic fibrosis), long-term antibiotics (gut flora wiped out)
- High PT and PTT, since all vitamin K-dependent factors are affected

A prolonged PT or PTT that doesn't correct on mixing; causes include autoantibodies against factor 8 and lupus anticoagulant.

> Hemophilia A and B both prolong only the PTT, and both correct on mixing.

Library: Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Hemostasis, Thrombosis, and Bleeding Disorders

## Mechanism

IgG against the heparin-platelet factor 4 (PF4) complex binds platelet Fc receptors, causing massive platelet activation, thrombosis, and platelet consumption. Despite the low count, patients are clotting, not bleeding.

## 4 Ts (pre-test score, 0-8)

1. Thrombocytopenia: more than a 50% drop
2. Timing: days 5-10 of heparin
3. Thrombosis: new clot or skin necrosis
4. oTher causes ruled out

## Management

1. Stop all heparin immediately, including flushes
2. Start a direct thrombin inhibitor: argatroban (synthetic, approved for HIT, cleared by the liver) or bivalirudin
3. Don't use LMWH; it cross-reacts with HIT antibodies
4. Don't start warfarin until platelets are over 150k, because rapid protein C depletion causes venous gangrene and skin necrosis
5. Confirm only after stopping heparin: PF4-heparin ELISA to screen, serotonin release assay to confirm
6. Don't transfuse platelets

> In HIT, stop all heparin and start argatroban; no LMWH, no warfarin yet, no platelets.

Library: Acute vs. chronic myeloid neoplasms
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Myeloid Neoplasms

## Vocabulary

- Leukemia arises in the marrow and usually involves the blood; lymphoma arises in lymph nodes or lymphoid tissue and is essentially always lymphoid
- Myeloid means any blood cell outside the lymphoid line: neutrophils, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils, red cell precursors, megakaryocytes

| Feature | Acute | Chronic |
|---|---|---|
| Cells | Blasts (myeloblasts, megakaryoblasts, erythroblasts, monoblasts, atypical promyelocytes) | Mature-appearing cells |
| Course | Fatal if untreated | Comparatively indolent |

## Blasts

Large cells with a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, fine chromatin, and sometimes prominent nucleoli. The only definitive myeloid feature is the Auer rod, a needle-shaped aggregate of fused primary granules seen only in myeloid blasts.

## Categories

| Category | Defining feature |
|---|---|
| AML | Acute; 20% or more blasts |
| MDS | Dysplastic, ineffective hematopoiesis; under 20% blasts |
| MPN | Chronic overproduction of mature cells |
| MDS/MPN | Features of both |

## Myeloid sarcoma

A mass of myeloid blasts outside the marrow and blood; it behaves like AML even without meeting blast criteria.

> Auer rods are the one definitive marker of myeloid blasts.

Library: Acute myeloid leukemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Myeloid Neoplasms

## Definition

20% or more myeloid blasts in blood or marrow. PML-RARA, inv(16), and t(8;21) establish AML regardless of blast percentage.

## Presentation (marrow failure)

- Neutropenia: recurrent or severe infections
- Thrombocytopenia: bleeding, bruising, gum bleeding
- Anemia: fatigue, pallor
- Bone pain from marrow expansion

## Classification

The older FAB system (M0-M7) was morphologic; WHO and ICC now use genetics. Two FAB names stay high-yield: M3 is acute promyelocytic leukemia, and M7 is acute megakaryoblastic leukemia, classically in Down syndrome before age 5.

| Abnormality | Prognosis | Associations |
|---|---|---|
| t(8;21) | Good | AML with maturation |
| inv(16) | Good | Abnormal eosinophils with large blue-purple granules (harlequin cells) and monocytic differentiation (AML-M4Eo) |
| 11q23 (KMT2A, formerly MLL) | Poor | Monocytic differentiation (M4/M5) |

- Monocytic differentiation causes gingival hyperplasia, lymphadenopathy, organomegaly, and myeloid sarcoma

> Gum infiltration in AML means monocytic differentiation.

Library: Acute promyelocytic leukemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Myeloid Neoplasms

## Molecular pathogenesis

t(15;17) fuses PML (chromosome 15) to the retinoic acid receptor alpha (RARA, chromosome 17). PML-RARA blocks promyelocyte maturation. APL is about 5-10% of AML.

## Morphology

Atypical promyelocytes, often with bundled Auer rods; flow shows CD34-negative, HLA-DR-negative, strong MPO. A hypogranular variant is harder to spot but carries the same DIC risk.

## DIC

The granules contain procoagulants: prolonged PT/PTT, low fibrinogen, high D-dimer and LDH, low haptoglobin, low platelets. Standard chemotherapy in unrecognized APL releases the granules and worsens DIC.

## ATRA

All-trans-retinoic acid binds the RARα portion of PML-RARA and causes its degradation, so promyelocytes can mature. This differentiation therapy lifts the maturation block without spilling procoagulant granules. Start it immediately on suspicion, before FISH confirms PML::RARA; stop it if the diagnosis isn't confirmed.

## Differentiation syndrome

Capillary leak from cytokines released by maturing promyelocytes, days to weeks after starting ATRA or arsenic trioxide: dyspnea, weight gain over 5 kg, fever of 38°C or more, hypotension, rising WBC, high bilirubin and creatinine, lung opacities or effusions. Treat with dexamethasone and hold ATRA only if severe.

> Start ATRA the moment APL is suspected; waiting for FISH risks death from DIC.

Library: Myelodysplastic syndromes
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Myeloid Neoplasms

## Key findings

- Stem cells make dysplastic myeloid cells that struggle to leave the marrow (ineffective hematopoiesis)
- A hypercellular marrow with peripheral cytopenias: the marrow is busy, but the blood is empty
- Always under 20% blasts
- More common in the elderly
- Can progress to AML as cytogenetic changes accumulate

## Characteristic morphology

- Pseudo-Pelger-Huët cells: hypogranular neutrophils with bilobed nuclei
- Micromegakaryocytes
- Multinucleated erythroid precursors
- Ring sideroblasts on iron stain

## Risk-based treatment

Treatment is guided by IPSS-R (ANC, hemoglobin, platelets, marrow blast percentage, cytogenetics) and IPSS-M (adds mutations in 31 MDS genes), and it isn't automatically cytotoxic.

> MDS is a busy marrow with empty blood and under 20% blasts.

Library: MPN principles and chronic myeloid leukemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Myeloid Neoplasms

## Myeloproliferative neoplasms

The marrow overproduces maturing myeloid cells, driven by a constitutively active signaling mutation. Core members are CML, PV, ET, and PMF. Patients are usually elderly, often asymptomatic or with fatigue or splenomegaly, and any MPN can progress to acute leukemia. BCR-ABL1 defines CML; JAK2 (or CALR or MPL) underlies PV, ET, and PMF.

## CML pathology

- Leukocytosis with a pronounced left shift, neutrophilia, eosinophilia, and prominent basophilia
- Low LAP score, which separates it from a benign leukemoid reaction (high LAP, no prominent basophilia)
- Hypercellular marrow with small monolobate (dwarf) megakaryocytes

## Phases

Chronic (low blasts) → accelerated (about 10-19% blasts) → blast phase (20% or more), which can be myeloid or lymphoid because the mutation is in an early stem cell.

## Molecular driver

Essentially all CML carries the Philadelphia chromosome, t(9;22), making BCR-ABL1, a constitutively active tyrosine kinase. It's detected by FISH, karyotype, or PCR (used for monitoring). The same translocation in ALL is a poor-prognosis marker.

## Therapy

Imatinib and related TKIs occupy the ATP-binding site of BCR-ABL, blocking its signaling rather than killing all dividing cells: about 95% complete hematologic and 74% complete cytogenetic response. Kinase-domain mutations cause resistance, and dasatinib and nilotinib cover many of them at greater cost and toxicity.

> Low LAP and basophilia separate CML from a leukemoid reaction.

Library: Polycythemia vera
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Myeloid Neoplasms

## Mechanism

JAK2 normally relays cytokine receptor signals (such as from the EPO receptor). Mutated JAK2 signals without ligand, so cells proliferate whether or not they're told to. Essentially all PV carries a JAK2 activating mutation.

## Key findings

- Red cell proliferation dominates; the marrow shows panmyelosis, and platelets and WBC may also be high
- Hyperviscosity and thrombosis: MI, stroke, DVT, PE, Budd-Chiari, headache, vision changes, hemorrhage
- Aquagenic pruritus (itching after a hot shower)
- Facial plethora
- Erythromelalgia: burning hand and foot pain from microvascular thrombi that responds to aspirin
- Spent phase: marrow fibrosis with cytopenias and worsening splenomegaly

## PV vs. secondary polycythemia

EPO is suppressed in PV by negative feedback but normal or high in secondary polycythemia (hypoxia, altitude, smoking, obesity, sleep apnea, EPO-secreting tumors, high-affinity hemoglobins). Spurious polycythemia is from dehydration.

## Treatment

Phlebotomy, with or without hydroxyurea.

> A low EPO level separates polycythemia vera from secondary polycythemia.

Library: Essential thrombocythemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Myeloid Neoplasms

## Key findings

- Dominated by thrombocytosis, without significant leukocytosis or polycythemia (which separates it from CML and PV)
- Usually adults, but consider it in young women with unexplained thrombocytosis
- Normo- to slightly hypercellular marrow with large staghorn megakaryocytes; fibrosis isn't typical at diagnosis
- Drivers: about 50% JAK2, otherwise CALR or MPL
- Often asymptomatic, but can cause bleeding, clotting, or erythromelalgia

## Diagnosis of exclusion

Most thrombocytosis is reactive (iron deficiency, cancer, trauma, surgery, infection, hyposplenism), so ET is diagnosed only after these are excluded.

## Treatment

Aspirin, with hydroxyurea if high risk.

> Most thrombocytosis is reactive, so ET is a diagnosis of exclusion.

Library: Primary myelofibrosis
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Myeloid Neoplasms

## Phases

| Phase | Findings |
|---|---|
| Prefibrotic | Granulocytes and megakaryocytes predominate, often with thrombocytosis and leukocytosis; less basophilia than CML and no true polycythemia |
| Fibrotic | Abnormal megakaryocytes release fibroblast-stimulating cytokines; hypocellular marrow, worsening cytopenias, massive splenomegaly from extramedullary hematopoiesis |

## Smear and marrow

A myelophthisic (leukoerythroblastic) smear with teardrop cells (dacrocytes), nucleated red cells, and immature granulocytes; a dry tap confirmed by reticulin stain.

## PMF vs. ET

Fibrosis always eventually develops in PMF but not in ET, and PMF megakaryocytes cluster and are markedly atypical, unlike the discrete staghorn forms of ET.

## Other features

JAK2 in about 50%, otherwise CALR or MPL; older patients; the least common core MPN. Treatment is supportive care, JAK inhibitors, or stem cell transplant.

> Teardrop cells and a dry tap point to primary myelofibrosis.

Library: Chronic myelomonocytic leukemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Myeloid Neoplasms

## Key findings

- The most common MDS/MPN overlap neoplasm, with both proliferative and dysplastic features
- Persistent monocytosis: at least 0.5 × 10⁹/L absolute and 10% relative
- Under 20% blasts
- Dysplasia in at least one myeloid line
- CML and other defined MPNs must be excluded
- Subtyped by WBC and blast/promonocyte percentage into CMML-1 and CMML-2

> Persistent monocytosis with dysplasia and under 20% blasts means CMML.

Library: Leukemia vs. lymphoma and lymphoid neoplasm categories
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Lymphoid Neoplasms

## Leukemia vs. lymphoma

- Leukemia arises in the marrow and circulates in the blood; lymphoma arises as a mass, usually in a lymph node
- These terms describe the presentation, not always different diseases: SLL and CLL are the same disease, called SLL with a mass and CLL when cells circulate above 5,000/μL

## Categories

| Split | Key points |
|---|---|
| Immature vs. mature | Immature (blasts) give B- or T-lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma; mature split into Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin |
| Hodgkin vs. non-Hodgkin | Hodgkin has malignant Reed-Sternberg cells in a reactive background |
| B vs. T/NK non-Hodgkin | Most NHL is B cell; T/NK lymphomas such as mycosis fungoides are less common |

## Mature B cell neoplasms

Peripherally derived, often multicentric and widespread at diagnosis, mostly in older adults (peak 55-60). Prognosis depends on subtype, tumor burden, stage, bulk, LDH, age, and performance status.

> SLL and CLL are the same disease; the name depends on where the cells are.

Library: B-ALL and T-ALL

## Shared features

- Proliferation of lymphoblasts: large cells with a high N:C ratio
- TdT positive (a DNA polymerase used early in V(D)J recombination), marking lymphoblasts of either lineage
- Most common cancer in children (peak 3-7); also in older adults with worse prognosis

| Feature | B-ALL | T-ALL |
|---|---|---|
| Share | 80-85% of childhood ALL; the most common childhood malignancy | Less common; adolescent males |
| Markers | TdT+, CD19+, CD10+ (CALLA), CD20+/-, CD22+, CD34+, PAX5+ | TdT+, CD2+, CD3+, CD7+ (variable CD1a, CD4, CD8) |
| Presentation | Like a leukemia: bruising, bleeding, fatigue, infections, bone pain | Like a lymphoma: "thymic mass in a teenager," anterior mediastinal mass with SVC syndrome or airway compression |

## Prognosis in B-ALL

| Favorable | Unfavorable |
|---|---|
| Age 2-10, normal or low WBC, rapid response, t(12;21) | CNS or testicular involvement at presentation, t(9;22) Philadelphia chromosome (far more common in adults) |

## Sanctuary sites

ALL hides in the CNS and testes, so prophylactic intrathecal chemotherapy is mandatory.

> B-ALL presents like a leukemia; T-ALL presents like a lymphoma, as a thymic mass in a teenager.

Library: Hodgkin lymphoma
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Lymphoid Neoplasms

## General features

- Lymph node-based and spreads predictably by contiguity (NHL is often widespread at diagnosis)
- Usually above the diaphragm; Waldeyer's ring rarely involved
- Reed-Sternberg cell: large, binucleate, prominent nucleoli ("owl's eyes"); CD15+, CD30+, CD45-negative, variable B cell markers (CD20 in about 30%, PAX5 weakly in about 95%)
- RS cells are a minority; the mass is mostly reactive eosinophils, plasma cells, histiocytes, and fibrosis drawn in by their cytokines
- Painless lymphadenopathy, B symptoms (fever, chills, night sweats, weight loss), sometimes pruritus

## Classical subtypes

| Subtype | Key features |
|---|---|
| Nodular sclerosis | Most common; young women; mediastinal mass; collagen bands and lacunar cells |
| Mixed cellularity | EBV; many eosinophils (RS cells secrete IL-5) |
| Lymphocyte rich | Best prognosis |
| Lymphocyte depleted | Worst prognosis; HIV and elderly |

## Nodular lymphocyte predominant HL

Popcorn (L&H) cells that are CD20+, CD45+, CD15-negative, CD30-negative, the opposite of classical HL. Very indolent, with late recurrences, and not usually mediastinal.

> Reed-Sternberg cells are CD15+, CD30+, and CD45-negative.

Library: CLL and SLL
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Lymphoid Neoplasms

## Key findings

- Neoplasm of naive B cells; CLL mainly in blood and marrow, SLL mainly in nodes
- Most common adult leukemia, median age 70
- Small mature lymphocytes with clumped "soccer ball" chromatin; smudge cells on the smear
- Proliferation centers (pale nodules of prolymphocytes) in nodes are pathognomonic for SLL
- CD5+ (aberrant), CD23+ (separates it from mantle cell), CD19+, dim CD20

## Complications

- Hypogammaglobulinemia, because the clone doesn't make effective immunoglobulin; infection is the most common cause of death
- AIHA and ITP from autoantibodies made by the clone
- Richter transformation to aggressive DLBCL: suspect it when a stable patient develops a rapidly growing mass or new fevers

> CLL is CD5+ and CD23+; CD23 is what separates it from mantle cell lymphoma.

Library: Hairy cell leukemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Lymphoid Neoplasms

## Key findings

- B cells with fine, hair-like cytoplasmic projections
- Triad: massive splenomegaly (red pulp infiltration), pancytopenia (marrow failure), and a dry tap (marrow fibrosis)
- CD19+, CD20+, CD11c+, CD103+, CD25+, Annexin A1+; TRAP (tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase) positive
- BRAF V600E mutation
- Excellent prognosis

## Treatment

Cladribine (2-CDA), an adenosine deaminase inhibitor.

> Hairy cell leukemia is TRAP-positive with BRAF V600E and responds to cladribine.

Library: Follicular lymphoma
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Lymphoid Neoplasms

## Mechanism

A neoplasm of germinal center B cells. t(14;18) moves BCL2 (chromosome 18) next to IgH (chromosome 14), causing BCL2 overexpression. BCL2 stabilizes the mitochondrial membrane and blocks cytochrome c release, so B cells that fail somatic hypermutation and should die survive.

## Morphology

A nodular pattern effacing the node, made of centrocytes (small, cleaved) and centroblasts (large); graded 1-3 by centroblast count, and grade 3 is treated like DLBCL.

## Clinical

The prototype indolent lymphoma: waxing and waning painless generalized lymphadenopathy, usually incurable with repeated relapses; marrow involved in 40-70%; may transform to DLBCL.

## Follicular lymphoma vs. reactive hyperplasia

| Feature | Follicular lymphoma | Reactive follicles |
|---|---|---|
| Architecture | Disrupted; no preserved mantle zone or polarity | Preserved |
| Tingible-body macrophages | Absent (nothing is dying) | Present |
| BCL2 | Positive (with CD19, CD20, CD10) | Negative |

> Follicular lymphoma is BCL2-positive and lacks tingible-body macrophages.

Library: Marginal zone (MALT) lymphoma
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Lymphoid Neoplasms

## Key findings

- Indolent B cell lymphoma, divided into splenic, nodal, and extranodal (MALT) forms
- Arises from chronic inflammation: stomach (H. pylori), thyroid (Hashimoto thyroiditis), salivary glands (Sjögren syndrome)
- t(11;18)

## Treatment

For gastric MALT lymphoma, antibiotics to treat H. pylori alone can cure the lymphoma.

> Gastric MALT lymphoma can be cured by treating H. pylori.

Library: Mantle cell lymphoma
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Lymphoid Neoplasms

## Key findings

- About 5% of NHL; older men with fatigue and diffuse lymphadenopathy
- Often involves the GI tract and Waldeyer's ring; marrow involvement is common, and a leukemic phase can mimic CLL
- Expands the mantle zone around follicles
- t(11;14): cyclin D1 (chromosome 11) moves to IgH (chromosome 14), driving G1 to S
- CD5+ like CLL but CD23-negative, and cyclin D1+ on nuclear stain
- More aggressive than the other small cell lymphomas, with a poor prognosis

> Mantle cell lymphoma is CD5+ but CD23-negative, with cyclin D1 from t(11;14).

Library: Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Lymphoid Neoplasms

## Key findings

- The most common NHL and most common lymphoma overall in adults; only about 15% of childhood lymphomas
- Diffuse sheets of large cells (nuclei over twice a normal lymphocyte) disrupting the node
- Arises de novo or by transformation from a low-grade lymphoma (CLL, follicular)
- Mutations in BCL2, BCL6, and MYC
- Germinal center B-cell subtype has a better prognosis than the activated B-cell subtype
- Rapidly enlarging, symptomatic nodal or extranodal mass; spleen and marrow involvement is less common but worsens prognosis

## Treatment

Aggressive without treatment but potentially curable with R-CHOP.

> DLBCL is the most common adult lymphoma and potentially curable with R-CHOP.

## Key findings

- Highly aggressive, a STAT diagnosis; children and young adults (about 30% of childhood NHL); often extranodal

- Starry sky: sheets of intermediate-sized tumor cells (the sky) with pale tingible-body macrophages (the stars) eating dying cells

| Endemic (African) | Jaw or facial mass; EBV |
| Sporadic (North America) | Abdominal mass (bowel, retroperitoneum, ovaries), classically the ileocecal valve |

Library: Mycosis fungoides and Sézary syndrome

## Mycosis fungoides

- The most common T cell lymphoma, in adults and the elderly
- Mimics contact dermatitis, atopic dermatitis, or psoriasis, with insidious onset favoring non-sun-exposed skin
- Patch → plaque → tumor, which can wax and wane and eventually ulcerate
- Epidermotropic infiltrate with Pautrier microabscesses (neoplastic T cells in the epidermis)
- CD3+, CD4+, CD7-negative (helper T cell)

## Sézary syndrome

Spread to the blood: Sézary cells with cerebriform nuclei, and erythroderma.

> Pautrier microabscesses mark mycosis fungoides; cerebriform cells in blood mark Sézary syndrome.

Library: Malaria life cycle
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Protozoan Parasites of Blood and Lymphoid Systems

## Classification and vector

Plasmodium is an apicomplexan (an apical complex on the merozoite mediates red cell entry) and a coccidian parasite. All human malaria is spread by the female Anopheles mosquito.

## The cycle

1. Inoculation: sporozoites injected into skin travel to the liver and invade hepatocytes; this stage is silent
2. Liver schizogony: each sporozoite becomes a liver schizont with thousands of merozoites; in P. vivax and P. ovale, some become dormant hypnozoites that cause relapse months to years later (P. falciparum forms none and doesn't relapse)
3. Red cell invasion: merozoites enter red cells, with tropism that differs by species
4. Erythrocytic schizogony: ring trophozoite → trophozoite → schizont → rupture, releasing merozoites, parasite DNA, and hemozoin; rupture causes fever
5. Sexual cycle: gametocytes taken up in a blood meal fuse in the mosquito midgut → ookinete → oocyst → sporozoites in the salivary glands

## Red cell tropism

| Tropism | Species | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Reticulocytes only (CD71+) | P. vivax, P. ovale, P. knowlesi | Lower parasite burden, milder disease |
| Senescent cells only | P. malariae | Lowest parasite burden |
| Any red cell | P. falciparum | Highest parasitemia and severity |

> Only P. vivax and P. ovale form liver hypnozoites and relapse.

Library: Malaria pathogenesis and host resistance
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Protozoan Parasites of Blood and Lymphoid Systems

## Fever

Red cell rupture releases merozoites, hemozoin, and parasite DNA that TLR9 senses, triggering a TNF-alpha surge and the paroxysm.

## Cerebral malaria

P. falciparum exports PfEMP1 into the red cell cytoskeleton, forming sticky knobs that cause cytoadherence and rosetting. Parasitized cells sequester in cerebral capillaries, causing occlusion, edema, and anoxia, with retinal whitening and hemorrhages. Death usually comes from reduced venous outflow, cerebral edema, and herniation.

## Host resistance

| Factor | Protection |
|---|---|
| Sickle cell trait (HbAS) | 80-90% protection from severe or fatal falciparum malaria |
| HbAC, beta-thalassemia trait, hereditary ovalocytosis, G6PD deficiency | Partial protection against falciparum |
| Duffy antigen negativity (West and Central Africans) | About 100% protection from P. vivax, which uses Duffy as its receptor |
| Maternal antibody | Protects infants for 3-6 months; children under 5 then have the highest mortality |

- These traits are a textbook example of balancing selection

> PfEMP1 makes falciparum-infected cells stick in brain capillaries.

Library: Clinical features of malaria
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Protozoan Parasites of Blood and Lymphoid Systems

## Paroxysm

In a non-immune traveler, symptoms classically start about 6 weeks after leaving a malarious area.

| Stage | Features |
|---|---|
| Cold | Chills for 15 minutes to 1 hour, with schizont rupture and the TNF-alpha surge |
| Hot | High fever, restlessness, myalgia for 6-10 hours |
| Sweating | Drenching sweats as the fever breaks |

## Fever periodicity

| Cycle | Species | Name |
|---|---|---|
| Every 48 hours | P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale | Tertian |
| Every 72 hours | P. malariae | Quartan |
| Daily | P. knowlesi | Quotidian |

## Severe malaria

- Children: decreased consciousness, shock, respiratory distress, jaundice, prostration, severe anemia, hypoglycemia
- Pregnancy: mortality up to 50%, as parasitized cells sequester in the placenta
- Blackwater fever: massive intravascular hemolysis with dark urine, possibly with cerebral malaria, jaundice, renal failure, DIC, and pulmonary edema; death can come within 1-2 days

> P. malariae is quartan (72 hours); P. knowlesi is daily.

Library: Antimalarial drugs
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Protozoan Parasites of Blood and Lymphoid Systems

## The drug target

In its food vacuole, the parasite digests hemoglobin with plasmepsins into toxic heme. Heme polymerase converts it to hematin and then inert hemozoin (malarial pigment).

## Drug classes

| Class | Agents | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Heme-detoxification blockers (erythrocytic forms only) | Chloroquine, hydroxychloroquine, quinine, mefloquine, amodiaquine | Bind free heme and block heme polymerase, so toxic heme kills the parasite; shared mechanism means cross-resistance |
| Artemisinins | Artemether, artesunate (from wormwood, Artemisia annua) | Endoperoxide bridge makes reactive oxygen species that hit many parasite targets; always combined with a partner drug |
| Atovaquone | | Coenzyme Q analog that collapses the parasite's mitochondrial electron transport chain |
| Proguanil | | Inhibits parasite dihydrofolate reductase; synergistic with sulfa drugs |
| 8-aminoquinolines | Primaquine, tafenoquine | Eradicate liver hypnozoites |

> Chloroquine and its relatives block heme polymerase; artemisinins make reactive oxygen species.

Library: Malaria treatment and prevention
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 7 / Protozoan Parasites of Blood and Lymphoid Systems

## Regimens

| Scenario | Regimen |
|---|---|
| Uncomplicated P. falciparum or unknown species, outside the Caribbean | Artemether + lumefantrine (Coartem) or atovaquone + proguanil (Malarone) |
| Uncomplicated, Caribbean | Chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine (sensitivity persists) |
| Uncomplicated P. vivax, P. ovale, or P. malariae | Chloroquine plus primaquine or tafenoquine to clear hypnozoites |
| Severe malaria | IV artesunate plus supportive care |

## The core asymmetry

P. falciparum is treated as chloroquine-resistant in most of the world and needs no anti-relapse therapy. P. vivax and P. ovale need hypnozoite-directed therapy on top of blood-stage treatment, or they relapse.

## Cautions

- Check G6PD before primaquine or tafenoquine; both are contraindicated in pregnancy
- Severe pediatric malaria can come with invasive Salmonella bacteremia, so draw blood cultures and give empiric ceftriaxone

## Prevention

Chemoprophylaxis, plus the RTS,S and R21 vaccines against the sporozoite's circumsporozoite protein, which are WHO-recommended with about 75% efficacy for 6-12 months in young children.

> Vivax and ovale need primaquine or tafenoquine (after a G6PD check); falciparum doesn't.

## Organism and vector

- Babesia microti (US) and B. divergens (Europe), apicomplexans like Plasmodium
- Spread by Ixodes ticks (Ixodes scapularis in the US), usually the nymph stage, the same vector as Lyme disease and anaplasmosis, so co-infection is common
- Mostly zoonotic; mortality around 20% in the young, elderly, and immunocompromised

## Life cycle

- In the tick: transovarial and transstadial transmission
- In humans: sporozoites invade red cells directly, with no liver stage, and multiply by merogony (binary fission), not schizogony

## Clinical features

- After 1-6 weeks: fever, chills, headache, myalgia, arthralgia lasting 1-2 weeks; anemia and fatigue can last months
- Hemolytic anemia: low hemoglobin and platelets, high liver enzymes, bilirubin, and LDH
- Severe disease with asplenia, age over 50, or immunocompromise: ARDS, heart failure, DIC, renal failure

## Diagnosis

Consider it with exposure in an endemic area (US Northeast and upper Midwest, parts of Europe), with or without a known tick bite, or a blood transfusion in the past 6 months. Confirm with PCR, IFA, or smear showing the Maltese cross tetrad.

## Treatment

| Severity | Regimen |
|---|---|
| Mild to moderate | Atovaquone + azithromycin for 7-10 days |
| Severe | Clindamycin + quinine |

> The Maltese cross tetrad is pathognomonic for Babesia.

## Key findings

| Feature | Malaria | Babesiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Vector | Female Anopheles mosquito | Ixodes tick |
| Liver stage | Yes, hepatic schizogony (with or without hypnozoites) | None |
| Red cell replication | Schizogony | Merogony (binary fission) |
| Pathognomonic smear | Crescent gametocytes; never a Maltese cross | Maltese cross tetrad |
| Severity risk | Non-immune status, pregnancy, young children | Asplenia, age over 50, immunocompromise |
| First-line treatment | Artemisinin combination or chloroquine, by species and region | Atovaquone + azithromycin (mild); clindamycin + quinine (severe) |

> Only Babesia forms a Maltese cross, and only Babesia has no liver stage.

## Key findings

- Toxoplasma gondii is a coccidian parasite; the cat is the definitive host
- Humans are infected by oocysts (cat litter, produce) or tissue cysts in undercooked meat
- 80-90% of immunocompetent infections are asymptomatic; symptomatic infection is a heterophile-negative mono-like illness (EBV is heterophile-positive)
- Congenital toxoplasmosis: chorioretinitis, hydrocephalus, intracranial calcifications
- Reactivation in AIDS (CD4 under 100): ring-enhancing brain lesions, treated with pyrimethamine plus sulfadiazine

> Toxoplasma mono-like illness is heterophile-negative, unlike EBV.

## Life cycle

- Trypanosoma cruzi is a flagellated protozoan (not an apicomplexan) spread by the triatomine (kissing, reduviid) bug, which feeds near the mouth or eyes
- The bug doesn't inject the parasite: its feces, carrying trypomastigotes, are rubbed into the bite wound or mucous membranes, often during sleep
- In the bug: trypomastigotes become epimastigotes and back again in the hindgut
- In humans: trypomastigotes invade muscle and nerve cells, become intracellular amastigotes, and replicate until the cell bursts
- 60-80% of infected people remain permanently asymptomatic

## Pathogenesis

Years of amastigote replication and inflammation destroy myenteric neurons and cardiac muscle and conduction tissue. Chronic Chagas is a set of denervation and dilation syndromes, which is why late antiparasitic drugs can't reverse it.

> Chagas is spread by kissing bug feces rubbed into the wound, not by the bite.

## Acute phase

- Nonspecific fever with hepatosplenomegaly and atypical lymphocytosis; a tender swelling at the bite (chagoma); trypomastigotes on the smear
- Romaña sign: one-sided periorbital swelling from a bite near the eye
- Rare (under 1%): meningoencephalitis, myocarditis

## Chronic phase

Most patients stay in an indeterminate form (asymptomatic, positive serology).

| Disease | Share | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| GI | 5-35% | Megaesophagus and megacolon: dysphagia, odynophagia, constipation |
| Cardiac | 20-40% | Dilated cardiomyopathy with apical atrophy or aneurysm, arrhythmias, thromboembolism, sudden death |

- Memory hook: "T. cruzi causes big problems" (dilated cardiomyopathy, megacolon, megaesophagus)

## Diagnosis

- Acute: microscopy or PCR for circulating trypomastigotes
- Chronic: ELISA for anti-T. cruzi IgG (sensitivity about 94%, specificity about 98%)

## Treatment

- Benznidazole or nifurtimox, converted by parasite nitroreductase into metabolites toxic to its DNA, RNA, and proteins
- Indicated for acute, congenital, and chronic infection in women of childbearing age or children; far more effective before organ damage
- No drug reverses established organ damage, which is managed with standard achalasia, megacolon, heart failure, and arrhythmia care

> Antitrypanosomal drugs don't reverse the heart and GI damage of chronic Chagas disease.
