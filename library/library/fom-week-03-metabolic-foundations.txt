Library: ATP generation and creatine phosphate
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Cellular Energetics

## Key findings

- About 90% of ATP is made from ADP by ATP synthase (complex V)
- Substrate-level phosphorylation occurs during glycolysis
- Transphosphorylation lets higher-energy phosphates such as creatine phosphate make ATP
- Phosphocreatine is a ready source of energy for ATP synthesis in muscle, and creatine kinase catalyzes the reaction

## Amphibolic pathways

A process that involves both anabolism and catabolism is amphibolic. The citric acid cycle is the example: it handles the aerobic catabolism of carbohydrates, lipids, and amino acids, and it also supplies intermediates as starting points for biosynthesis.

> About 90% of ATP comes from ATP synthase (complex V); glycolysis makes ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation.

Library: Adenylate kinase and energy charge
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Cellular Energetics

## Key findings

- Adenylate kinase catalyzes the interconversion of AMP, ADP, and ATP, and it acts as a metabolic monitor
- The adenylate (energy) charge measures the energy stored in a cell
- Energy charge = ([ATP] + ½[ADP]) / ([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP])
- A cell aims for an energy charge of about 0.9

## What the charge controls

| Energy charge | Response |
|---|---|
| High | Catabolic pathways shut down |
| Low | Anabolic pathways shut down and catabolic pathways turn on to make ATP |

> Cells hold their energy charge near 0.9; a falling charge turns on catabolism.

## Caloric value

Caloric value is the energy released when 1 gram of a molecule is completely oxidized.

| Fuel | kcal/g | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | 4 | More oxidized; needs less oxygen to release its energy |
| Fatty acid | 9 | More reduced; needs more oxygen |

## Respiratory quotient

- Respiratory quotient (RQ) = CO2 produced / O2 consumed
- It lets you estimate the mix of fuels being oxidized

> RQ = CO2 produced / O2 consumed.

## Key findings

| Enzyme | Inhibited by | Stimulated by |
|---|---|---|
| Citrate synthase | ATP, NADH, succinyl-CoA, citrate | Not listed |
| Isocitrate dehydrogenase | ATP, NADH | ADP, NAD+ |
| Alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase | ATP, NADH, succinyl-CoA | Not listed |

- The inhibitors are downstream products, and the stimulators are precursors of ATP production

## Anabolic precursors

- The citric acid cycle provides intermediates for biosynthesis
- The mitochondrial tricarboxylate transporter exchanges citrate from the mitochondrial matrix for malate. Citrate lyase then turns citrate into acetyl-CoA in the cytoplasm for fatty acid synthesis
- Inhibitors of this transporter reduce fatty acid synthesis

> Isocitrate dehydrogenase is inhibited by ATP and NADH and stimulated by ADP and NAD+.

## Key findings

- Hexokinase is the first step, converting glucose to glucose-6-phosphate, which traps glucose in the cell
- The two regulated irreversible steps are both highly allosterically controlled

| Step | Enzyme | Activated by | Inhibited by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fructose-6-P → fructose-1,6-bisphosphate | Phosphofructokinase (PFK) | AMP, fructose-2,6-bisphosphate | ATP, citrate |
| Phosphoenolpyruvate → pyruvate | Pyruvate kinase | Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate | ATP, acetyl-CoA |

> PFK is activated by AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate and inhibited by ATP and citrate.

Library: Anaerobic glycolysis and lactic acidosis
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Clinical Significance of Metabolic Pathways Involved in Glucose Homeostasis

## Key findings

- Red blood cells have no mitochondria, so they rely on glycolysis for ATP
- Anaerobic glycolysis also occurs in hypoxic conditions
- Lactate dehydrogenase converts pyruvate to lactate, which the liver can then use

## Causes of lactic acidosis

- Hypoxia, as in COPD, exercise, or anemia
- Reduced clearance of upstream products such as pyruvate. In pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency, pyruvate can't become acetyl-CoA
- Reduced clearance from renal or hepatic dysfunction

> Red cells have no mitochondria, so glycolysis is their only source of ATP.

Library: 2,3-BPG and hemoglobin oxygen affinity
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Clinical Significance of Metabolic Pathways Involved in Glucose Homeostasis

## Key findings

- 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG, or DPG) is made from 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate
- It stabilizes the low-affinity state of hemoglobin

| Condition | DPG | Curve | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| High ATP | More DPG (1,3-BPG is pushed toward the DPG pathway) | Right shift | Oxygen binds less well and is delivered to tissues more easily |
| High ADP | Less DPG | Left shift | Oxygen binds well but is harder to deliver |

## Fetal hemoglobin

Fetal hemoglobin doesn't bind DPG, so it has a higher oxygen affinity and takes up oxygen from the mother.

> More DPG shifts the curve right and unloads oxygen to tissues.

Library: Glycolytic enzyme deficiencies in red cells
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Clinical Significance of Metabolic Pathways Involved in Glucose Homeostasis

## Mechanism

When glycolysis runs too slowly, red cells can't make ATP fast enough. The sodium/potassium pumps stop working, and the cells become echinocytes (thorny projections), leading to hemolytic anemia.

## Key findings

| Deficiency | DPG | Curve | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hexokinase | No DPG | Left shift | Most fatal |
| Phosphofructokinase | No DPG | Left shift | Not listed |
| Pyruvate kinase | DPG present | Right shift | Most common; chronic, autosomal recessive, worldwide distribution |

- Listed symptoms of hemolytic anemia from defective glycolysis include fainting, low blood pressure, and shortness of breath

> Deficiencies before the DPG branch point (hexokinase, PFK) remove DPG and shift the curve left; pyruvate kinase deficiency shifts it right.

Library: Glycogen synthesis and breakdown
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Clinical Significance of Metabolic Pathways Involved in Glucose Homeostasis

## Key findings

- Portal circulation sends all nutrient-rich blood through the liver before the rest of the body
- Glycogenin is the primer for making glycogen from glucose
- Glucose-6-phosphatase turns glucose-6-phosphate into free glucose

| Enzyme | Role | Partner enzyme | Allosteric control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glycogen synthase | Makes glycogen | Branching enzyme | Activated by glucose-6-phosphate |
| Glycogen phosphorylase | Breaks down glycogen | Debranching enzyme | Inhibited by glucose and glucose-6-phosphate |

## Hormonal and muscle control

- Glucagon and epinephrine activate glycogen phosphorylase
- In skeletal muscle, which needs ATP quickly, glycogen phosphorylase is activated by high AMP and calcium. Glycolysis is also activated by AMP

> Glucagon and epinephrine activate glycogen phosphorylase; in muscle, AMP and calcium do too.

Library: Gluconeogenesis and the Cori cycle
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Clinical Significance of Metabolic Pathways Involved in Glucose Homeostasis

## Gluconeogenesis

Gluconeogenesis runs glycolysis backward, converting many non-carbohydrates into glucose. It can make glucose without carbohydrates in the diet, and the liver uses it to convert amino acids to glucose.

## Cori cycle

1. Pyruvate from skeletal muscle is turned into lactate and alanine
2. These travel to the liver
3. Gluconeogenesis in the liver turns them into glucose
4. The glucose goes back to the muscle

> In the Cori cycle, muscle lactate and alanine become glucose in the liver and return to muscle.

Library: Glycogen storage diseases
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Clinical Significance of Metabolic Pathways Involved in Glucose Homeostasis

Most glycogen storage diseases come from decreased glycogen breakdown. Liver forms cause inter-meal hypoglycemia and an enlarged liver; muscle forms cause exercise intolerance and muscle cramping or wasting. Memory hook for types I to VII: "Viagra Pills Cause A Major Hard-on."

| Type | Deficient enzyme | Tissue | Key findings |
|---|---|---|---|
| I, von Gierke | Glucose-6-phosphatase | Liver and kidney | Most lethal; hepatomegaly, inter-meal hypoglycemia, failure to thrive; gluconeogenesis can't compensate because its glucose can't be dephosphorylated either |
| II, Pompe | Lysosomal alpha-1,4-glucosidase | All organs | Glycogen builds up in lysosomes; severe muscle damage; cardiorespiratory failure, often death before age 2 |
| III, Cori | Debranching enzyme | Liver and muscle | Mild hypoglycemia; can improve as the liver adjusts |
| IV, Andersen | Branching enzyme | Liver | Unbranched glycogen is attacked by the immune system; liver cirrhosis; lethal |
| V, McArdle | Muscle glycogen phosphorylase | Muscle | Exercise intolerance, cramps, rhabdomyolysis, myoglobinuria; "second wind" after about 10 minutes |
| VI, Hers | Liver glycogen phosphorylase | Liver | Like a milder von Gierke; gluconeogenesis keeps blood glucose up |
| VII, Tauri (Tarui) | Muscle phosphofructokinase | Muscle | Glucose is shunted into glycogen; rhabdomyolysis, myoglobinemia |

> Von Gierke is the most severe because glucose-6-phosphatase is needed for both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.

|---|---|

Library: Leptin and genetic obesity
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Energy Balance and Over and Under Nutrition

## Leptin

- Adipose releases leptin in response to insulin, cortisol, and the products of glucose metabolism
- High leptin lowers the desire to eat until fat stores fall, which then lowers leptin release (a feedback loop)
- Genetic loss of leptin can cause human obesity, and it can be treated with leptin infusion
- Most people with obesity are hyperleptinemic, which points to leptin resistance

## Genes associated with obesity

- Pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC)
- Melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R)
- Leptin

> Most people with obesity have high leptin levels, which reflects leptin resistance.

Library: Fasting, deprivation, and starvation
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Energy Balance and Over and Under Nutrition

## Three levels of energy deprivation

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| Prolonged fasting | Glucose is spared for the brain; glycogen is broken down, then glucose is made from amino acids |
| Extended deprivation | Carbohydrate sources are gone; ketone production from fat rises; non-essential plasma proteins drive gluconeogenesis |
| Starvation | Fat reserves are depleted and protein is the only source left; essential cellular proteins are degraded |

> In starvation, loss of immune function leads to infection, the most common cause of death.

Library: Galactosemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Minor Pathways of Hexose Metabolism and Associated Diseases

## Background

Infants get galactose from milk from birth, and it's mostly processed in the liver.

## Key findings

| Type | Deficient enzyme | Key findings |
|---|---|---|
| Classic (type 1) | Galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase (GALT) | Most fatal; presents in the first weeks after birth with vomiting, diarrhea, hepatomegaly, jaundice, cataracts, failure to thrive, and susceptibility to E. coli infections; autosomal recessive |
| Type 2 (non-classical) | Galactokinase | Cataracts, because galactose becomes galactitol in the lens |
| Type 3 (non-classical) | GALE | Like type 1 but milder and very rare |

## Mechanism

Phosphate gets trapped in the intermediate, causing transient hypophosphatemia. Without inorganic phosphate, the electron transport chain and citric acid cycle shut down, fatty acids can't be used for energy, and glycolysis is left as the only energy source, so pyruvate is converted to lactate.

> Classic galactosemia presents in the first weeks of life, once milk feeding begins.

Library: Fructose metabolism disorders
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Minor Pathways of Hexose Metabolism and Associated Diseases

## Normal metabolism

- Fructose is metabolized in the liver, and its products enter glycolysis
- Fructokinase isn't regulated, so it bypasses hormonal control and feeds glycolysis in an unregulated way (the "fat gene")
- Fructokinase is much faster than aldolase B, so fructose-1-phosphate can accumulate and lower free phosphate

## Key findings

| Disorder | Deficient enzyme | Key findings |
|---|---|---|
| Essential fructosuria | Fructokinase | Benign; normal life |
| Hereditary fructose intolerance | Aldolase B | Fructose-1-phosphate accumulates, trapping phosphate; hypoglycemia, vomiting, jaundice, diarrhea, liver damage |

## Treatment

Hereditary fructose intolerance requires a fructose-, sucrose-, and sorbitol-free diet. Sorbitol from the polyol pathway can also make fructose, which can be lethal.

> Hereditary fructose intolerance looks like galactosemia; the difference is timing, since it appears when fructose-containing foods are introduced.

Library: Polyol pathway
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Minor Pathways of Hexose Metabolism and Associated Diseases

## Key findings

- Aldose reductase converts aldose sugars into their sugar alcohols
- These alcohols are very hydrophilic and can't leave the cell
- Sorbitol accumulates in the lens and nerve cells, which have a lower capacity to process it, causing swelling and cataracts
- In galactosemia, the same pathway turns galactose into galactitol and causes cataracts

## Diabetic complications

With chronic hyperglycemia, glucose is pushed down the polyol pathway, and sorbitol accumulation contributes to diabetic cataracts, neuropathy, retinopathy, and nephropathy.

> Sorbitol trapped in the lens and nerves drives diabetic cataracts and neuropathy.

Library: Pentose phosphate pathway
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Minor Pathways of Hexose Metabolism and Associated Diseases

## Where it's active

- Dividing tissues (nucleotide synthesis)
- Tissues under high oxidative stress, such as red blood cells
- Tissues with high biosynthetic activity that need electron donors

## Products

- NADPH keeps the cell in a reducing state and helps eliminate oxidative radicals
- Ribose-5-phosphate is used to make purines and pyrimidines

## Key enzymes

- Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) is the rate-limiting enzyme of the oxidative branch. It makes NADPH from NADP+; NADPH inhibits it and NADP+ promotes it. The gene is on the X chromosome
- Transketolase needs thiamine pyrophosphate and is cytosolic, unlike most thiamine-dependent enzymes. Red cell transketolase activity estimates the body's thiamine reserves

## Glutathione

- Glutathione is an antioxidant tripeptide. Reduced glutathione (GSH) reacts with hydrogen peroxide and becomes oxidized (GSSG)
- Glutathione reductase uses NADPH to regenerate GSH
- Glutathione peroxidase contains four selenium ions and breaks down hydrogen peroxide

> G6PD is the rate-limiting enzyme of the oxidative branch and the source of NADPH.

Library: G6PD deficiency
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Minor Pathways of Hexose Metabolism and Associated Diseases

## Mechanism

Without G6PD, red cells can't make NADPH, so glutathione can't be reduced and the cells are exposed to oxidative stress. This causes lipid peroxidation and hemolysis. Oxidized hemoglobin precipitates as Heinz bodies, and the spleen removes them, leaving bite cells.

## Key findings

- The main symptom is hemolytic anemia
- It isn't chronic; it flares during infections or with oxidizing drugs
- Triggers: infection (most common), fava beans, sulfa drugs such as TMP-SMX, and primaquine
- Back pain and hemoglobinuria days after exposure
- X-linked recessive; about 1 in 10 African American males carry the mutation
- Partially protective against P. falciparum

## Smear

- Heinz bodies are clumps of irreversibly denatured hemoglobin attached to the membrane, seen with supravital staining
- Bite cells

## Management

Avoid triggers, give supportive care during a crisis, and check G6PD before giving primaquine or tafenoquine.

> Heinz bodies and bite cells after an infection or oxidizing drug point to G6PD deficiency.

Library: Respiratory burst and chronic granulomatous disease
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Minor Pathways of Hexose Metabolism and Associated Diseases

## Respiratory burst

Activated phagocytes increase oxygen consumption during infection to make hypochlorous acid (HOCl), which kills bacteria.

NADPH oxidase → superoxide dismutase → myeloperoxidase → HOCl

## Chronic granulomatous disease (CGD)

- Deficient NADPH oxidase; about 75% of mutations are X-linked and 25% autosomal
- Low superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, and HOCl, so catalase-positive organisms escape destruction
- Severe, recurrent infections and granulomas, such as S. aureus infections
- Dihydrorhodamine (DHR) test: low green signal means CGD
- Prophylaxis: TMP-SMX with itraconazole

## Marker of oxidative damage

Malondialdehyde is a three-carbon lipid breakdown product. Measuring it estimates lipid peroxidation and membrane damage.

> CGD leaves patients vulnerable to catalase-positive organisms.

Library: Fatty acid activation and the carnitine shuttle
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Disorders of Lipid Metabolism

## Triglyceride breakdown

- Fatty acid oxidation is stimulated by glucagon, epinephrine, and ACTH
- Adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) is turned on by glucagon and off by insulin, and it's active when phosphorylated
- Triglyceride → diglyceride → monoglyceride

## Activation and entry

- Acyl-CoA synthetase (ACS) adds CoA to the fatty acid for import into mitochondria, using 2 ATP

| Chain length | Route into mitochondria |
|---|---|
| Short and medium | Directly |
| Long | Carnitine shuttle (CAT-I and CAT-II) |
| Very long | Shortened in the peroxisome first |

- A deficiency of carnitine or of CAT-I/CAT-II causes muscle weakness and cramps with exercise

> Long-chain fatty acids need the carnitine shuttle; very-long-chain fatty acids go to the peroxisome first.

Library: Beta oxidation
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Disorders of Lipid Metabolism

## Four steps

1. Oxidation
2. Hydration
3. Oxidation
4. Thiolysis

- The first oxidation uses acyl-CoA dehydrogenase, which has three mitochondrial forms for different chain lengths: SCAD, MCAD, and LCAD

## Alcohol and fatty liver

Ethanol metabolism uses up NAD+, and the third step of beta oxidation needs NAD+. With beta oxidation blocked, fat builds up in the liver, leading to fatty liver and cirrhosis.

> Ethanol consumes NAD+, blocking beta oxidation and causing fatty liver.

Library: MCAD deficiency
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Disorders of Lipid Metabolism

## Key findings

- Medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase no longer works
- Intolerance to fasting, impaired ketogenesis, hypoglycemia, vomiting, lethargy, coma, and death
- Omega oxidation is upregulated when beta oxidation is blocked and serves as a biological marker of MCAD deficiency

## Related conditions

- Sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) is believed to be due in part to MCAD deficiency: glucose runs out during sleep and fatty acids can't be broken down
- Unripe ackee fruit causes acute MCAD deficiency by inhibiting acyl-CoA dehydrogenase, producing MCAD-like symptoms

> MCAD deficiency presents with fasting hypoglycemia and impaired ketogenesis.

Library: Odd-chain, unsaturated, alpha, and omega oxidation
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Disorders of Lipid Metabolism

## Key findings

| Situation | What happens |
|---|---|
| Odd number of carbons | The last cut leaves three-carbon propionyl-CoA, which can later become glucose via a carboxylase and a mutase |
| Unsaturated fats | Isomerases correct the bond configuration so beta oxidation can continue |
| Long branched-chain fatty acids | Alpha oxidation in peroxisomes, catalyzed by alpha-hydroxylase at the alpha carbon |
| Beta oxidation blocked | Omega oxidation in the endoplasmic reticulum, from the opposite end of the fatty acid |

- Loss of the carboxylase or mutase that processes propionyl-CoA causes propionic or methylmalonic aciduria

> Odd-chain fatty acids end in propionyl-CoA, the one part of a fatty acid that can become glucose.

Library: Zellweger syndrome, adrenoleukodystrophy, and Refsum disease
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Disorders of Lipid Metabolism

## Key findings

| Disorder | Defect | Key findings |
|---|---|---|
| Zellweger syndrome | No functional peroxisomes | Very-long-chain and branched-chain fatty acids accumulate; jaundice, hepatomegaly, seizures, hypotonia, facial abnormalities; autosomal recessive; diet change |
| Adrenoleukodystrophy (ALD) | Peroxisomal acyl-CoA synthetase | Very-long-chain fatty acids build up in the nervous system, adrenals, and testes; adrenal insufficiency is often the first sign, then progressive demyelination; X-linked recessive |
| Refsum disease | Alpha oxidation (alpha-hydroxylase) | Can't metabolize phytanic acid; scaly skin, shortened toes, ataxia, anosmia, cataracts, night blindness; autosomal recessive |

## Treatment of Refsum disease

A diet without phytanic acid, and plasmapheresis.

> Refsum disease is an alpha oxidation defect, so dietary phytanic acid must be avoided.

Library: Ketone bodies
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Disorders of Lipid Metabolism

## Key findings

- Ketones are usually made during prolonged fasting
- They're made in liver mitochondria when the citric acid cycle is less active
- Acetyl-CoA units are joined to make acetoacetate, and beta-hydroxybutyrate is used for energy in peripheral tissues
- Thiophorase converts acetoacetate back to acetyl-CoA for the citric acid cycle in peripheral tissues, and it's absent from the liver
- The heart prefers ketones over glucose, which saves glucose for the brain

> The liver makes ketones but can't use them, because it lacks thiophorase.

Library: Fatty acid synthesis
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Disorders of Lipid Metabolism

## Steps

1. Acetyl-CoA leaves the mitochondria as citrate (exchanged for malate by the tricarboxylate transporter)
2. Citrate lyase turns citrate back into acetyl-CoA in the cytoplasm
3. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) makes malonyl-CoA
4. Fatty acid synthase grows the chain by adding more malonyl-CoA

## Essential fatty acids

Linoleic and linolenic acids are essential fatty acids.

> Acetyl-CoA carboxylase makes malonyl-CoA, the building block fatty acid synthase adds to the chain.

Library: HMG-CoA reductase and statins
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Cholesterol Metabolism

## Short-term regulation

- HMG-CoA reductase is the rate-limiting step of cholesterol synthesis, and it's active when not phosphorylated
- High insulin promotes phosphatase activity, activating the reductase
- High glucagon promotes kinase activity, inactivating it

## Long-term regulation

- The HMG-CoA reductase gene is regulated by cellular cholesterol
- High cholesterol keeps the regulatory complex in the endoplasmic reticulum
- Low cholesterol moves the complex to the Golgi, and a signal eventually reaches the nucleus to turn the gene on

## Drugs

Statins, such as lovastatin, inhibit HMG-CoA reductase.

> Insulin activates HMG-CoA reductase by dephosphorylating it; glucagon inactivates it.

Library: Bile acids and cholelithiasis
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Cholesterol Metabolism

## Elimination

- Cholesterol leaves the body by conversion to bile acids
- 7-alpha-hydroxylase converts cholesterol to primary bile acids and salts; inhibiting it prevents cholesterol breakdown

## Cholelithiasis

- When cholesterol is greater than 15% of bile, it can aggregate into gallstones
- Causes include 7-alpha-hydroxylase deficiency and bile salt deficiency
- Listed approaches: ultrasound, cholecystectomy, sonic disruption, and bile salt administration

> 7-alpha-hydroxylase is the enzyme that turns cholesterol into bile acids.

Library: Lipoprotein structure and lipases
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Plasma Lipid Transport (Lipoproteins)

## Structure

- Free fatty acids travel on albumin; triglycerides, cholesterol, and cholesterol esters travel in lipoproteins
- A lipoprotein has a core of hydrophobic lipids (triglycerides and cholesterol esters) and a surface of cholesterol, phospholipids, and apolipoproteins
- Some apolipoproteins stay with their particle, and others move between particles
- Lipoproteins are classified by density; chylomicrons and VLDL are too large to leave blood vessels

## Lipases

| Lipase | Made by | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Lipoprotein lipase (LPL) | Adipocytes, skeletal and cardiac myocytes | Anchored to endothelium; must be activated by ApoCII on chylomicrons and VLDL; breaks down lipoprotein triglycerides for storage or fuel |
| Hepatic lipase (HL) | Hepatocytes | Hydrolyzes lipids in HDL and acts on IDL |

> Lipoprotein lipase needs ApoCII to break down triglycerides in chylomicrons and VLDL.

Library: Chylomicron and VLDL assembly
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Plasma Lipid Transport (Lipoproteins)

## Key findings

| Feature | Chylomicrons | VLDL |
|---|---|---|
| Made in | Intestinal mucosal cells | Hepatocytes |
| Carries | Absorbed dietary lipids (mostly triglyceride) | Lipids synthesized in the liver or taken up from chylomicrons |
| Secreted into | Lymph | Interstitial fluid |
| Apolipoprotein | ApoB48 | ApoB100 |

- Lipids are assembled in the smooth ER, and ApoB proteins are made in the rough ER
- ApoB48 and ApoB100 come from the same gene; hepatocytes read the whole gene
- ApoB48 lacks the LDL receptor binding region, so it can't interact with the LDL receptor
- Microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTTP) moves triglycerides and cholesterol esters onto ApoB and is needed to assemble both particles
- Long and very-long-chain fatty acids go into chylomicrons; shorter fatty acids go straight to the liver through the portal system

> ApoB48 is on chylomicrons and ApoB100 is on VLDL and LDL; both come from one gene.

Library: Abetalipoproteinemia
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Plasma Lipid Transport (Lipoproteins)

## Key findings

- Defective microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTTP), so chylomicrons and VLDL can't be made
- Lipid malabsorption with steatorrhea (fatty stool)
- Retinal degeneration, ataxic neuropathy, and acanthocytes (spiculated red cells)
- Many of these findings come from fat-soluble vitamin deficiency

## Treatment

The diet is usually shifted to shorter fatty acids, which don't need to be packaged into chylomicrons or VLDL.

> MTTP defects block chylomicron and VLDL assembly, causing steatorrhea and acanthocytes.

Library: LDL and the LDL receptor
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Plasma Lipid Transport (Lipoproteins)

## Where LDL comes from

- IDL that isn't taken up (from excess calories) trades triglyceride for cholesterol ester with HDL and hands its ApoE to HDL
- Hepatic lipase then acts on the ApoE-free IDL, raising its cholesterol ester content and forming LDL
- LDL carries ApoB100

## LDL receptors

- LDL delivers cholesterol esters to tissues through LDL receptors
- Receptors are made in the ER, processed in the Golgi, and sit in coated pits on the membrane
- They bind ApoB100, the complex is endocytosed, and lysosomes release the cholesterol
- Uptake depends on receptor number, which depends on intracellular cholesterol

## Atherosclerosis

The longer LDL stays in plasma, the more likely it is to be oxidized. Macrophage scavenger receptors take up oxidized LDL and aren't downregulated by cellular cholesterol, so macrophages become foam cells that contribute to plaques.

> Scavenger receptors aren't downregulated by cholesterol, so macrophages keep taking up oxidized LDL and become foam cells.

Library: HDL and reverse cholesterol transport
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Plasma Lipid Transport (Lipoproteins)

## Synthesis

- ApoA1 is the main structural protein of HDL, made in the liver and small intestine
- ABCA1 loads cholesterol and phospholipids from peripheral cells onto HDL
- LCAT moves a fatty acid from phospholipid to cholesterol, making cholesterol ester. The ester slips to the center, turning nascent HDL into a mature spherical particle

## Reverse transport

- Direct: HDL offloads cholesterol ester in the liver and recirculates; some HDL is endocytosed by the liver
- Indirect: cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP) swaps cholesterol ester for triglyceride on larger lipoproteins, and the liver takes up those ApoB particles

## Disorders

| Disorder | Defect | Key findings |
|---|---|---|
| Tangier disease | ABCA1 | Cholesterol and phospholipids aren't moved out of cells; low HDL; higher cardiovascular risk |
| LCAT deficiency | LCAT | Similar to Tangier disease; cholesterol deposits in the corneas and kidneys |

> Tangier disease (ABCA1) causes very low HDL because cells can't export cholesterol.

As a chylomicron shrinks, it trades ApoCII for ApoE. Once small enough, it passes through the endothelium, and ApoE binds a receptor outside the vessel.

There's no natural cutoff between normal and abnormal lipid levels. A diagnosis needs a fasting lipid profile with abnormal values on two separate occasions.

Library: Transamination and oxidative deamination
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Disorders of the Urea Cycle

## Transamination

- Transaminases (aminotransferases) move an amino acid's amine group onto alpha-ketoglutarate, making glutamate; each is specific to its amino acid
- Alanine transaminase (ALT): alanine → pyruvate
- Aspartate aminotransferase (AST): aspartate → oxaloacetate
- Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), the active form of vitamin B6, is the cofactor
- Glutamine synthetase: glutamate → glutamine

## Oxidative deamination

- Glutamate dehydrogenase in hepatocytes removes the amino group from glutamate as free ammonia for the urea cycle and regenerates alpha-ketoglutarate
- Glutaminase: glutamine → glutamate

## Urea cycle

The urea cycle has 5 steps, two of them in the mitochondria, and turns ammonia into urea.

> Transaminases need PLP, the active form of vitamin B6.

Library: Ammonia handling in brain, muscle, and kidney
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Disorders of the Urea Cycle

## Key findings

| Tissue | How it handles ammonia |
|---|---|
| Brain | Glutamine synthetase captures ammonia as glutamine for safe transport; the liver breaks glutamine back to glutamate |
| Muscle | During exercise, ALT2 turns excess pyruvate into alanine instead of lactic acid; alanine goes to the liver for transamination and deamination |
| Kidney | Takes up glutamine and deaminates it with glutaminase and glutamate dehydrogenase; the ammonia neutralizes protons in tubule cells. Metabolic acidosis increases glutaminase |

## Liver failure

As liver function falls, ammonia builds up in the brain. At high ammonia levels, glutamate dehydrogenase can run in reverse and make glutamate from ammonia, depleting alpha-ketoglutarate and shutting down the citric acid cycle.

> High ammonia drains alpha-ketoglutarate and shuts down the citric acid cycle in the brain.

- All are recessive genetic defects in one of the urea cycle enzymes

Library: Branched-chain amino acid metabolism
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Clinical Aspects of Amino Acid Metabolism

## Key findings

- The branched-chain amino acids are leucine, isoleucine, and valine (LIV), and all are essential
- BCAA aminotransferase removes the amino group, needs vitamin B6, and produces the alpha-keto acids of the BCAAs
- Branched-chain alpha-ketoacid dehydrogenase (BCKD) has three subunits (E1, E2, E3) and breaks the keto acids down to acyl-CoA derivatives, which can become acetyl-CoA for the citric acid cycle

## Disorders by step

| Step | Disorder |
|---|---|
| BCKD | Maple syrup urine disease |
| After BCKD, leucine | Isovaleric acidemia |
| After BCKD, valine and isoleucine | Propionic and methylmalonic acidemia |

> BCKD is the enzyme lost in maple syrup urine disease.

Library: Maple syrup urine disease
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Clinical Aspects of Amino Acid Metabolism

## Cause

- Mutation in BCKD subunits E1, E2, or E3 (E3 is the most common and most serious), which stops BCAA catabolism and the citric acid cycle
- BCKD needs five vitamin-derived cofactors, and deficiencies of B1, B2, B3, or B5 can also cause it
- Alpha-keto acids of the BCAAs accumulate, and the buildup is believed to inhibit pyruvate and alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, causing lactic acidosis

## Key findings

- Very high leucine
- Sweet or burnt-sugar odor in urine, sweat, and earwax
- Hypertonia or hypotonia
- Cyanotic episodes (blue skin, purple toes)
- Metabolic crisis with seizures, coma, and death if BCAA intake isn't restricted

## Treatment

Avoid foods containing all three branched-chain amino acids.

> Burnt-sugar odor with very high leucine points to maple syrup urine disease.

Library: Phenylketonuria
Folder: Foundations of Medicine / Week 3: Metabolic Foundations / Clinical Aspects of Amino Acid Metabolism

## Key findings

Phenylalanine is converted to tyrosine, which depends on both an enzyme and a cofactor.

| Form | Defect | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Typical PKU | Phenylalanine hydroxylase | Restrict phenylalanine; supplement tyrosine |
| Atypical PKU | Dihydrobiopterin reductase, so tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) isn't made | Restrict phenylalanine; supplement tyrosine and BH4 |

- Excess phenylalanine becomes phenylpyruvate (a phenyl ketone), causing a musty or mousy odor

A mother with PKU can cause defects in her baby, so she needs a phenylalanine-restricted diet with tyrosine supplementation during pregnancy.

> Musty odor with hypopigmentation points to PKU.
